# Intermittent Fasting Versus Regular Dietary Advice for Percentage Change in Body Weight in Adults With Overweight or Obesity: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Intermittent fasting is widely used for weight management, but its comparative effect versus continuous calorie restriction or standard healthy-eating advice remains uncertain.

### Objective
To estimate the effect of intermittent fasting versus regular dietary advice on percentage change in body weight from baseline at the longest follow-up up to and including 12 months after randomisation in adults aged 18 years or older with overweight or obesity.

### Methods
Eligible interventions comprised time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, modified alternate-day fasting, 5:2 fasting, and other periodic fasting regimens lasting at least four weeks. Eligible comparators were continuous daily calorie restriction or standard healthy-eating advice for weight loss. The prespecified effect was the mean difference (MD), coded as intermittent fasting minus regular dietary advice; negative values favor intermittent fasting, whereas positive values favor regular dietary advice. A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. The supplied dataset contained 15 candidate effect-size rows. Fourteen rows, totaling 833 participants, contributed to native MD pooling; one row was excluded because the raw inputs required for native MD analysis were unavailable.

### Results
The pooled MD was 0.0900653222730412 percentage points (95% CI −0.637 to 0.817; p=0.808078724030153; k=14; n=833), with the numerical direction designated as favoring regular dietary advice. The confidence interval included no difference and effects favoring either intervention. Statistical heterogeneity was absent in the fitted model (I²=0.0%; τ²=0.0; Q=7.06, p=0.899). Egger’s test (intercept −0.6173; p=0.5012) and Begg’s rank test (Kendall’s τ=0.1209; p=0.5906) were non-significant. Trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀=0) and produced an adjusted MD of 0.0759. Publication-bias risk was classified as low.

### Conclusions
At follow-up no later than 12 months, the available pooled result provides no evidence that intermittent fasting differs from regular dietary advice in percentage body-weight change. The estimate is highly compatible with little or no average difference. Interpretation is limited by absent bibliographic, search, study-characteristic, follow-up, extraction-provenance, and risk-of-bias information in the supplied results.

## Introduction

Overweight and obesity are major contributors to cardiometabolic disease and other adverse health outcomes. Dietary energy restriction is a central component of weight-management treatment, but sustained adherence to continuous daily restriction can be difficult. Intermittent-fasting approaches—including time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, modified alternate-day fasting, 5:2 fasting, and other periodic fasting regimens—have therefore been proposed as potentially more acceptable or effective alternatives.

The relevant comparative question is not whether intermittent fasting produces weight loss in isolation, but whether it changes body weight more than regular dietary advice intended for weight loss, including continuous daily calorie restriction and standard healthy-eating advice. This report evaluates percentage change in body weight from baseline, which places weight change on a common relative scale across populations with different starting weights.

The objective was to determine, among adults aged at least 18 years with overweight or obesity, the effect of intermittent fasting versus regular dietary advice on percentage change in body weight from baseline at the longest reported follow-up up to and including 12 months after randomisation.

## Search / Screening

### Eligibility criteria

The review question specified the following criteria:

- **Population:** Adults aged 18 years or older with overweight or obesity, including populations with comorbidities.
- **Design:** Individually randomised or cluster-randomised controlled trials.
- **Intervention:** Time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, modified alternate-day fasting, 5:2 fasting, or another periodic-fasting regimen lasting at least four weeks.
- **Comparator:** Regular dietary advice for weight loss, defined as continuous daily calorie restriction or standard healthy-eating advice.
- **Outcome:** Percentage change in body weight from baseline at the longest reported assessment up to and including 12 months after randomisation.
- **Excluded outcomes:** Body-weight change in kilograms as a separate pooled outcome, BMI, waist circumference, achievement of at least 5% weight loss, quality of life, adverse events, glycaemic outcomes, and lipid outcomes. Assessments after 12 months were also ineligible.

For multi-arm trials, the intended procedure was to exclude no-intervention and wait-list groups, combine all eligible intermittent-fasting groups by pooling means and standard deviations and summing sample sizes, and retain one comparison against the eligible regular-dietary-advice group. Clustering-adjusted estimates were to be preferred for cluster-randomised trials.

## Effect Sizes

### Effect definition and direction

The confirmed native effect measure was the **Mean Difference (MD)** in percentage change in body weight from baseline, expressed in percentage points. Effects were coded as:

> percentage weight change with intermittent fasting minus percentage weight change with regular dietary advice.

Under this coding, an MD below 0 indicates greater weight loss with intermittent fasting and favors intermittent fasting; an MD above 0 indicates less weight loss or more weight gain with intermittent fasting and favors regular dietary advice.

The intended data hierarchy was to use arm-level means and standard deviations, converting standard errors or confidence intervals to standard deviations when necessary. Where only change in kilograms was available, the intended conversion for each arm was:

> percentage weight change = (weight change in kg / baseline weight in kg) × 100.

When arm-level results were unavailable, an adjusted or unadjusted between-group difference and its uncertainty could be used; an SE could be derived from a 95% CI as `(upper limit − lower limit) / 3.92`. The supplied aggregate results do not identify which rows used arm-level percentage data, kilogram-to-percentage conversion, or model-based between-group estimates. Row-specific conversion calculations therefore cannot be documented.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used [2]. The synthesis of 14 comparison rows and 833 participants yielded a **pooled Mean Difference (MD) of 0.0900653222730412 percentage points** (95% CI **−0.637 to 0.817**; p=**0.808078724030153**).

The supplied `favors` field designated the result as favoring **regular dietary advice (continuous daily calorie restriction or standard healthy-eating advice for weight loss)**. This designation is consistent with the small positive point estimate under the stated coding. However, the estimate was not statistically distinguishable from no difference, and its confidence interval included values favoring either strategy. In practical terms, the pooled point estimate corresponds to approximately 0.09 percentage points less weight loss with intermittent fasting than with regular dietary advice—an extremely small average difference.

A cumulative meta-analysis figure was generated at **`cumulative.png`**. Because the ordering variable used for that figure was not reported, it should be regarded as descriptive; no claim about temporal stabilization or chronological evidence accumulation can be made.

## Heterogeneity

Observed statistical heterogeneity was absent:

- **I²:** 0.0%
- **τ²:** 0.0
- **Cochran’s Q:** 7.06 on 13 degrees of freedom
- **Q-test p value:** 0.899

Thus, the fitted model found no excess between-row variability beyond sampling error. The heterogeneity summary is shown at **`heterogeneity_summary.png`**.

An I² of 0% does not establish that the trials were clinically identical, particularly because intervention schedules, comparator intensity, populations, adherence, and follow-up durations were not supplied. It also does not exclude modest heterogeneity when the number and precision of comparisons are limited. Nevertheless, the quantitative evidence does not indicate detectable inconsistency.

## Publication Bias

Small-study-effect and publication-bias diagnostics were conducted on the **Mean Difference (MD)** scale:

- **Egger’s test:** intercept −0.6173; p=0.5012.
- **Begg’s rank test:** Kendall’s τ=0.1209; p=0.5906.
- **Trim-and-fill:** k₀=0 imputed studies; adjusted MD=0.0759.
- **PET estimate:** −0.6173; p=0.4682.
- **PEESE estimate:** −0.3238.
- **PET–PEESE selection:** PET, giving a selected corrected estimate of −0.6173.

Neither Egger’s nor Begg’s test was statistically significant, and trim-and-fill estimated no missing comparisons. The supplied assessment therefore classified publication-bias risk as **low**. The trim-and-fill estimate of 0.0759 was close to the primary pooled MD of 0.0900653222730412.

The PET–PEESE result should be interpreted cautiously. PET was selected and yielded a corrected MD of −0.6173, but its associated p value was 0.4682, and these regression-based methods can be unstable with only 14 comparisons and restricted variation in standard errors. More generally, non-significant asymmetry tests do not prove the absence of publication bias because their power is limited in small meta-analyses [3–5].

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This random-effects synthesis found no evidence of a difference between intermittent fasting and regular dietary advice in percentage change in body weight at the longest follow-up no later than 12 months. The pooled MD was 0.0900653222730412 percentage points, numerically favoring regular dietary advice as designated in the supplied analysis, but the 95% CI ranged from −0.637 to 0.817 percentage points and the p value was 0.808078724030153.

The point estimate is close to the null and appears unlikely to represent a clinically important average difference. The confidence interval also excludes large average advantages for either strategy on the percentage-point scale, although a threshold for clinical importance was not prespecified. Statistical heterogeneity was 0.0%, and all individual comparison confidence intervals included no difference.

### Interpretation

The findings suggest that, on average, the timing pattern of energy restriction may not materially alter relative body-weight change when intermittent fasting is compared with an active dietary strategy. This does not mean that the approaches are interchangeable for every individual. Adherence, acceptability, eating schedule, medication use, comorbidity, and personal preference may influence outcomes and could guide shared decision-making when average weight effects are similar.

The analysis addresses comparative efficacy rather than the absolute effectiveness of either intervention. Both groups may have lost weight, maintained weight, or gained weight; the MD alone cannot establish absolute within-group change. Moreover, the review intentionally pooled only percentage change in body weight. It provides no comparative evidence about adverse events, quality of life, metabolic outcomes, waist circumference, or the probability of reaching a clinically defined weight-loss target.

### Strengths

The analysis used the prespecified native outcome scale, with an interpretable percentage-point effect. Direction was explicitly coded, active dietary comparators were distinguished from no-intervention controls, follow-up was restricted to no later than 12 months, and REML random-effects estimation was used. A non-native standardized row was excluded rather than mixed with confirmed native MDs. Several complementary small-study-effect diagnostics were also supplied.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` object was included, the supplied results contain material validation issues that must be stated plainly:

1. The pipeline summary reports **0 studies** despite providing **15 effect-size rows**. The number of unique trials is therefore unresolved.
2. Native MD pooling included **14 rows (n=833)**. `study_7_R7` was excluded because native MD raw inputs were unavailable; its standardized estimate was not combined with the MDs.
3. The row-level effect field was labeled `hedges_g`, whereas analysis metadata confirmed native **Mean Difference (MD)** pooling. This report follows the confirmed `measure_label` and `native_pooling_used=true` metadata.
4. Bibliographic linkage was absent, so duplicate publications, multiple reports of the same trial, and statistical dependence among rows could not be checked.
5. Exact follow-up times were absent, preventing independent verification that every pooled estimate was the longest eligible observation at or before 12 months.
6. Extraction provenance was absent. It was not possible to identify rows converted from kilograms, rows derived from SEs or confidence intervals, model-based estimates, numbers analyzed versus randomized, multi-arm combinations, or any clustering adjustment.
7. Search, screening, trial-characteristic, and risk-of-bias data were unavailable.

## Limitations

The most important limitation is incomplete evidence provenance. Without search methods and a study flow, the review’s completeness and reproducibility cannot be established. Without trial citations and report linkage, it is impossible to verify eligibility, identify duplicate or overlapping samples, or confirm that k reflects independent trials.

Second, participant and intervention characteristics were unavailable. This prevents assessment of applicability to particular age groups, obesity classes, clinical populations, fasting schedules, or comparator programs. It also precludes explaining whether low heterogeneity reflects genuine clinical consistency or incomplete characterization.

Third, risk of bias was not assessed. A precise pooled result can still be misleading if its component studies have systematic missing data, differential adherence, selective reporting, or other design limitations. No certainty-of-evidence rating can be assigned responsibly from the supplied information.

Fourth, the outcome derivation could not be audited. Although the intended conversion and variance rules were specified, row-level baseline weights, changes in kilograms, arm means, SDs, SEs, confidence intervals, and model adjustments were not supplied. The treatment of change-score correlations cannot be evaluated.

Fifth, publication-bias tests have limited power with k=14. Their non-significance and the trim-and-fill result support, but do not establish, absence of selective publication. PET–PEESE estimates are especially uncertain in a dataset of this size.

Finally, the synthesis addresses only body weight through 12 months. It does not establish longer-term maintenance, safety, nutritional adequacy, quality of life, cardiometabolic effects, or relative acceptability.

## Conclusion

Among 14 analyzable comparison rows totaling 833 participants, intermittent fasting did not produce a statistically detectable difference in percentage body-weight change compared with continuous daily calorie restriction or standard healthy-eating advice at the longest follow-up up to 12 months. The pooled Mean Difference was 0.0900653222730412 percentage points (95% CI −0.637 to 0.817; p=0.808078724030153), numerically favoring regular dietary advice according to the supplied direction field. Heterogeneity was absent (I²=0.0%; τ²=0.0), and publication-bias risk was classified as low.

The most defensible interpretation is that the average weight effect of intermittent fasting is similar to that of regular dietary advice over this time horizon. Confidence in that conclusion is constrained by unresolved study counts and missing search, bibliographic, clinical, extraction, and risk-of-bias information. A fully auditable update should link every effect to a unique trial report, document all weight-percentage conversions, report exact eligible follow-up times, evaluate risk of bias, and examine whether adherence or fasting subtype modifies the effect.
