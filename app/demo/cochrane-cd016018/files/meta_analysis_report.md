# Once-Weekly Tirzepatide Versus Placebo for Medium-Term Percentage Body-Weight Change in Adults Living With Obesity: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Tirzepatide is used for chronic weight management, but its medium-term effect on percentage body-weight change requires quantitative synthesis.

**Objective:** To evaluate once-weekly tirzepatide at any dose, compared with placebo, for percentage change in body weight among adults aged at least 18 years living with obesity, using follow-up from 6 to 24 months.

**Methods:** The supplied dataset contained nine effect-size records and a random-effects meta-analysis fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The prespecified native effect measure was the tirzepatide-minus-placebo Mean Difference (MD) in percentage points; negative values indicate greater weight loss with tirzepatide. Where multiple doses were available, dose arms were to be combined according to the prespecified hierarchy. Publication-bias assessments included Egger’s regression, Begg’s rank test, trim-and-fill, and PET–PEESE. Search records, screening decisions, trial identifiers, bibliographic details, and arm-level extraction data were not supplied and therefore could not be independently verified.

**Results:** Nine effect-size records representing 4,640 participants were pooled. The REML pooled Mean Difference was −16.71622735423728 percentage points (95% CI −19.048 to −14.384; p=7.792385701648329×10^−45), favoring once-weekly tirzepatide. Heterogeneity was considerable (I²=93.2%; τ²=11.672462; Q=133.23, with the supplied Q-test p value recorded as 0.0). Egger’s test (intercept −10.7239; p=0.1321) and Begg’s test (Kendall’s τ=−0.2778; p=0.3585) did not provide statistically significant evidence of small-study effects. Trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀=0) and yielded an adjusted MD of −16.6929 percentage points. The PET-selected corrected estimate was −7.2278 percentage points (p=0.1119), but PET–PEESE is unreliable with fewer than 10 studies.

**Conclusions:** The supplied analysis indicates a large, statistically significant medium-term reduction in percentage body weight with once-weekly tirzepatide versus placebo. However, very high heterogeneity and incomplete study-level provenance substantially limit certainty, reproducibility, and assessment of applicability. The pooled estimate should be interpreted as an analysis of nine supplied effect-size records rather than as a fully auditable systematic review until study identities, screening records, follow-up times, estimands, doses, and native extraction data are verified.

## Introduction

Obesity is a chronic, relapsing disease associated with substantial cardiometabolic morbidity and reduced quality of life. Tirzepatide, a once-weekly glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist, produces clinically meaningful weight loss in adults living with obesity. Quantifying its placebo-adjusted effect over a medium-term period is important because weight-management trials differ in dose, estimand, follow-up duration, use of lifestyle lead-ins, and randomized-withdrawal design.

The review question was: **In adults aged 18 years or older living with obesity, what is the effect of once-weekly tirzepatide at any dose, administered for at least 6 months, compared with placebo, on percentage change in body weight from baseline at medium-term follow-up (6 to 24 months)?**

The outcome was restricted to percentage change in body weight from baseline, measured at the assessment closest to the end of the 6-to-24-month window. The target contrast was tirzepatide minus placebo in percentage points. On this native scale, a Mean Difference (MD) below zero indicates a greater percentage reduction in body weight with tirzepatide.

## Search/Screening

### Eligibility criteria

The intended eligibility framework was:

- **Population:** Adults aged ≥18 years living with obesity.
- **Intervention:** Once-weekly tirzepatide at any dose, administered for at least 6 months.
- **Comparator:** Placebo.
- **Outcome:** Percentage change in body weight from baseline.
- **Follow-up:** 6 to 24 months, using the assessment closest to the end of that interval.
- **Design considerations:** For randomized-withdrawal trials, change was to be measured from randomization through the randomized period. The same principle applied after an intensive lifestyle lead-in. Follow-up beyond 24 months and off-treatment periods were excluded.

The prespecified extraction hierarchy prioritized: (1) an adjusted tirzepatide-minus-placebo estimated treatment difference with a 95% confidence interval from ANCOVA or a mixed model; (2) arm-level estimated or least-squares means with standard errors or confidence intervals; and (3) arm-level raw means with standard deviations. For multi-dose trials, a reported pooled tirzepatide group was preferred; otherwise, dose arms were to be combined, or the 15-mg contrast used when only dose-specific treatment differences were available.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled nine effect-size records representing 4,640 participants. The **pooled Mean Difference (MD)** was **−16.71622735423728 percentage points** (95% CI **−19.048 to −14.384**; p=**7.792385701648329×10^−45**). According to the supplied direction field, this result favors **once-weekly tirzepatide (any dose; dose arms combined where appropriate)** over placebo.

Thus, within the supplied analytical dataset, tirzepatide was associated with an average placebo-adjusted reduction in percentage body weight of approximately 16.72 percentage points during medium-term follow-up. The confidence interval excludes the null value by a wide margin. This is a treatment-minus-control contrast: the negative sign denotes greater weight reduction with tirzepatide, not harm.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was considerable:

- **Q-test p value:** 0.0 as recorded in the supplied output (indicating a value below the software’s displayed numerical precision)

The I² value indicates that most observed variability was attributed to between-record heterogeneity rather than sampling error under the model. The τ² estimate likewise indicates substantial dispersion in true MDs on the squared percentage-point scale. Accordingly, the pooled MD should be interpreted as an average across heterogeneous effects, not as a single effect expected uniformly in every setting.

## Publication Bias

Small-study-effect and publication-bias diagnostics were conducted on the **Mean Difference (MD)** scale:

- **Egger’s regression:** intercept −10.7239; p=0.1321.
- **Begg’s rank test:** Kendall’s τ=−0.2778; p=0.3585.
- **Trim-and-fill:** k₀=0 imputed studies; adjusted MD −16.6929 percentage points.
- **PET:** estimate −7.2278 percentage points; p=0.1119.
- **PEESE:** estimate −12.2435 percentage points.
- **Selected PET–PEESE estimate:** PET, −7.2278 percentage points.

Neither Egger’s nor Begg’s test was statistically significant at the conventional 0.05 level, and trim-and-fill did not impute missing records. These results were classified in the supplied analysis as indicating a low overall risk of publication bias or small-study effects.

Nevertheless, absence of statistical significance is not evidence that publication bias is absent. With only nine records, funnel-plot asymmetry tests have limited power, and trim-and-fill can perform poorly in the presence of high heterogeneity. The supplied analysis explicitly notes that PET–PEESE estimates are unreliable with fewer than 10 studies. The PET-selected estimate is also much closer to the null than the primary pooled MD, but it is imprecise and should be considered exploratory rather than a replacement for the primary result.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

The supplied REML random-effects analysis found that once-weekly tirzepatide produced substantially greater medium-term percentage body-weight reduction than placebo. The pooled MD of −16.71622735423728 percentage points was highly statistically significant, with a 95% CI from −19.048 to −14.384. On the requested treatment-minus-placebo scale, this corresponds to greater weight loss with tirzepatide.

The effect is large in clinical terms, but between-record heterogeneity was also very high (I²=93.2%). The average effect therefore does not describe all included settings equally well. Variation in dose, trial phase, randomized treatment period, participant composition, treatment adherence, estimand, and handling of intercurrent events may materially alter the observed treatment effect.

### Interpretation

The analysis answers a narrowly defined efficacy question concerning percentage body-weight change over 6 to 24 months. It does not address weight change in kilograms, body mass index, waist circumference, categorical weight-loss thresholds, safety, cardiovascular outcomes, mortality, quality of life, glycaemic outcomes, or lipids. No conclusions about these outcomes should be drawn from this synthesis.

The result is an average relative treatment effect in percentage points, not the percentage of participants reaching a threshold and not a standardized effect. For example, an MD of −16.71622735423728 percentage points means that the mean percentage change was approximately 16.72 points lower in the tirzepatide group than in the placebo group under the source analyses; it does not mean that every participant lost 16.72% more weight.

### Robustness

The primary result remained materially similar after trim-and-fill (−16.6929 percentage points), because no missing records were imputed. However, this apparent robustness must be balanced against the limited power of bias diagnostics and extreme heterogeneity. The exploratory PET estimate (−7.2278 percentage points) suggests that conclusions about the precise magnitude may be sensitive to modeled small-study effects, although PET was non-significant (p=0.1119) and unreliable at k=9.

### Applicability

Applicability cannot be assessed adequately because participant demographics, comorbidities, diabetes status, baseline weight, geographical setting, tirzepatide doses, and follow-up times were unavailable. The pooled result should not be assumed to apply equally across all adults living with obesity or across all tirzepatide doses.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Several data-integrity issues require explicit consideration:

1. The pipeline summary reports **zero studies** but **nine effect sizes**. The synthesis therefore uses nine analytical records without confirmation that they represent nine distinct eligible trials.
2. Study names, citations, trial registrations, and bibliographic identifiers were absent, preventing verification against source reports and assessment of duplicate publication.
3. The record-level values supplied were Hedges’ g, whereas the pooled analysis was a native **Mean Difference (MD)** in percentage points. Individual native-scale MDs and standard errors were not provided, so the pooled MD could not be independently reproduced from the visible record-level data.
4. Dose-arm combination methods, assessment times, estimands, analyzed sample sizes by arm, and handling of randomized-withdrawal or lifestyle lead-in designs were not documented at record level.
5. The eligibility of each record for the required 6-to-24-month on-treatment window could not be confirmed.
6. No risk-of-bias assessment was supplied.
7. The Q-test p value was exported as exactly 0.0, which reflects numerical display or underflow rather than a literal probability of zero.

These issues do not negate the supplied numerical output, but they materially reduce auditability and confidence in the evidence-selection process.

## Limitations

This report has important limitations. First, no reproducible search strategy or PRISMA study-selection accounting was available. Second, the discrepancy between `n_studies=0` and nine effect-size records creates uncertainty regarding the true number of independent trials. Third, absent study identifiers and trial characteristics precluded verification of eligibility, duplicate inclusion, follow-up timing, dose pooling, and independence.

Fourth, individual native-scale MDs were unavailable even though the primary synthesis used that measure. The visible standardized effect sizes cannot be used to reproduce the reported pooled MD without additional arm-level means, standard deviations, or native treatment contrasts. Fifth, no risk-of-bias judgments or certainty-of-evidence assessment could be completed. Sixth, very high heterogeneity limits the interpretability of the mean effect, and no moderator analysis was available to explain it. Seventh, publication-bias analyses were underpowered at k=9 and potentially confounded by heterogeneity. Finally, no prediction interval was supplied, so the expected range of effects in a new comparable setting cannot be quantified from the reported output.

## Conclusion

Based on nine supplied effect-size records and 4,640 participants, once-weekly tirzepatide was associated with a statistically significant and clinically large reduction in percentage body weight compared with placebo over medium-term follow-up. The REML pooled **Mean Difference** was **−16.71622735423728 percentage points** (95% CI **−19.048 to −14.384**; p=**7.792385701648329×10^−45**), favoring tirzepatide.

Confidence in the exact magnitude is limited by considerable heterogeneity (I²=93.2%), incomplete study provenance, the inconsistency between the reported study count and effect-size count, and the absence of record-level native MDs. Before publication or clinical guideline use, the analysis should be validated against identified trial reports, a reproducible search and PRISMA flow, confirmed independent comparisons, risk-of-bias assessments, and complete native-scale extraction data.
