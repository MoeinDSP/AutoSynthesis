# Once-Weekly Tirzepatide Versus Placebo for Medium-Term Percentage Body-Weight Change in Adults Living With Obesity: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

## Abstract

**Background:** Tirzepatide is a once-weekly glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist that produces clinically important weight loss. This review evaluated its effect on percentage change in body weight among adults living with obesity.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons involved adults aged at least 18 years living with obesity, once-weekly tirzepatide at any dose for at least 6 months, placebo control, and percentage body-weight change assessed within 6–24 months. Where appropriate, dose arms were to be combined, and randomized-withdrawal or intensive-lifestyle lead-in trials were evaluated from randomization through the randomized period. The supplied dataset contained 8 studies, 9 effect estimates, and 4,640 participants. Mean differences (MDs; tirzepatide minus placebo, in percentage points) were pooled with a restricted maximum-likelihood random-effects model.

**Results:** Across 9 estimates, the pooled mean difference was −16.71622735423728 percentage points (95% CI −19.048 to −14.384; p = 7.792385701648329 × 10^−45), favoring once-weekly tirzepatide. Heterogeneity was considerable (Q = 133.23; I² = 93.2%; τ² = 11.672462; reported Q-test p = 0.0). The study-level standardized effects supplied for descriptive purposes ranged from −2.662875 to −1.251916. Egger’s intercept was −0.8158 with a reported p value of 0.0, indicating marked funnel-plot asymmetry. Trim-and-fill imputed no missing estimates (k₀ = 0) and yielded a standardized estimate of −1.7133; this standardized value is not directly comparable with the pooled MD.

**Conclusions:** Once-weekly tirzepatide produced substantially greater medium-term percentage body-weight reduction than placebo. However, very high between-estimate heterogeneity and a high assessed risk of publication bias reduce confidence in a single common magnitude of benefit. Interpretation is additionally limited because study identities, bibliographic details, search records, risk-of-bias judgments, and study-level native-scale MDs were not provided.

# Introduction

Obesity is a chronic, relapsing disease associated with substantial cardiometabolic morbidity. Tirzepatide, a dual glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide and glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonist administered once weekly, has been evaluated as a pharmacological treatment for weight management. Percentage change in body weight is a directly interpretable efficacy endpoint, but trial estimates may vary according to dose, follow-up duration, population characteristics, treatment estimand, and trial design.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: in adults aged 18 years or older living with obesity, what is the effect of once-weekly tirzepatide at any dose, administered for at least 6 months, compared with placebo, on percentage change in body weight from baseline at medium-term follow-up (6–24 months)? The prespecified native effect was the tirzepatide-minus-placebo mean difference (MD) in percentage points. On this scale, a value below zero indicates greater weight reduction with tirzepatide.

# Search/Screening

## Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies compared once-weekly tirzepatide at any dose with placebo in adults aged at least 18 years living with obesity. Treatment was required to last at least 6 months, and the outcome was percentage change in body weight at the assessment closest to the end of the 6-to-24-month window. Follow-up beyond 24 months and off-treatment periods were excluded.

The intended extraction hierarchy was: (1) an adjusted tirzepatide-minus-placebo estimated treatment difference and 95% confidence interval from ANCOVA or a mixed model, with the standard error derived as `(upper confidence limit − lower confidence limit)/3.92`; (2) arm-level estimated or least-squares means with standard errors or 95% confidence intervals, converting standard errors to standard deviations as `SE × √n`; and (3) arm-level raw means and standard deviations. Analysed sample size was preferred when reported; otherwise, randomized sample size was acceptable, particularly for treatment-regimen estimands.

For multiple tirzepatide doses, a reported pooled tirzepatide group was preferred. Otherwise, dose-arm means and standard deviations were to be combined and sample sizes summed; if only dose-specific adjusted contrasts were available, the 15-mg comparison was eligible. For randomized-withdrawal studies, change was measured from randomization through the randomized phase. The same principle applied following an intensive lifestyle lead-in. Two substudy estimates within one report could be combined when arm-level data allowed; otherwise, separate eligible contrasts could be retained within the trial.

Only percentage body-weight change was eligible. Body weight in kilograms, BMI, waist circumference, categorical weight-loss thresholds, adverse events, cardiovascular events, quality of life, mortality, glycaemic outcomes, and lipid outcomes were outside scope.

# Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A restricted maximum-likelihood random-effects model combined 9 estimates from 8 studies and 4,640 participants. The **pooled mean difference was −16.71622735423728 percentage points** (95% CI −19.048 to −14.384; p = 7.792385701648329 × 10^−45). According to the prespecified tirzepatide-minus-placebo scale and the supplied `favors` field, this result favors once-weekly tirzepatide.

The estimate indicates that, on average, tirzepatide produced approximately 16.72 percentage points greater body-weight reduction than placebo at medium-term follow-up. The confidence interval excludes no difference by a wide margin. This is a large treatment effect in clinical terms, although its precise applicability to any particular dose, population, follow-up time, or trial design is uncertain because those characteristics were not supplied and heterogeneity was substantial.

# Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was considerable: Q = 133.23, I² = 93.2%, and τ² = 11.672462. The supplied Q-test p value was 0.0, which should be interpreted as below the numerical reporting precision rather than literally zero. With 9 estimates, the implied degrees of freedom are 8.

An I² of 93.2% indicates that most observed dispersion was attributable to between-estimate differences rather than sampling error. The estimated between-study variance, τ² = 11.672462 percentage-points squared, corresponds to an approximate τ of 3.416 percentage points. No prediction interval was supplied, so one was not reconstructed. Potential explanations include differences in tirzepatide dose, duration, study design, baseline participant characteristics, estimand, missing-data assumptions, lifestyle co-interventions, and whether randomized-withdrawal or lead-in phases were used; these explanations remain hypotheses because study-level covariates were unavailable.

# Publication Bias

Egger’s regression yielded an intercept of −0.8158 with a reported p value of 0.0. The supplied interpretation classified this as highly significant (p < 0.0001), indicating substantial funnel-plot asymmetry and a **high risk of publication bias**. With only 9 estimates and very high heterogeneity, however, Egger’s test may reflect small-study effects, methodological differences, or clinical heterogeneity as well as selective publication.

Trim-and-fill estimated no missing effects (k₀ = 0) and reported an adjusted standardized estimate of −1.7133. This value is on a standardized scale and is not directly comparable with the pooled native-scale MD of −16.71622735423728 percentage points. The absence of imputed estimates does not negate the Egger result or rule out reporting bias, especially given the limited number of estimates and marked heterogeneity.

# Discussion

This random-effects meta-analysis found that once-weekly tirzepatide, across eligible doses, produced substantially greater percentage body-weight reduction than placebo after at least 6 months and within 24 months. The pooled mean difference of −16.71622735423728 percentage points was highly statistically significant and favored tirzepatide. The confidence interval, from −19.048 to −14.384 percentage points, remained compatible only with a large average benefit.

The main interpretive concern is inconsistency. An I² of 93.2% and τ² of 11.672462 demonstrate pronounced between-estimate variation. Therefore, the pooled MD is best viewed as the mean of a distribution of trial effects rather than a universally expected effect. Differences in dose, exposure duration, analysis estimand, adherence, discontinuation handling, lifestyle treatment, and randomized-withdrawal design could materially alter the observed contrast with placebo.

A second concern is possible small-study or publication bias. Egger’s test was strongly significant, and the evidence was classified as carrying high publication-bias risk. Trim-and-fill did not impute missing estimates, but this method has limited ability to resolve bias under substantial heterogeneity. These findings warrant caution regarding the exact magnitude of the pooled benefit, even though all supplied estimates appeared far from the null on their reported standardized scale.

The analysis was appropriately conducted on the requested native outcome scale—percentage-point change in body weight—which is more clinically interpretable than a standardized effect. Nevertheless, the study-level data supplied in the context were standardized rather than native-scale MDs. This prevents independent verification of the pooled MD from the displayed study-level records and complicates assessment of weighting, dose-arm combination, and estimand selection.

# Data Quality & Validation Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` object was supplied, several internal data-quality issues are evident from the provided results:

1. The dataset reports 8 studies but 9 estimates, without identifying which estimates came from the same study or whether their dependence was modelled.
2. Study-level records are standardized Hedges’ g values, whereas the primary synthesis is a native-scale percentage-point MD; the native study-level inputs needed to reproduce the pooled MD were not supplied.
3. The supplied standardized effects are negative despite the requested convention that positive Hedges’ g indicates greater weight reduction with tirzepatide. This is an unresolved sign-coding inconsistency. Favorability was therefore stated only from the explicit `favors` field for the primary meta-analysis.
4. Egger and trim-and-fill outputs appear to use standardized effects, while the principal model uses MDs. Their numerical estimates should not be compared directly.
5. The Q-test and Egger p values were supplied as 0.0. Such values reflect rounding or numerical underflow and are not literal probabilities of zero.
6. Search records, trial citations, native extraction data, risk-of-bias judgments, and details of dose-arm combination were unavailable for validation.

# Limitations

First, the underlying search strategy and screening flow were unavailable, preventing assessment of search completeness, duplicate handling, or selective inclusion. Second, bibliographic and clinical study characteristics were omitted, so external validity and sources of clinical heterogeneity could not be evaluated. Third, no domain-level risk-of-bias assessments or certainty-of-evidence evaluation were provided.

Fourth, at least one study contributed multiple estimates, but the relevant study and the handling of within-study dependence were not identified. Treating correlated contrasts as independent can underestimate uncertainty. Fifth, study-level native-scale MDs and variances were unavailable, preventing independent reproduction of the pooled result and verification that dose arms were combined according to the prespecified hierarchy. Sixth, no prediction interval or influence analysis was supplied despite very high heterogeneity. Seventh, publication-bias methods were based on only 9 estimates and are difficult to interpret under substantial heterogeneity. Finally, no moderator data were available to explain variation in effects or to estimate dose- or duration-specific benefits.

# Conclusion

In adults living with obesity, once-weekly tirzepatide administered for at least 6 months was associated with a pooled **16.71622735423728-percentage-point greater reduction in body weight than placebo** at 6–24 months (MD −16.71622735423728; 95% CI −19.048 to −14.384; p = 7.792385701648329 × 10^−45), favoring tirzepatide. The evidence indicates a large average treatment benefit, but the magnitude should be interpreted cautiously because heterogeneity was very high (I² = 93.2%) and publication-bias risk was assessed as high. Access to trial-level native MDs, full study characteristics, risk-of-bias judgments, and reproducible search records is required for a fully auditable PRISMA-compliant review and a more secure estimate of treatment effect.
