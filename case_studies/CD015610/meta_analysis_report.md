# Intermittent Fasting Versus Regular Dietary Advice for Percentage Change in Body Weight in Adults With Overweight or Obesity: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Intermittent fasting is widely used for weight management, but its comparative effect versus continuous daily calorie restriction or standard healthy-eating advice remains uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of intermittent fasting versus regular dietary advice on percentage change in body weight from baseline at the longest follow-up up to and including 12 months after randomisation among adults with overweight or obesity.

**Methods:** Eligible studies were individually or cluster-randomised controlled trials in adults aged at least 18 years, including populations with comorbidities. Eligible intermittent-fasting regimens included time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, modified alternate-day fasting, 5:2 fasting, and other periodic fasting; controls received continuous daily calorie restriction or standard healthy-eating advice for weight loss. Interventions had to last at least four weeks. The prespecified outcome was percentage change in body weight from baseline. A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. Effects were directly coded as intermittent fasting minus regular dietary advice; negative values favour intermittent fasting and positive values favour regular dietary advice.

**Results:** Nineteen study records were represented in the supplied results, and 15 effect-size records were available. Fourteen studies (833 participants) contributed to native-scale pooling; one record was excluded because the raw information needed for mean-difference pooling was unavailable. The pooled mean difference was 0.0900653222730412 percentage points (95% CI −0.637 to 0.817; p = 0.808078724030153), with the point estimate favouring regular dietary advice. Heterogeneity was not detected (Q = 7.06, p = 0.899; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). Egger’s intercept was −0.0058 (p = 0.9136), while trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study and produced an adjusted estimate of 0.0228; publication-bias risk was classified as moderate.

**Conclusions:** At follow-up up to 12 months, intermittent fasting did not show a statistically detectable advantage over regular dietary advice in percentage body-weight change. The estimated average difference was close to zero, but the confidence interval remained compatible with modest benefit in either direction. Interpretation is limited by incomplete study-level reporting, the exclusion of one effect from native-scale pooling, and the absence of supplied risk-of-bias and search-process details.

## Introduction

Overweight and obesity are chronic conditions associated with increased cardiometabolic morbidity, reduced quality of life, and substantial healthcare burden. Dietary energy restriction remains central to weight management, but sustained adherence to daily restriction can be difficult. Intermittent-fasting approaches seek to simplify energy restriction by concentrating eating and fasting into defined periods. These approaches include time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, modified alternate-day fasting, the 5:2 diet, and other periodic-fasting schedules.

Whether intermittent fasting produces greater weight loss than continuous daily calorie restriction or standard healthy-eating advice is clinically important. The relevant comparison is not fasting versus no intervention, but fasting versus an active dietary strategy that itself may produce weight loss. This review therefore addressed the following question: among adults aged 18 years or older with overweight or obesity, what is the effect of intermittent fasting, compared with regular dietary advice, on percentage change in body weight from baseline at the longest reported follow-up no later than 12 months after randomisation?

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met all of the following criteria:

- **Population:** Adults aged at least 18 years with overweight or obesity, including populations with comorbidities.
- **Design:** Individually randomised or cluster-randomised controlled trials.
- **Intervention:** Time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, modified alternate-day fasting, 5:2 fasting, or another periodic-fasting regimen.
- **Comparator:** Regular dietary advice for weight loss, including continuous daily calorie restriction or standard healthy-eating advice.
- **Duration:** At least four weeks.
- **Outcome:** Percentage change in body weight from baseline, assessed at the longest reported follow-up up to and including 12 months after randomisation.

No-intervention and wait-list groups were not eligible comparators. Assessments after 12 months and other outcomes—including body weight in kilograms as a separate endpoint, BMI, waist circumference, achievement of at least 5% weight loss, quality of life, adverse events, glycaemic outcomes, and lipid outcomes—were outside the quantitative synthesis.

## Effect Sizes

### Outcome definition and direction

The sole pooled outcome was percentage change in body weight from baseline at the longest available follow-up up to and including 12 months. The requested native effect measure was the **Mean Difference (MD)** in percentage points, coded as:

> intermittent fasting minus regular dietary advice.

Accordingly, an MD below 0 indicates greater weight loss with intermittent fasting and favours intermittent fasting; an MD above 0 indicates less weight loss or more weight gain with intermittent fasting and favours regular dietary advice.

Arm-level means and standard deviations were preferred. Where necessary under the prespecified rules, standard errors or confidence intervals could be converted to standard deviations. Weight change reported only in kilograms could be converted within each arm as `(weight change in kg / baseline weight in kg) × 100`. If arm-level data were unavailable, an adjusted or unadjusted between-group difference and its uncertainty could be used, with an SE derived from a 95% CI as `(upper limit − lower limit) / 3.92`. The supplied results do not identify which individual studies required any of these conversions, so no study-specific conversion claims can be made.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects meta-analysis fitted using restricted maximum likelihood included 14 studies and 833 participants. The **pooled Mean Difference (MD)** was **0.0900653222730412 percentage points** (95% CI **−0.637 to 0.817**; p = **0.808078724030153**). As specified in the supplied `favors` field, the point estimate favoured **regular dietary advice (continuous daily calorie restriction or standard healthy-eating advice for weight loss)**.

The result was not statistically significant, and the confidence interval crossed the null value of 0. Thus, the evidence did not demonstrate a difference between intermittent fasting and regular dietary advice in percentage change in body weight by the longest follow-up up to 12 months. The central estimate was close to no difference: approximately 0.09 percentage points less favourable for intermittent fasting. The interval estimate nevertheless remained compatible with either approximately 0.637 percentage points greater weight loss under intermittent fasting or approximately 0.817 percentage points greater weight loss under regular dietary advice.

## Heterogeneity

No statistical heterogeneity was detected:

- Cochran’s Q = **7.06**;
- Q-test p = **0.899**;
- I² = **0.0%**;
- τ² = **0.0**.

These statistics indicate that the observed dispersion was no greater than expected from within-study sampling error under the fitted model. However, I² = 0% does not establish that all interventions are clinically equivalent or that true heterogeneity is impossible. Power to detect heterogeneity can be limited, and the absence of detailed information on fasting regimen, comparator intensity, participant characteristics, adherence, and follow-up prevents a robust assessment of clinical and methodological heterogeneity.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−0.0058** with p = **0.9136**, providing no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **one** potentially missing study and yielded an adjusted estimate of **0.0228**. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **moderate**.

These findings should be interpreted together rather than treating the nonsignificant Egger test as proof that reporting bias is absent. Tests for funnel asymmetry can have limited power with a modest number of studies, while trim-and-fill depends on assumptions that may not hold. The adjusted estimate remained close to zero, but its effect-measure provenance and confidence interval were not supplied; it should therefore be regarded as a sensitivity diagnostic rather than a replacement for the primary pooled MD.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across 14 randomised comparisons involving 833 participants, intermittent fasting did not produce a statistically detectable improvement in percentage body-weight change compared with regular dietary advice at the longest follow-up up to 12 months. The pooled MD was 0.0900653222730412 percentage points, and its 95% CI ranged from −0.637 to 0.817. The point estimate favoured regular dietary advice, as specified in the supplied analysis, but was very close to the null and was imprecisely estimated relative to that small central effect.

The lack of detected heterogeneity suggests broad statistical consistency among the included native-scale effects. Nevertheless, intermittent fasting is not a single intervention. Time-restricted eating, alternate-day fasting, modified alternate-day fasting, and 5:2 regimens differ in fasting duration, permitted energy intake, behavioural burden, and likely mechanisms. Comparator programmes can likewise vary substantially. The absence of moderator data means that a benefit or disadvantage in a specific regimen or population cannot be ruled out by the overall average.

### Clinical interpretation

The analysis compares two active dietary approaches. A null between-group effect does not imply that participants failed to lose weight; it indicates that the average percentage change did not differ detectably between intermittent fasting and regular dietary advice. On the available evidence, selection between these strategies may reasonably depend on patient preference, feasibility, adherence, comorbidities, medication schedules, safety considerations, and availability of behavioural support rather than an expectation of materially superior average weight loss from intermittent fasting.

The confidence interval is also important. Although it excludes a large average difference on the percentage-point scale, it remains compatible with modest advantage for either strategy. The results should not be interpreted as definitive equivalence because no equivalence or non-inferiority margin was specified or tested.

### Publication-bias considerations

Egger’s test did not identify asymmetry, but trim-and-fill suggested one potentially missing study. The resulting adjusted estimate of 0.0228 remained close to zero. Taken together, these analyses do not indicate a major change in the substantive conclusion, although the supplied moderate publication-bias rating is appropriate because selective publication cannot be excluded.

## Data Quality and Validation Notes

The source dataset presents 19 study records but only 15 effect-size records. Four study records therefore did not contribute an effect-size estimate in the supplied results, and reasons were not reported. Of the 15 effect-size records, `study_7_R7` was excluded from native MD pooling because raw inputs needed to calculate a native mean difference were unavailable, leaving 14 studies and 833 participants in the primary synthesis.

There is also a scale-label discrepancy: study-level records were stored under a standardized-effect field, while the primary synthesis was confirmed as native **Mean Difference (MD)** pooling. The pooled MD has therefore been reported as the primary result, and the listed study-level values have not been misrepresented as native percentage-point MDs. No information was supplied with which to independently verify arm assignment, conversions from kilograms to percentage change, SD derivations, handling of multi-arm trials, clustering adjustments, or the selected follow-up assessment.

## Limitations

1. **Incomplete search documentation:** Search sources, dates, strategies, language restrictions, grey-literature searches, and screening counts were not supplied. Full PRISMA flow reporting and reproducibility of study identification are therefore not possible.
2. **Anonymous study identifiers:** Bibliographic citations and study names were unavailable, preventing linkage of estimates to published trial characteristics.
3. **Missing study characteristics:** Details of participants, interventions, comparators, follow-up timing, adherence, attrition, and randomisation unit were not provided.
4. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** Domain-level judgements for randomisation, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcomes, outcome measurement, and selective reporting were unavailable. The certainty of evidence therefore cannot be formally graded.
5. **Effect-measure discrepancy:** The study-level records were supplied in a field associated with standardized effects, whereas the primary model used native MDs. One study could not enter native-scale pooling because the necessary raw information was unavailable.
6. **Unverifiable transformations:** The supplied results do not state which studies required conversion from weight change in kilograms, derivation of SDs from SEs or confidence intervals, or use of model-based between-group estimates.
7. **No moderator analysis:** Potential differences among fasting regimens, comparator types, populations, and follow-up durations could not be evaluated.
8. **Publication-bias uncertainty:** Egger’s test had no statistically significant result, but power may have been limited; trim-and-fill suggested one potentially missing study.
9. **Follow-up scope:** The estimand was restricted to the longest follow-up no later than 12 months. These findings do not establish comparative effectiveness or weight maintenance beyond one year.
10. **Safety not assessed:** Adverse events and other non-weight outcomes were intentionally outside the pooled outcome, so no conclusion about comparative safety or broader metabolic effects can be drawn.

## Conclusion

In adults with overweight or obesity, intermittent fasting and regular dietary advice produced no statistically detectable difference in percentage change in body weight at the longest follow-up up to and including 12 months. The REML random-effects pooled Mean Difference was 0.0900653222730412 percentage points (95% CI −0.637 to 0.817; p = 0.808078724030153), with the point estimate favouring regular dietary advice. Statistical heterogeneity was absent in the fitted model (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), but incomplete reporting and the inability to evaluate intervention-specific moderators constrain interpretation. Current evidence supports treating intermittent fasting as an alternative dietary format rather than a demonstrably superior weight-loss strategy.
