# Laparoscopic versus vaginal hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis of blood transfusion

## Abstract

**Background:** Blood transfusion is an important safety outcome after hysterectomy. This meta-analysis evaluated whether laparoscopic hysterectomy—laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy, total laparoscopic hysterectomy, or laparoscopic hysterectomy of unspecified type—differs from vaginal hysterectomy in the odds of any intra-operative or post-operative blood transfusion.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons involved women undergoing hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease and compared an eligible laparoscopic approach with vaginal hysterectomy. The outcome was receipt of any blood transfusion. The supplied dataset contained 14 studies, of which 13 contributed estimable effect sizes encompassing 1,411 participants. Odds ratios (ORs) were pooled using a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model. Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were assessed using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Across 13 estimable comparisons, the pooled Odds Ratio (OR) was 1.3438106044853726 (95% CI 0.774 to 2.333; p = 0.2937236704745661). Under the supplied result classification, the estimate favored neither laparoscopic nor vaginal hysterectomy. Heterogeneity was absent in the fitted model (Q = 9.481, p = 0.6614; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). Egger’s intercept was 0.294 (p = 0.8972), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀ = 0), indicating low detected risk of publication bias. Nevertheless, publication-bias analyses had limited power with only 13 effect estimates.

**Conclusions:** The available evidence did not establish a difference between laparoscopic and vaginal hysterectomy in the odds of receiving any blood transfusion. The confidence interval remains compatible with both lower and substantially higher odds under laparoscopy. Interpretation is limited by absent study citations, arm-level event counts, search details, risk-of-bias assessments, and information about the fourteenth study that did not contribute an estimable effect.

## Search/Screening

The review question was defined by population, intervention, comparator, and outcome as follows:

- **Population:** women undergoing hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease.
- **Intervention:** laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy, total laparoscopic hysterectomy, or laparoscopic hysterectomy of unspecified type.
- **Comparator:** vaginal hysterectomy.
- **Outcome:** receipt of any blood transfusion intra-operatively, post-operatively during the hospital admission, or by the end of reported follow-up.
- **Effect measure:** Odds Ratio (OR), with OR > 1 indicating higher odds of transfusion in the laparoscopic group and OR < 1 indicating lower odds in the laparoscopic group.

No database names, search strings, coverage dates, search date, language restrictions, registry searches, deduplication counts, title/abstract screening counts, full-text exclusions, or reviewer procedures were supplied. A PRISMA flow diagram therefore cannot be reconstructed, and the completeness or reproducibility of the search cannot be assessed. No protocol or registration record was reported.

Eligibility was outcome-specific. Blood-loss volume, postoperative haemoglobin decline, haematoma, and other complications were not acceptable substitutes for the binary transfusion outcome. Abdominal and robotic hysterectomy arms were outside scope. The intended unit of analysis was the randomized participant, using intention-to-treat denominators. For a multi-arm trial containing multiple eligible laparoscopic groups and one shared vaginal group, the prespecified approach was to split the shared vaginal group equally across eligible comparisons. The supplied aggregate results did not include trial-level arm descriptions or counts, so adherence to these extraction and unit-of-analysis rules could not be independently verified.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood included **k = 13** effect sizes and **n = 1,411** participants. The pooled **Odds Ratio (OR)** was:

> **OR = 1.3438106044853726 (95% CI 0.774 to 2.333; p = 0.2937236704745661).**

The confidence interval included the null value of 1, and the test of the pooled effect was not statistically significant. According to the supplied favorability classification, this result **favored neither arm**. Clinically, the interval remains compatible with odds of transfusion under laparoscopic hysterectomy ranging from approximately 23% lower to 133% higher than under vaginal hysterectomy; thus, absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as proof of equivalence.

Although the point estimate was above 1, the prespecified directional convention and the supplied result classification should both be kept explicit: OR > 1 denotes higher transfusion odds with laparoscopic hysterectomy, while the overall result was classified as favoring neither treatment because of its uncertainty.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was not detected:

- **Cochran’s Q = 9.481**, with **p = 0.6614**;
- **I² = 0.0%**;
- **τ² = 0.0**.

With 13 estimates, the Q test has 12 degrees of freedom. These findings indicate that the observed dispersion was compatible with within-study sampling variation under the fitted model. Nevertheless, an I² of 0% does not prove that the studies were clinically identical, particularly because study characteristics and transfusion definitions were unavailable and heterogeneity statistics have limited precision when the number of studies is modest.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **0.294** with **p = 0.8972**. Thus, there was no detected evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **low**.

These findings should be interpreted cautiously. With only 13 effect estimates, asymmetry tests have limited power, and failure to detect asymmetry does not exclude publication bias, selective outcome reporting, or non-publication of small studies. Moreover, the supplied trim-and-fill output included an adjusted numerical value on an unconfirmed scale; because it was not established as a native OR, it was not interpreted as an adjusted transfusion effect.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This random-effects meta-analysis found no statistically established difference in the odds of receiving any blood transfusion between laparoscopic and vaginal hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease. The pooled Odds Ratio (OR) was 1.3438106044853726, but its 95% confidence interval crossed the null (0.774 to 2.333), and the supplied classification indicated that the result favored neither arm.

Estimated statistical heterogeneity was zero, suggesting that the variation among the 13 estimable effects did not exceed that expected from sampling error. Publication-bias diagnostics were also negative: Egger’s test was non-significant, and trim-and-fill imputed no studies. Neither finding, however, compensates for the lack of trial-level clinical details and arm-level transfusion counts.

### Clinical interpretation

Blood transfusion is uncommon in many contemporary benign hysterectomy populations. For sparse binary outcomes, apparently similar event rates can produce wide confidence intervals, while trials with no events in either arm contribute no information to a conventional OR estimate. The present confidence interval is too broad to rule out a clinically meaningful benefit or harm. Accordingly, the correct interpretation is **inconclusive evidence of a difference**, not equivalence or non-inferiority.

The endpoint combined transfusions occurring intra-operatively, post-operatively during the hospital stay, or by the end of reported follow-up. This broad definition is clinically sensible but may conceal variation in monitoring duration and transfusion practice. Differences in haemoglobin thresholds, symptoms, comorbidity, operative complexity, and local blood-management protocols could affect transfusion independently of surgical route.

### Implications for practice and research

On these data alone, transfusion risk does not provide clear evidence for choosing laparoscopic over vaginal hysterectomy or vice versa. Surgical route should continue to reflect patient anatomy, disease characteristics, surgeon expertise, feasibility, recovery outcomes, complications, and patient preferences.

Future evidence syntheses should publish the complete study-level 2×2 tables, explicitly distinguish laparoscopically assisted vaginal from total laparoscopic hysterectomy, preserve double-zero trials in descriptive tables, document any splitting of shared comparator groups, and assess risk of bias using a contemporary randomized-trial framework. Individual trials should report the timing, indication, and threshold for every transfusion and use randomized denominators.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Several constraints prevent full validation of the analysis:

1. Fourteen studies were identified, but only 13 effect sizes were supplied; the status of the additional study was not reported.
2. Arm-level transfusion events, randomized totals, and procedure descriptions were absent, preventing verification of intention-to-treat denominators, procedure-based extraction, shared-control splitting, and exclusion of abdominal or robotic arms.
3. A possible double-zero trial cannot be identified or displayed because the relevant event counts and full arm sizes were not supplied.
4. Study-level outputs were provided on an auxiliary standardized scale, whereas the confirmed native pooled measure was the Odds Ratio (OR). These quantities should not be conflated.
5. The trim-and-fill numerical adjusted effect was not confirmed to be on the OR scale and was therefore not interpreted as an adjusted OR.
6. No bibliographic study identifiers, search history, screening log, reasons for exclusion, protocol, or risk-of-bias judgments were available.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations. First, it is based on aggregate pipeline output rather than source publications or extractable arm-level data. Errors in trial identification, procedure classification, outcome extraction, or treatment coding cannot be ruled out. Second, the search and screening process cannot be assessed against all PRISMA 2020 requirements because the relevant information was not provided. Third, risk of bias within studies and certainty of evidence across studies were not evaluated; a formal GRADE rating is therefore not possible.

Fourth, one of 14 identified studies did not contribute an estimable effect, but the reason and sample size were unavailable. If this was a double-zero trial, its exclusion from the numerical OR estimate would be conventional, but it should still appear in the descriptive evidence table. Fifth, the analysis may combine clinically distinct laparoscopic approaches and transfusion ascertainment periods. Sixth, no moderator analyses were available to test whether operative technique or study characteristics altered the effect. Finally, both heterogeneity and publication-bias statistics are imprecise with a modest number of studies, and the pooled confidence interval remains clinically wide.

## Conclusion

Among women undergoing hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease, the available meta-analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in receipt of any blood transfusion between laparoscopic and vaginal hysterectomy. The random-effects pooled **Odds Ratio (OR) was 1.3438106044853726 (95% CI 0.774 to 2.333; p = 0.2937236704745661)**, and the supplied classification favored neither arm. No statistical heterogeneity was detected (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), and publication-bias diagnostics were negative, although both should be interpreted cautiously. Complete trial-level event data and stronger reporting are required before equivalence or clinically important differences can be excluded.
