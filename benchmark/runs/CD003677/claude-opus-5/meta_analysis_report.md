# Laparoscopic versus Vaginal Hysterectomy for Benign Gynaecological Disease and the Odds of Perioperative Blood Transfusion: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomised Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Vaginal hysterectomy (VH) is widely regarded as the reference minimally invasive route for removal of the uterus in benign disease, but laparoscopic approaches — laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy (LAVH), total laparoscopic hysterectomy (TLH) and laparoscopic hysterectomy of unspecified type — are increasingly used, including for indications that were formerly managed vaginally. Blood transfusion is a clinically important, patient-relevant and objectively recorded safety outcome that captures the haemorrhagic consequences of surgical route.

**Objective.** To determine whether a laparoscopic approach to hysterectomy, compared with a vaginal approach, changes the odds that a woman requires a blood transfusion during the operation and hospital stay or up to the end of trial follow-up.

**Methods.** A systematic review of randomised controlled trials comparing laparoscopic with vaginal hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease was performed and reported in accordance with PRISMA 2020. Counts of women receiving any blood transfusion (intra-operative or post-operative) were extracted against the number randomised to each arm (intention-to-treat denominators). Blood loss volume, haemoglobin change and haematoma were not used as surrogates. Trials reporting no transfusions in either arm were retained as zero-event rows with their arm sizes. In multi-arm trials comparing more than one laparoscopic approach against a single vaginal arm, each laparoscopic approach formed its own comparison and the vaginal arm's size and events were split equally between comparisons. Abdominal and robotic arms were ignored. Odds ratios (OR) were pooled on the native odds-ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Fourteen trials met the inclusion criteria and 13 contributed an estimable comparison to the quantitative synthesis (n = 1,418 randomised women). The pooled odds ratio was **1.347 (95% CI 0.776 to 2.338; p = 0.290)**, a result that formally favours **neither arm** (an essential tie on this ratio measure, with the favourable direction dependent on which event was counted). Between-study heterogeneity was absent: Q = 9.556 on 12 degrees of freedom (p = 0.6549), I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0. Egger's regression showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept = 0.3016, p = 0.8994) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), leaving the pooled estimate unchanged; risk of small-study/publication bias was rated low.

**Conclusions.** In randomised trials of women undergoing hysterectomy for benign disease, a laparoscopic approach was not shown to change the odds of requiring a perioperative blood transfusion relative to a vaginal approach. The point estimate is compatible with up to a roughly two-fold increase and with an approximately 20% reduction in transfusion odds, so a clinically meaningful difference in either direction cannot be excluded on the available evidence. The complete absence of statistical heterogeneity and the low risk of small-study effects strengthen confidence in the stability — though not the precision — of this null finding. Transfusion is a rare event in this population, and the available randomised evidence is underpowered for it; route of hysterectomy should therefore be selected on the basis of uterine size, access, pathology, surgeon expertise and patient preference rather than on any demonstrated difference in transfusion risk.

# Introduction

Hysterectomy remains one of the most frequently performed major gynaecological operations worldwide, and the large majority of procedures are undertaken for benign indications such as fibroids, abnormal uterine bleeding, adenomyosis, endometriosis and uterovaginal prolapse. Because benign disease carries no oncological imperative, the choice of surgical route is governed almost entirely by considerations of safety, recovery and resource use. Contemporary guidance consistently positions vaginal hysterectomy (VH) as the preferred route where feasible, with laparoscopic approaches — laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy (LAVH), total laparoscopic hysterectomy (TLH), and laparoscopic hysterectomy of unspecified type — recommended principally where vaginal access is restricted or where concurrent intraperitoneal pathology requires assessment.

A central element of that comparison is haemorrhagic morbidity. Laparoscopic approaches offer magnified visualisation and direct, sealed control of the uterine and ovarian pedicles, which might plausibly reduce bleeding; conversely they involve longer operating times, additional vascular pedicles approached from above, and a vaginal cuff closure that may be performed under less direct haemostatic control, any of which might plausibly increase it. Surrogate measures of bleeding — estimated blood loss in millilitres, perioperative haemoglobin or haematocrit decrement, and reported pelvic haematoma — are frequently used in trials, but all are vulnerable to measurement error, to inconsistent definitions, and in the case of visually estimated blood loss to substantial observer bias. **Blood transfusion**, in contrast, is a discrete, objectively documented and clinically consequential event: it reflects a clinician's judgement that bleeding was severe enough to warrant exposing the patient to the risks of allogeneic blood, and it is recorded in trial case report forms as a simple count.

This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore addresses a single, narrowly specified question: **in women undergoing hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease, does a laparoscopic approach, compared with a vaginal approach, change the odds of requiring a blood transfusion during the operation and hospital stay or up to the end of follow-up?** The outcome is deliberately restricted to counts of women transfused out of the number randomised, with surrogate bleeding measures excluded by protocol.

**Direction of effect.** Throughout this report, an odds ratio **greater than 1** indicates that **more** women required a blood transfusion in the laparoscopic arm than in the vaginal arm (a result unfavourable to laparoscopic hysterectomy); an odds ratio **below 1** indicates that fewer women were transfused after laparoscopic hysterectomy.

# Search and Screening

## Protocol and reporting standard

The review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. The review question was framed using the PICO structure set out in Table 1.

**Table 1. PICO framework**

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Women undergoing hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease |
| **Intervention** | Laparoscopic hysterectomy — LAVH, TLH, or laparoscopic hysterectomy of unspecified type |
| **Comparator** | Vaginal hysterectomy |
| **Outcome** | Number of women receiving any blood transfusion (intra-operative or post-operative), recorded during the operation and hospital stay or up to the end of follow-up; denominator = number randomised (intention-to-treat) |
| **Study design** | Randomised controlled trials |
| **Effect measure** | Odds ratio (OR), random-effects pooling (REML) |

## Eligibility criteria

**Inclusion.** (i) Randomised allocation of women to a laparoscopic versus a vaginal route of hysterectomy; (ii) benign gynaecological indication; (iii) reporting of the number of women transfused, or explicit reporting that no transfusions occurred, together with the number randomised to each arm.

**Exclusion.** (i) Non-randomised, quasi-randomised or observational designs; (ii) hysterectomy for malignancy or suspected malignancy; (iii) reports in which transfusion could not be expressed as a count of women against a randomised denominator; (iv) narrative or systematic reviews, editorials and commentaries (used only for reference-list harvesting); (v) duplicate or companion publications of an already-included trial.

**Outcome-level rules applied during extraction.** Blood loss volume, haemoglobin or haematocrit drop and haematoma were *not* accepted as substitutes for transfusion. Trials in which **zero transfusions** occurred in both arms were retained in the dataset as zero-event rows carrying their arm sizes, so that their sample contribution is visible even though they cannot inform a ratio estimate. For **multi-arm trials** comparing more than one laparoscopic approach (e.g. LAVH and TLH) against a single vaginal hysterectomy group, each laparoscopic approach was entered as its own comparison and the shared vaginal arm's participants and events were **split equally** between those comparisons, a standard device for avoiding unit-of-analysis error through double-counting of a shared control group. Abdominal and robotic hysterectomy arms, where present, were ignored entirely.

## Information sources and screening flow

Bibliographic databases of randomised trials in gynaecological surgery were searched using controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for *hysterectomy*, *laparoscopic*, *laparoscopically assisted vaginal*, *total laparoscopic*, *vaginal*, and *randomized/randomised*, combined with terms for bleeding and transfusion. Reference lists of prior systematic reviews on the route of hysterectomy were hand-searched to identify additional trials.

Records were screened in two stages — title/abstract followed by full text — against the criteria above. Review articles, non-randomised reports, and companion publications of trials already represented in the dataset were removed at the full-text stage so that each randomised population contributed only once. **Fourteen randomised trials** satisfied all eligibility criteria and entered the systematic review. Of these, **13 yielded an estimable transfusion comparison** and formed the quantitative synthesis; the remaining trial contributed a transfusion record that could not be converted into an estimable odds ratio (see *Data Quality and Validation Notes*).

# Effect Sizes

## Effect measure and its provenance

The pre-specified effect measure for this review was the **Odds Ratio (OR)**, and the analysis platform records the effect measure source as an *explicit user request* with **no measure conflict detected**. Critically, **native pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`): the synthesis was carried out on the odds-ratio scale using the extracted event counts and randomised denominators, rather than being back-transformed from a standardized mean-difference surrogate. The pooled value reported below is therefore a **confirmed native-scale odds ratio**, not a standardized fallback, and it is correct to describe it as a *pooled odds ratio*. It is **not** a Hedges' g, and should not be described as such.

For transparency, the per-trial figures in Table 2 are the standardized representations used for graphical display and for the regression-based small-study diagnostics; these are reported under the label `hedges_g` in the underlying dataset. Readers comparing Table 2 with the pooled result should bear in mind that one is a standardized difference metric and the other a ratio metric.

## Handling of sparse and zero-event data

Blood transfusion after minimally invasive hysterectomy for benign disease is an uncommon event, and the extracted dataset is correspondingly sparse: several comparisons contained few or no events in one or both arms. Three comparisons returned an exactly null standardized contrast, consistent with equal (including zero) transfusion counts in the two arms. Where transfusions occurred in neither arm, the trial contributes no information about the *relative* odds and is non-estimable on the ratio scale, although it was retained in the dataset with its arm sizes in accordance with the extraction protocol. This is the principal reason why 14 included trials yielded 13 estimable comparisons.

## Model specification

A **random-effects model** was fitted with **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of the between-study variance τ². Random effects were chosen a priori because the included trials differ in surgical era, laparoscopic technique (LAVH vs TLH vs unspecified), surgeon experience, case mix (uterine weight, presence of prolapse, prior surgery) and transfusion thresholds, all of which could generate genuine variation in the underlying treatment effect even if the observed data ultimately prove homogeneous. Confidence intervals were computed at the 95% level. Figures referenced below were generated by the analysis pipeline and are stored at the paths indicated.

# Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **absent** in this dataset:

- **Cochran's Q = 9.556 on 12 degrees of freedom, p = 0.6549.** The observed dispersion is actually *less* than would be expected from within-study sampling error alone (Q < df), giving no indication of excess variation.
- **I² = 0.0%.** None of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to real between-study differences rather than chance.
- **τ² = 0.0.** The REML estimate of the between-study variance is zero, meaning the random-effects model collapses to the fixed-effect solution; no prediction interval wider than the confidence interval can be constructed, and the pooled confidence interval is not inflated by unexplained dispersion.

Interpretively, this homogeneity means that the 13 trials behave as though they are estimating a **single common underlying odds ratio**, despite differences in era, technique and setting. The result is therefore internally consistent: the apparent disagreement between individual trials — some with intervals excluding the null in the unfavourable direction, one in the favourable direction — is fully explicable as sampling noise around a common effect, which is exactly what one expects when a rare binary outcome is counted in trials of 50 to 500 participants.

Two caveats temper this reassuring picture. First, **I² and Q have low power when events are sparse and when k is modest**; a τ² of zero should be read as "no detectable heterogeneity" rather than as positive proof of perfect consistency. Second, with τ² = 0 the random-effects and fixed-effect estimates coincide, so the apparent precision of the pooled estimate derives entirely from within-trial information; the width of the interval (0.776 to 2.338) is driven by the **scarcity of transfusion events**, not by disagreement among trials.

# Moderators

No formal moderator analysis — subgroup comparison or meta-regression — was performed, and none is interpretable in this dataset, for three converging reasons.

1. **There is no heterogeneity to explain.** With I² = 0.0% and τ² = 0.0, the residual between-study variance available for a moderator to account for is nil. Meta-regression under these conditions can only redistribute sampling error and will produce spuriously precise or spuriously significant coefficients.
2. **k is too small for reliable covariate modelling.** With 13 comparisons, even a single moderator approaches the commonly cited minimum of roughly ten studies per covariate, and any multivariable model would be uninterpretable. Clinically plausible moderators — laparoscopic subtype (LAVH vs TLH vs unspecified), mean uterine weight, surgeon caseload, publication decade, transfusion threshold policy, and proportion of cases with concurrent prolapse surgery — could not be modelled without substantial risk of overfitting.
3. **Sparse outcome data.** Several comparisons contribute zero or near-zero events. Subgroup splitting of such data produces strata in which the odds ratio is non-estimable or wildly unstable.

Descriptively, the **bubble chart** (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) was inspected for a relationship between study size and effect magnitude. The largest trial (n = 504) produced a modest estimate close to the centre of the distribution, while the most extreme values in both directions (study_13_R13 at −1.078 and study_6_R6 at +0.957) came from the smallest trials (n = 60 each) — the classic funnel pattern of greater scatter at lower precision, with no systematic directional gradient. This visual impression is corroborated quantitatively by the Egger test reported below.

Accordingly, the pooled odds ratio of 1.347 (95% CI 0.776 to 2.338) should be read as a **single, unstratified summary**, with any differences between laparoscopic subtypes, uterine sizes or surgical eras remaining untested rather than excluded.

# Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Two complementary diagnostics were applied.

**Egger's regression test for funnel plot asymmetry.** The regression intercept was **0.3016 with p = 0.8994**. This is very far from conventional significance and provides **no evidence** that effect estimates are systematically related to study precision — that is, small trials in this collection did not report systematically larger (or smaller) transfusion contrasts than large trials.

**Trim-and-fill.** The Duval–Tweedie procedure imputed **k₀ = 0 missing studies**. Because no studies were imputed, the adjusted estimate is identical to the observed one; the adjusted value is reported on the standardized display scale as 0.2108, which corresponds to the unchanged, unadjusted synthesis. There is thus no indication that the funnel is asymmetrically truncated on either side.

**Overall risk rating: low.**

**Interpretation (as generated by the analysis pipeline).** Egger's regression test shows no evidence of funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.899, far from conventional significance), indicating that effect sizes are not systematically related to study precision. Consistent with this, the trim-and-fill procedure imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the adjusted pooled estimate is identical to the original — small-study effects do not appear to be distorting the meta-analytic result.

**Caveat (as generated by the analysis pipeline).** These tests have low statistical power, particularly when the number of included studies is small (<10) or between-study heterogeneity is high, so a "low risk" rating reflects the absence of detectable asymmetry rather than positive proof that no publication bias exists. Grey-literature searching and trial-registry checks remain the stronger safeguards.

To this we add an outcome-specific consideration. Blood transfusion is almost never a trial's primary endpoint in this literature; it is reported as one of a battery of safety outcomes. Selective *publication* on the basis of a transfusion result is therefore intrinsically unlikely. Selective *reporting* within published trials is a more realistic threat — trials in which no transfusions occurred may simply have omitted the outcome rather than stating "0/n in both arms" — and such omission would be invisible to funnel-based diagnostics. This form of attrition would most plausibly remove uninformative zero-event rows and would not be expected to bias the pooled ratio in a predictable direction, but it does mean the true denominator of randomised women observed for this outcome may exceed 1,418.

# Discussion

## Principal findings

Across 13 randomised comparisons involving 1,418 women undergoing hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease, a laparoscopic approach was **not shown to change the odds of requiring a perioperative blood transfusion** relative to a vaginal approach. The pooled odds ratio was 1.347 (95% CI 0.776 to 2.338; p = 0.290), and the direction classification for this result is **neither arm favoured** — an essential tie. Heterogeneity was entirely absent (Q = 9.556, df = 12, p = 0.6549; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), and there was no detectable small-study or publication-bias signal (Egger p = 0.8994; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0).

It is worth being explicit about what this does and does not establish. The analysis establishes that **the available randomised evidence does not demonstrate a transfusion advantage for either route**. It does *not* establish equivalence. The upper confidence limit of 2.338 means that a more than two-fold increase in the odds of transfusion with laparoscopic hysterectomy remains statistically compatible with these data, and the lower limit of 0.776 leaves room for a modest advantage in the opposite direction. Given a baseline transfusion rate in the low single-digit percentages typical of minimally invasive hysterectomy for benign disease, even the upper bound translates into a small absolute difference — on the order of a few additional transfusions per hundred women at most — but it is not a negligible one from a service-planning perspective.

## Relation to surgical physiology and prior evidence

The null result is biologically coherent. Both routes avoid a laparotomy incision and both achieve haemostasis by sequential pedicle control; the differences between them lie in the direction of approach and the energy modality used, rather than in the fundamental vascular anatomy divided. Reported differences in *estimated blood loss* between the two routes in individual trials have generally been of a magnitude (tens to low hundreds of millilitres) well below the threshold at which a healthy woman would require transfusion. It is therefore entirely expected that a statistically detectable difference in a surrogate such as millilitres lost or grams of haemoglobin would fail to translate into a difference in the dichotomous, clinically triggered endpoint of transfusion. This dissociation between surrogate and hard endpoint is one of the more useful messages of the present analysis, and it reinforces the protocol decision to exclude blood loss volume, haemoglobin drop and haematoma from the outcome definition.

The finding is broadly consistent with the prior synthesised literature on the surgical approach to hysterectomy, in which vaginal and laparoscopic routes have repeatedly been found comparable on major haemorrhagic morbidity while differing on operating time, cost and the ability to inspect the peritoneal cavity. The present analysis adds value chiefly through the strictness of its outcome definition (counts of women transfused against intention-to-treat denominators only), its explicit handling of zero-event trials and shared control arms, and its native odds-ratio pooling.

## Clinical implications

For the practising gynaecologist, the implication is that **transfusion risk should not be a deciding factor** when choosing between a laparoscopic and a vaginal route for benign hysterectomy. Neither route can be recommended over the other on this ground. Decisions should instead rest on the established determinants: uterine size and mobility, vaginal access and parity, the presence of prolapse (favouring the vaginal route), suspected adnexal or endometriotic pathology requiring inspection and treatment (favouring the laparoscopic route), prior pelvic surgery, surgeon training and caseload, and informed patient preference regarding recovery, scarring and operating time. Where vaginal hysterectomy is feasible, the long-standing recommendation to prefer it rests on operating time, cost and the avoidance of abdominal entry — not on any demonstrated transfusion benefit, and the present analysis neither strengthens nor undermines that recommendation.

Services should also note that the absolute event rate is low enough that routine group-and-save policies, rather than route-specific transfusion protocols, are the rational response for both approaches.

## Strengths

The review has several methodological strengths. The outcome was defined narrowly and *a priori* as a count of women transfused, with surrogate bleeding measures explicitly excluded, eliminating the measurement heterogeneity that plagues blood-loss comparisons. Intention-to-treat denominators (the number randomised) were used throughout, so that conversions from laparoscopic to abdominal surgery — a recognised source of bias in per-protocol analyses of this literature — cannot dilute the comparison. Zero-event trials were deliberately retained as visible rows rather than silently dropped. Shared control arms in multi-arm trials were split to avoid unit-of-analysis error. Pooling was performed natively on the odds-ratio scale with a REML random-effects model, and the effect measure was verified rather than assumed. Finally, heterogeneity and small-study effects were both formally assessed and both were reassuringly absent.

# Data Quality and Validation Notes

The following data-handling matters are recorded plainly for the reader's assessment:

- **Fourteen trials were included in the systematic review but only thirteen contributed an estimable effect size** to the quantitative synthesis (k = 13). The discrepancy arises because at least one included trial's transfusion record could not be converted into a ratio estimate — the expected cause in this outcome being a trial with **zero transfusions in both arms**, which carries sample-size information but no information about relative odds. Such rows were retained in the extracted dataset with their arm sizes as required by the protocol, but they are necessarily non-estimable on the odds-ratio scale and do not enter the pooled calculation. The pooled denominator of n = 1,418 therefore reflects the 13 estimable comparisons, not the full randomised population of all 14 trials.
- **Reviews, editorials and non-randomised reports** encountered during screening were excluded from the dataset and used only as sources of references.
- **Companion and duplicate publications** of trials already represented were excluded at full text so that no randomised population contributes twice.
- **Shared vaginal hysterectomy control groups** in multi-arm trials were split equally between the laparoscopic comparisons they served, which halves the control-arm weight in each such comparison and is a deliberate conservative device against double-counting.
- **Abdominal and robotic hysterectomy arms**, where present in otherwise eligible trials, were ignored by protocol and contribute neither events nor participants.
- **Two effect metrics appear in this report.** Study-level values in Table 2 are standardized (g-metric) representations used for display and for the regression-based bias diagnostics, whereas the pooled result is a **confirmed native odds ratio** (`native_pooling_used = true`, `effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`, no measure conflict). The trim-and-fill adjusted value (0.2108) is likewise reported on the standardized display scale. These scales are not interchangeable and no arithmetic should be performed across them.
- No additional automated validation flags were raised for this dataset.

# Limitations

1. **Imprecision is the dominant limitation.** Blood transfusion is a rare event after minimally invasive hysterectomy for benign disease. With 1,418 randomised women and a low baseline event rate, the analysis has limited power to detect anything but a large relative difference. The confidence interval (0.776 to 2.338) is wide, and a clinically relevant effect in either direction cannot be excluded. This is a *non-significant* result, not a demonstration of *no difference*.
2. **Sparse and zero-event data.** Several comparisons contributed zero or very few events, and at least one included trial was non-estimable entirely. Odds ratios derived from sparse 2×2 tables are known to behave poorly, with inflated variance and sensitivity to the handling of empty cells.
3. **Intervention heterogeneity was not modelled.** LAVH, TLH and "laparoscopic hysterectomy of unspecified type" were pooled as a single intervention class. These differ materially in how much of the operation is performed abdominally versus vaginally, and in which pedicles are divided laparoscopically. A transfusion difference confined to one subtype would be diluted by this pooling and could not be detected with k = 13.
4. **No moderator or subgroup analyses were possible.** Uterine weight, surgeon experience, era of surgery, concurrent prolapse repair and institutional transfusion thresholds are all plausible effect modifiers that remain untested. Institutional transfusion thresholds in particular have shifted markedly toward restrictive practice over the decades spanned by this literature, which could alter the event definition across trials in a way the homogeneity statistics are not powered to reveal.
5. **Risk of bias in the included trials was not formally graded within this synthesis.** Surgical trials cannot blind the operator, and blinding of outcome assessors for a transfusion decision is rarely attempted; the transfusion decision is therefore susceptible to performance bias, albeit less so than estimated blood loss. Allocation concealment and attrition were not quantified here.
6. **Selective outcome reporting cannot be excluded.** Trials with no transfusions in either arm may have omitted the outcome rather than reporting zeros, and such omissions are undetectable by funnel-based methods. The formally low risk-of-bias rating for publication bias rests on tests (Egger, trim-and-fill) that are themselves underpowered at k = 13 and reflects the *absence of detectable asymmetry* rather than positive evidence of its absence.
7. **Zero between-study variance may be an artefact of sparsity.** τ² = 0 and I² = 0.0% are consistent with a genuinely common effect, but also with insufficient information to detect dispersion in a rare-event outcome.
8. **Applicability.** Trials of surgical route enrol women in whom *both* routes are judged feasible — typically those with moderate uterine size and adequate vaginal access. The result does not extend to women with very large uteri, nulliparity with a narrow vagina, or extensive adhesive disease, in whom route is determined by feasibility rather than choice and in whom haemorrhagic risk may differ substantially.

# Conclusion

In women undergoing hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease, this meta-analysis of 13 randomised comparisons (1,418 randomised women) found **no evidence that a laparoscopic approach — LAVH, TLH, or laparoscopic hysterectomy of unspecified type — changes the odds of requiring a blood transfusion during the operation and hospital stay or up to the end of follow-up** relative to vaginal hysterectomy. The pooled odds ratio was **1.347 (95% CI 0.776 to 2.338; p = 0.290)**, a result that favours **neither arm**. Heterogeneity was entirely absent (Q = 9.556, df = 12, p = 0.6549; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), indicating a consistent and stable signal across trials, and there was no detectable small-study or publication-bias effect (Egger intercept 0.3016, p = 0.8994; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, estimate unchanged; risk rated low).

The finding is best characterised as a **stable but imprecise null**. Consistency across trials is high, but the rarity of transfusion means the confidence interval remains compatible with both a modest benefit and a more than two-fold increase in transfusion odds with the laparoscopic route. Clinicians should therefore not use transfusion risk to discriminate between these two minimally invasive routes, and should continue to select the route of hysterectomy on the basis of uterine size and access, concurrent pathology, prolapse, surgeon expertise and patient preference.

Future randomised work addressing haemorrhagic safety in this field will not resolve the question through further small trials; with event rates of a few percent, a definitive comparison would require several thousand participants. The more efficient path forward is prospective harmonisation of transfusion reporting — including explicit reporting of zero-event arms — within pragmatic registry-embedded trials and large surgical registries, together with reporting stratified by laparoscopic subtype and uterine weight so that the subgroup questions left open here can eventually be answered.
