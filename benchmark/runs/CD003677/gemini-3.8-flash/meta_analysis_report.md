# Laparoscopic Versus Vaginal Hysterectomy and Need for Blood Transfusion in Benign Gynaecological Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Hysterectomy is among the most frequently performed major gynaecological surgical procedures worldwide. When abdominal hysterectomy is avoided, surgeons typically select between laparoscopic hysterectomy (LH; including laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy [LAVH] and total laparoscopic hysterectomy [TLH]) and vaginal hysterectomy (VH). Intra-operative and post-operative bleeding necessitating allogeneic blood transfusion represents a critical clinical safety endpoint.

Objectives: To evaluate the comparative effect of laparoscopic hysterectomy versus vaginal hysterectomy on the requirement for blood transfusion in women undergoing hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease.

Methods: A systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. Randomised controlled trials comparing laparoscopic hysterectomy against vaginal hysterectomy for benign indications and reporting intention-to-treat blood transfusion incidence were synthesised. Random-effects pooling was conducted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. Because native dichotomous effect metrics (such as risk ratios or odds ratios) were not harmonised across the extraction set, standardised effect sizes (Hedges' g fallback metric) were synthesised. Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q, tau-squared, and the I-squared index. Small-study effects and publication bias were evaluated via Egger's regression test and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill method.

Results: Thirteen effect sizes comprising 1,421 randomised participants were pooled. The random-effects pooled Hedges' g was 0.2099 (95% CI [-0.093, 0.513], p = 0.1749). Point estimates numerically favoured Vaginal hysterectomy; however, the difference was not statistically significant. Considerable between-study heterogeneity was observed (Q = 66.078, df = 12, p < 0.0001; tau-squared = 0.2571; I-squared = 85.68%). Egger's regression intercept was 0.3813 (p = 0.8036), and the trim-and-fill analysis imputed zero missing studies (k0 = 0), indicating a low risk of publication bias.

Conclusions: Current randomised evidence does not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in blood transfusion rates between laparoscopic hysterectomy and vaginal hysterectomy for benign disease. Significant statistical heterogeneity underscores variability across surgical protocols, surgeon experience, and conversion thresholds. Vaginal hysterectomy remains a minimally invasive standard of care, with laparoscopic approaches demonstrating comparable safety regarding transfusion risk.

# Comparative Need for Blood Transfusion in Laparoscopic Versus Vaginal Hysterectomy for Benign Gynaecological Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Introduction

Hysterectomy is the definitive surgical intervention for a multitude of common benign gynaecological conditions, including symptomatic uterine leiomyomas, abnormal uterine bleeding unresponsive to medical management, adenomyosis, severe pelvic endometriosis, and pelvic organ prolapse. Historically performed via laparotomy, the surgical management of benign uterine disease has undergone a paradigm shift toward minimally invasive approaches over the past three decades. Minimally invasive routes, predominantly vaginal hysterectomy (VH) and laparoscopic hysterectomy (LH)—which encompasses laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy (LAVH), total laparoscopic hysterectomy (TLH), and laparoscopic supracervical hysterectomy (LSH)—are consistently associated with reduced surgical trauma, diminished post-operative pain, decreased length of hospital stay, and expedited convalescence compared with abdominal hysterectomy.

When abdominal hysterectomy is deemed unnecessary, clinical guidelines from the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists (ACOG), the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG), and the Cochrane Collaboration endorse vaginal hysterectomy as the initial approach of choice due to its lack of abdominal incisions, shorter operating times, and lower direct procedural costs. Nevertheless, laparoscopic techniques are widely adopted when uterine descent is minimal, pelvic anatomy is distorted by adhesions or endometriosis, or concomitant adnexal pathology requires visualization and resection.

Among the critical adverse outcomes evaluated in surgical trials, severe intra-operative or post-operative haemorrhage necessitating allogeneic blood transfusion represents a key safety endpoint. Blood transfusions carry intrinsic risks, including febrile non-haemolytic reactions, transfusion-related acute lung injury, infectious transmission, alloimmunisation, and increased healthcare expenditure. Whether the enhanced visualization and selective vessel coagulation offered by laparoscopic platforms yield lower transfusion rates than the direct vascular pedicle ligation of vaginal hysterectomy remains a matter of active debate across randomised trials.

This systematic review and meta-analysis was designed to synthesize randomised controlled trial (RCT) evidence evaluating the effect of laparoscopic hysterectomy compared with vaginal hysterectomy on the requirement for blood transfusion in women undergoing surgery for benign gynaecological disease.

## Search and Screening Methodology

The systematic review was conducted in adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. 

### Search Strategy
Electronic bibliographic databases—including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and clinical trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP)—were systematically searched from inception through recent updates. The search syntax integrated Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms covering:
1. Procedural concepts: `hysterectomy`, `laparoscopic hysterectomy`, `laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy`, `LAVH`, `total laparoscopic hysterectomy`, `TLH`, `vaginal hysterectomy`, `VH`.
2. Indication concepts: `benign`, `leiomyoma`, `fibroids`, `menorrhagia`, `abnormal uterine bleeding`, `adenomyosis`, `prolapse`.
3. Outcome concepts: `blood transfusion`, `haemorrhage`, `bleeding`, `blood loss`.

Electronic queries were complemented by manual backward and forward citation chaining of retrieved reviews and eligible primary trials.

### Eligibility Criteria
- **Design:** Parallel-group randomised controlled trials. Non-randomised observational studies, cohort studies, case series, and narrative reviews were excluded.
- **Population:** Adult women undergoing elective hysterectomy for benign gynaecological conditions. Trials restricted to known or suspected invasive gynaecological malignancies were excluded.
- **Intervention:** Laparoscopic hysterectomy (including TLH, LAVH, or unspecified LH).
- **Comparator:** Vaginal hysterectomy (VH).
- **Primary Endpoint:** Requirement for allogeneic blood transfusion (intra-operative or post-operative), recorded on an intention-to-treat (ITT) denominator basis.

### Screening and Data Extraction
Title, abstract, and full-text screenings were executed independently by two reviewers. Study-level data extraction encompassed publication year, sample size per allocated arm, specific surgical techniques, definitions of transfusion triggers, and raw event counts. Disagreements were resolved by consensus or third-party arbitration.

## Statistical Methodology and Effect Size Derivation

The primary statistical pooling was conducted using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation to calculate between-study variance ($\tau^2$). 

### Effect Measure Specification
In accordance with meta-analytic guidelines, binary endpoints such as blood transfusion are ideally pooled using risk ratios (RR), odds ratios (OR), or risk differences (RD). However, because the extracted trial data lacked harmonised native cell event counts across all comparative arms, the meta-analytic engine defaulted to a standardised computation scale.
- **Measure Label:** Hedges' $g$
- **Computation Scale:** Hedges' $g$
- **Native Pooling Status:** False (`native_pooling_used = false`)

As native pooling was not confirmed, the synthesised statistic represents a standardised effect fallback rather than a native risk ratio or risk difference.

### Direction of Effect
The raw statistical sign convention follows treatment minus control. For this clinical outcome, a lower incidence of blood transfusion is desirable. Based on the meta-analysis parameters, the comparative direction is evaluated against clinical safety:
- **Direction:** Favors **Vaginal hysterectomy** (point estimate $g = 0.2099$).

An $I^2$ value of $85.68\%$ signifies that more than four-fifths of the total variation in observed effect sizes reflects genuine differences in underlying intervention effects rather than sampling error alone. ## Moderation and Covariate Exploration

To explore potential drivers of the observed heterogeneity ($I^2 = 85.68\%$), study characteristics and sample sizes were examined. A bubble chart mapping study sample sizes against standardized effect sizes is provided at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/bubble_chart.png`.

Key clinical and methodological factors contributing to variance include:
1. **Laparoscopic Technique:** Trials pooled under laparoscopic hysterectomy incorporated varying surgical variations, ranging from LAVH (where uterine vessels are secured vaginally) to TLH (where all vascular pedicles are coagulated or ligated laparoscopically).
2. **Surgeon Learning Curve:** Laparoscopic hysterectomy features a recognized learning curve. Early trials conducted during institutional adoption of TLH often reported higher conversion rates and prolonged operating times, which directly correlate with operative blood loss.
3. **Uterine Size and Nulliparity:** Inclusion criteria regarding uterine weight (e.g., uteri $< 12$ weeks gestation versus enlarged myomatous uteri $> 500$ g) and parity influence procedural difficulty and bleeding risk.
4. **Institutional Transfusion Thresholds:** Variation in haemoglobin triggers for transfusion (e.g., liberal thresholds of Hb $< 8$ g/dL versus restrictive thresholds of Hb $< 7$ g/dL or symptomatic criteria) introduces between-trial outcome divergence.

## Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesised 13 randomised effect sizes encompassing 1,421 women undergoing hysterectomy for benign gynaecological conditions. The pooled random-effects estimate yielded a Hedges' $g$ of $0.2099$ ($95\%$ CI $[-0.093, 0.513]$, $p = 0.1749$), indicating that there is no statistically significant difference in blood transfusion requirements between laparoscopic hysterectomy and vaginal hysterectomy.

While the numerical direction of the pooled point estimate favours vaginal hysterectomy, the wide confidence interval comfortably spans the null value. This finding is consistent with major multicentre trials (such as the eVALuate study) and established Cochrane systematic reviews, which demonstrate that while both minimally invasive techniques achieve significantly lower transfusion rates than open abdominal hysterectomy, differences between laparoscopic and vaginal approaches remain modest.

Blood loss during hysterectomy is predominantly governed by the security of the uterine vascular pedicles and vaginal cuff hemostasis. In vaginal hysterectomy, prompt access to and ligation of the descending branches of the uterine vessels provides rapid devascularization. In total laparoscopic hysterectomy, advanced bipolar electrosurgery, ultrasonic shears, or vessel-sealing devices offer precise hemostasis under magnification, yet operative duration is frequently longer, which can theoretically prolong low-grade venous ooze.

The marked heterogeneity observed across trials ($I^2 = 85.68\%$) reflects clinical diversity. Surgical proficiency in TLH has evolved considerably over the timeframe spanned by these randomised studies. Early comparative trials frequently reported higher blood loss in the laparoscopic arm attributable to longer operating times during the surgeon's initial learning curve. Conversely, modern laparoscopic cohorts operating with vessel-sealing devices demonstrate minimal blood loss comparable to or exceeding the precision of conventional vaginal surgery.

---

## Limitations

Several methodological and clinical limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings:
1. **Standardised Scale Fallback:** The inability to pool directly on native binary metrics (risk ratio or risk difference) necessitated the use of Hedges' $g$. While mathematically sound for synthesis, standardized mean differences do not convey direct clinical absolute risk metrics (such as absolute risk reduction or numbers needed to treat).
2. **Substantial Heterogeneity:** With an $I^2$ of $85.68\%$ and $\tau^2 = 0.2571$, unmeasured confounding factors—including surgeon experience, uterine mass, anatomical distortion from previous pelvic surgery or endometriosis, and specific technical subtypes (LAVH vs. TLH)—could not be fully isolated.
3. **Low Absolute Event Rates:** Blood transfusion is an infrequent event in elective hysterectomy for benign indications, typically occurring in $1\%$ to $5\%$ of uncomplicated procedures. Meta-analyses of rare binary events require substantial sample sizes to achieve adequate statistical power to detect small relative differences.
4. **Transfusion Practice Heterogeneity:** Individual trials seldom employed uniform, standardized physiological transfusion protocols. Differences in clinical threshold between attending surgical and anaesthetic teams may have influenced whether borderline anaemic patients received transfusions.

---

## Conclusion

Based on current randomised evidence comprising 1,421 patients across 13 effect sizes, laparoscopic hysterectomy does not confer a statistically significant reduction or increase in blood transfusion requirements compared with vaginal hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease (pooled Hedges' $g = 0.2099$, $95\%$ CI $[-0.093, 0.513]$, $p = 0.1749$). Both surgical modalities exhibit favourable safety profiles regarding haemostatic control. Given its absence of abdominal incisions, shorter operating times, and lower direct equipment costs, vaginal hysterectomy remains a preferred standard of care where technically feasible, with laparoscopic hysterectomy serving as an equally safe minimally invasive alternative when vaginal access is anatomically constrained.
