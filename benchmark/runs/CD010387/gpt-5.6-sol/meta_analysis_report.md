# Short-Term Effects of Exercise Therapy on Functional Ability in Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Patellofemoral pain syndrome can impair knee-related function. This review evaluated whether exercise therapy, delivered alone or added to a co-intervention, improves short-term functional ability compared with no treatment, placebo, waiting list, health educational material, or the same co-intervention without exercise.

### Methods
Eligible outcomes were validated knee-function questionnaires assessed up to 3 months after randomization or treatment initiation. When multiple measures were available, the intended hierarchy was Anterior Knee Pain Scale (AKPS)/Kujala score, Functional Index Questionnaire, WOMAC function, Lysholm score, and then another validated instrument. Outcomes were harmonized so that positive standardized effects represented better function with exercise. A random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled three effect sizes. Because the source effect measure could not be confirmed, Hedges' g was used as a standardized fallback rather than as a confirmed native-scale result.

### Results
The evidence pipeline contained six studies, but only three studies contributed estimable short-term functional outcomes (166 participants). Individual Hedges' g values were −0.262842 (95% CI −0.755 to 0.229; n=64), 1.266708 (95% CI 0.610 to 1.923; n=43), and 1.007446 (95% CI 0.466 to 1.549; n=59). The pooled Hedges' g was 0.6540248738013781 (95% CI −0.281 to 1.589; p=0.17050676166648984), favoring exercise therapy, alone or added to a co-intervention, but the confidence interval included no effect. Heterogeneity was considerable (I²=88.06%; τ²=0.599884; Q=17.793, p=0.0001). Egger's intercept was −3.9657 (p=0.1358), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀=0); these analyses were severely underpowered with only three studies.

### Conclusions
The point estimate favored exercise therapy for short-term functional ability, but statistical uncertainty and substantial between-study heterogeneity preclude a definitive conclusion. The sparse evidence base, unavailable study-level methodological details, and use of a standardized fallback effect measure limit confidence in the result.

## Introduction

Patellofemoral pain syndrome is a common cause of anterior knee pain and can substantially restrict daily, occupational, and sporting activities. Exercise therapy is frequently prescribed to improve pain, strength, movement control, and knee-related function. However, the magnitude and consistency of its short-term effect on patient-reported functional ability require quantitative synthesis.

The review question was: **In people with patellofemoral pain syndrome, what is the short-term effect (up to 3 months) of exercise therapy versus no treatment, placebo, waiting list, health educational material, or—when exercise is added to a co-intervention—the same co-intervention alone, on functional ability measured with knee-function questionnaires?**

The intervention of interest was exercise therapy administered alone or as an addition to a co-intervention. Eligible comparators were no treatment, placebo, waiting list, health educational material, or the identical co-intervention without exercise. Eligible outcomes were validated knee-function questionnaires measured no later than 3 months after randomization or treatment initiation. The intended outcome hierarchy was AKPS/Kujala, Functional Index Questionnaire (FIQ), WOMAC function, Lysholm score, and then another validated knee-function questionnaire. Where more than one eligible assessment occurred within 3 months, the intended selection rule was to use the assessment closest to 3 months without counting the same participants twice.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects meta-analysis using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) included **three studies and 166 participants**. The **pooled Hedges' g was 0.6540248738013781** (95% CI **−0.281 to 1.589**; p=**0.17050676166648984**). According to the prespecified result field, this point estimate **favored exercise therapy, alone or added to a co-intervention**. However, the 95% confidence interval included the null and extended from a possible disadvantage to a large benefit; the pooled result was not statistically significant at the conventional 0.05 level.

Although the point estimate is positive and of moderate standardized magnitude, its clinical importance cannot be established from these data. A standardized effect does not identify the corresponding change on a particular knee-function questionnaire, and no minimally important difference was supplied. Moreover, the effect-measure source was recorded as `unresolved_fallback`, and native-scale pooling was not used. The result is therefore a standardized fallback estimate, not a confirmed native-scale treatment effect.

The cumulative meta-analysis visualization is available at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/cumulative_plot.png`. Given the very small number of studies and their heterogeneity, any apparent evolution or stabilization of the cumulative estimate should be interpreted cautiously.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was substantial:

- **Cochran's Q:** 17.793
- **Q-test p value:** 0.0001
- **I²:** 88.06%
- **τ²:** 0.599884

The Q test rejected the hypothesis that all observed variation was attributable solely to sampling error. The I² value indicates that a large proportion of observed variability was associated with between-study differences rather than within-study sampling variation. The estimated between-study variance was also sizable on the standardized-effect scale.

The heterogeneity summary is shown at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/heterogeneity_summary.png`. Potential clinical explanations could include variation in exercise content and dose, comparator intensity, participant characteristics, selected questionnaires, and assessment timing. These explanations remain hypothetical because the necessary study-level characteristics were not provided. With only three effect sizes, τ², I², and the pooled confidence interval are themselves imprecisely estimated.

## Moderators

No numerical subgroup analysis or meta-regression results were supplied. Therefore, no moderator—such as exercise type, exercise dose, addition to a co-intervention, comparator category, questionnaire, follow-up time, baseline severity, or risk of bias—could be tested or claimed to explain heterogeneity.

## Publication Bias

Egger's regression produced an intercept of **−3.9657** with p=**0.1358**. The trim-and-fill procedure estimated **k₀=0** missing studies and retained an adjusted Hedges' g of **0.654**. The automated assessment classified publication-bias risk as low, and the supplied interpretation stated that there was no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects.

These results should not be interpreted as demonstrating the absence of publication bias. With only three studies, Egger's test has extremely low power, funnel-plot asymmetry cannot be assessed reliably, and trim-and-fill can fail to identify missing studies. The non-significant Egger result is therefore best described as **inconclusive rather than reassuring**. Selective non-reporting of functional outcomes also cannot be assessed without protocols or trial registrations.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across three studies involving 166 participants, the random-effects point estimate favored exercise therapy for short-term knee-related functional ability. Nevertheless, the pooled result was not statistically significant, and its wide 95% confidence interval encompassed both possible harm and substantial benefit. The evidence was highly inconsistent, with one estimate slightly favoring control and two estimates strongly favoring exercise.

The substantial heterogeneity is central to interpretation. A single average effect may not adequately represent all exercise programs, populations, comparator conditions, or measurement instruments included in the synthesis. In particular, pooling an exercise-only comparison with comparisons in which exercise was added to another intervention may combine clinically different treatment contrasts, even though both satisfy the stated research question.

### Clinical interpretation

The analysis suggests that exercise therapy **may** improve short-term function, but it does not provide conclusive evidence of an average benefit. The standardized point estimate cannot establish whether a typical patient would experience a clinically important improvement on the AKPS/Kujala score or another knee-specific questionnaire. Clinicians should therefore avoid treating the pooled value as a precise estimate applicable to every exercise regimen or patient subgroup.

Exercise decisions may still reasonably incorporate established clinical considerations such as patient preferences, symptom irritability, functional goals, feasibility, adherence, and tolerance. However, those considerations were not tested by this meta-analysis.

### Implications for research

Future trials should use clearly defined diagnostic criteria, prespecify a validated core functional outcome, report outcome data at standardized short-term time points, and provide sufficient data for native-scale synthesis. Exercise components, dose, progression, adherence, and co-interventions should be reported in replicable detail. Trial registration and complete reporting of all prespecified outcomes would facilitate assessment of selective reporting.

A future update should obtain complete study reports, verify participant overlap, apply the questionnaire hierarchy directly, conduct a structured risk-of-bias assessment, and—if enough studies accrue—investigate prespecified moderators. Native-scale mean differences should be preferred when trials use the same questionnaire and scale; standardized effects are appropriate only when genuinely different instruments measure the same construct.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

- The effect measure could not be confirmed from the source data. Hedges' g was therefore used as a **standardized fallback**, not as a confirmed native-scale effect.
- Six studies appeared in the evidence pipeline, but only three studies yielded effect sizes for the meta-analysis. The supplied data did not explain, at study level, why the other three did not contribute to pooling.
- One document (`manual:60c6ac38`) was excluded from the primary-study set because it was a systematic review/meta-analysis synthesizing ten trials rather than a newly conducted primary trial.
- Bibliographic and clinical details for the included studies were unavailable, preventing verification of outcome selection, follow-up timing, intervention comparability, participant overlap, and risk of bias.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations:

1. **Sparse evidence:** Only three studies and 166 participants contributed to the pooled estimate.
2. **Substantial heterogeneity:** I² was 88.06%, and the study estimates differed markedly in magnitude and direction.
3. **Unconfirmed effect measure:** The underlying measure was unresolved; Hedges' g was a standardized fallback rather than a confirmed native-scale result.
4. **Incomplete study information:** Trial citations, participant characteristics, intervention details, comparator details, questionnaires, exact follow-up times, and reasons for non-inclusion of three pipeline studies were not supplied.
5. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** The methodological limitations of the trials could not be evaluated, and a formal certainty-of-evidence rating was not possible.
6. **Limited search transparency:** Databases, dates, strategies, screening procedures, and full PRISMA flow counts were unavailable.
7. **Underpowered bias analyses:** Egger's test and trim-and-fill are not dependable with only three studies.
8. **No supported moderator analysis:** The small number of studies and absence of study-level covariates precluded investigation of heterogeneity.
9. **Clinical interpretability:** A standardized effect cannot be directly expressed in points on a familiar knee-function questionnaire or compared with a questionnaire-specific minimally important difference.

## Conclusion

The available random-effects meta-analysis produced a pooled Hedges' g of **0.6540248738013781** (95% CI **−0.281 to 1.589**; p=**0.17050676166648984**) across three studies and 166 participants, with the point estimate favoring exercise therapy, alone or added to a co-intervention. However, the confidence interval included no effect, and heterogeneity was substantial (**I²=88.06%; τ²=0.599884**). Accordingly, the evidence suggests a possible short-term functional benefit but is too uncertain and inconsistent to support a definitive effect estimate. Better-reported, adequately powered trials and a fully reproducible systematic-review update are required.
