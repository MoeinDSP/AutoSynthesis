# Exercise Therapy and Short-Term Functional Ability in Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS) is among the most common causes of anterior knee pain in physically active adolescents and adults. Exercise therapy is widely recommended as first-line management, but the magnitude and certainty of its effect on self-reported knee function in the short term remain contested.

**Objective.** To determine whether exercise therapy — delivered alone or added to a co-intervention — improves self-reported functional ability of the knee compared with control (no treatment, placebo/sham, waiting list, educational material, or the co-intervention alone) in people with PFPS at short-term follow-up (up to approximately 3 months).

**Methods.** A systematic search of controlled trials was screened against prespecified PICO criteria. Eligible studies enrolled people with PFPS, compared exercise therapy (alone or as an add-on) against a non-exercise or co-intervention-only control, and reported a validated patient-reported knee-function instrument (Anterior Knee Pain Scale/Kujala preferred, otherwise Functional Index Questionnaire, WOMAC function or total, Lysholm, or equivalent) at ≤3 months. Effect sizes were expressed as Hedges' g and pooled on the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Six studies met inclusion criteria; five contributed extractable short-term knee-function data (k = 5, N = 379 participants). Individual effects ranged from g = −0.520 (95% CI −1.018 to −0.022) to g = 1.267 (95% CI 0.610 to 1.923). The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was 0.399 (95% CI −0.210 to 1.008; p = 0.199), a point estimate favouring exercise therapy (alone or exercise plus co-intervention) but with a confidence interval that includes no effect. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial to considerable: Q = 26.032 (p < 0.001), I² = 87.77%, τ² = 0.4176 (τ = 0.646). Egger's regression showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = −1.354, p = 0.207) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0, adjusted g = 0.399), consistent with a low assessed risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** Pooled evidence from five small controlled trials gives a small-to-moderate point estimate favouring exercise therapy for short-term self-reported knee function in PFPS, but the effect is not statistically significant and is accompanied by considerable, unexplained heterogeneity — including one trial favouring control. The direction of effect is consistent with clinical guidance recommending exercise, yet the present synthesis cannot establish a precise or reliable magnitude of benefit. Larger, adequately powered trials using a common function instrument (ideally the Kujala score) and standardized short-term timepoints are needed.

**Note on the effect measure.** The pooled statistic reported here is a Standardized Mean Difference, computed and pooled natively on the SMD scale at explicit user request. Some source reports present results as raw mean differences; the user-specified SMD scale was retained as authoritative, so the pooled value should not be read as a native-scale mean difference in questionnaire points.


## 1. Introduction

Patellofemoral pain syndrome (PFPS), also described as anterior knee pain or retropatellar pain syndrome, is characterised by diffuse peri- or retropatellar pain provoked by activities that load the patellofemoral joint — squatting, stair ascent and descent, running, kneeling and prolonged sitting. It is one of the most frequently encountered musculoskeletal presentations in sports medicine and general physiotherapy practice, with a particularly high burden among adolescents, young adults, military recruits and recreational runners. A substantial proportion of affected individuals report persistent symptoms years after onset, and PFPS has been implicated as a potential precursor to patellofemoral osteoarthritis, making effective early management clinically important.

Conservative management dominates PFPS care, and exercise therapy — most commonly quadriceps strengthening, hip abductor and external rotator strengthening, closed- and open-kinetic-chain loading, and neuromuscular or proprioceptive retraining — is the cornerstone intervention endorsed by consensus statements and clinical practice guidelines. The mechanistic rationale is coherent: exercise is proposed to improve patellar tracking and load distribution, correct proximal and distal kinetic-chain deficits, restore quadriceps force-generating capacity, and desensitise the painful joint through graded loading.

Despite this consensus, the evidence base is less settled than the strength of the recommendations implies. Trials are typically small, use heterogeneous exercise prescriptions (differing in target muscle group, dose, supervision and duration), employ a wide variety of control conditions (true no-treatment, waiting list, sham, education-only, or a shared co-intervention such as taping or orthoses administered to both arms), and measure function with several different patient-reported instruments — the Anterior Knee Pain Scale (Kujala), the Functional Index Questionnaire (FIQ), the WOMAC, and the Lysholm score among them. Pain outcomes have received more synthesis attention than function, and short-term function in particular — the window in which patients and clinicians most often judge whether treatment is "working" — warrants dedicated quantitative appraisal.

This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore addresses a single, focused question: **in people with PFPS, does exercise therapy (alone or added to a co-intervention), compared with control (no treatment, placebo/sham, waiting list, health educational material, or the co-intervention alone), improve self-reported functional ability of the knee at short-term follow-up (up to approximately 3 months)?**

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Reporting standard and protocol

The review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. The review question, eligibility criteria, outcome hierarchy, effect measure and synthesis plan were specified before analysis.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria (PICO)

| Element | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **Population** | People of any age diagnosed with patellofemoral pain syndrome / anterior knee pain / retropatellar pain syndrome, by clinical criteria or exclusion of alternative intra-articular pathology. |
| **Intervention** | Exercise therapy delivered alone, or exercise therapy added to a co-intervention (e.g. exercise + taping, exercise + orthoses, exercise + education). |
| **Comparator** | Control: no treatment, placebo/sham intervention, waiting list, health educational material only, or the co-intervention alone (i.e. the same adjunct without the exercise component). |
| **Outcome** | Self-reported functional ability of the knee measured with a validated knee-function questionnaire. Instrument hierarchy where more than one was reported: (1) Anterior Knee Pain Scale (Kujala); (2) Functional Index Questionnaire (FIQ); (3) WOMAC function subscale or total; (4) Lysholm score; (5) an equivalent patient-reported knee-function measure. |
| **Timing** | Short-term follow-up, defined as up to approximately 3 months post-randomisation/baseline. |
| **Study design** | Controlled trials with a concurrent control arm. |

Studies reporting only pain, only impairment-based or performance-based measures (e.g. isokinetic torque, hop tests), only long-term (>3 month) function, or comparing two active exercise regimens against one another without an eligible control were not eligible for this outcome.

### 2.3 Outcome selection and effect direction

Where a study reported multiple eligible instruments, a single estimate was extracted according to the hierarchy above to preserve statistical independence. Scores were oriented so that **a positive Hedges' g / SMD indicates better functional ability (greater self-reported knee function, i.e. less functional limitation) in the exercise arm than in the control arm.** Instruments scored in the opposite direction (e.g. the FIQ in some formulations, where lower is better) were sign-reversed prior to pooling so that all effects share this common orientation.

### 2.4 Effect size computation

For each comparison, the standardized mean difference between exercise and control at the short-term timepoint was computed from reported means, standard deviations and group sample sizes, and corrected for small-sample bias using Hedges' correction, yielding Hedges' g with its sampling variance and 95% confidence interval.

### 2.5 Effect measure: scale and provenance

The analysis was conducted and pooled **natively on the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) scale** (`measure_label` = "Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)"; `native_pooling_used` = true). The SMD scale was adopted at explicit user request. It is important to record transparently that **a conflict was detected and resolved in favour of the user specification**: one or more source reports explicitly present their results as raw **mean differences (MD)** in questionnaire points, whereas the requested and applied analytic scale is the SMD. The user request was treated as authoritative. Consequently, all pooled values in this report are standardized units (standard-deviation units), not questionnaire points, and should not be back-read as native-scale mean differences. Because the SMD was the scale on which computation and pooling actually occurred, the pooled estimate is a confirmed native-scale result *for the SMD metric* and not a post-hoc conversion of a differently scaled pooled value.

### 2.6 Synthesis methods

Effect sizes were combined in a **random-effects model** with between-study variance (τ²) estimated by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**, which is appropriate given anticipated clinical and methodological diversity in exercise prescription, control condition and outcome instrument. Heterogeneity was characterised using Cochran's Q with its p-value, the I² statistic (proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error) and τ²/τ (absolute between-study variance and standard deviation on the SMD scale).

Robustness was examined through leave-one-out sensitivity analysis and cumulative meta-analysis ordered by precision, and the distribution of effects was inspected graphically. Publication bias / small-study effects were assessed with **Egger's regression test** of funnel asymmetry and **Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill** procedure.

Because only five estimates entered the pooled analysis, formal meta-regression was not undertaken; moderator influences are described qualitatively and graphically (bubble plot of effect size against study size), consistent with the convention that meta-regression requires approximately ten studies per covariate.

## 3. Search and Screening

Records identified through database searching were screened in two stages — title/abstract, then full text — against the PICO criteria in §2.2. Two automated validation gates were applied to the retrieved document set to protect against (i) inclusion of secondary rather than primary evidence and (ii) double-counting of the same trial across multiple reports.

**Six studies** satisfied all eligibility criteria and were retained as primary studies in the included set. Of these, **five** reported extractable short-term (≤3 months) knee-function data in a form permitting computation of a standardized effect size and therefore contributed to the quantitative synthesis; **one included study could not be converted into an effect size** for this outcome (study 4 in the internal numbering) owing to insufficient reported distributional statistics at the short-term timepoint, and so is described narratively but excluded from pooling. Total analysed sample across the five pooled comparisons was **N = 379 participants**.

One retrieved document was excluded at the document-type gate because it was itself a **systematic review/meta-analysis** rather than a primary trial, and one further document could not be classified with confidence by the automated gate and was retained on the basis of curator judgement. These adjudications are set out in full in §10, *Data Quality & Validation Notes*.

## 4. Included Studies

The five pooled comparisons are summarised below. All were controlled trials in participants with PFPS, comparing exercise therapy alone or as an add-on with a non-exercise control condition (no treatment, sham, waiting list, education, or the co-intervention alone), with self-reported knee function assessed at a short-term timepoint of up to approximately three months.

| Study ID | Analysed N | Hedges' g | 95% CI | Direction of individual result |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | 64 | −0.520 | −1.018 to −0.022 | Favours control (statistically significant) |
| study_2_R2 | 131 | 0.234 | −0.110 to 0.577 | Favours exercise (not significant) |
| study_3_R3 | 43 | 1.267 | 0.610 to 1.923 | Favours exercise (statistically significant) |
| study_5_R5 | 60 | 1.001 | 0.464 to 1.537 | Favours exercise (statistically significant) |
| study_6_R6 | 81 | 0.126 | −0.310 to 0.562 | Favours exercise (not significant) |
| **Pooled (k = 5)** | **379** | **0.399 (SMD)** | **−0.210 to 1.008** | **Favours exercise therapy; not significant** |

Several descriptive features of this evidence base bear on interpretation:

- **All trials are small.** Analysed sample sizes range from 43 to 131, with a median of 64. Only one trial (study_2_R2, N = 131) exceeds 100 participants. Small trials yield imprecise individual estimates and are known to be more susceptible to allocation and detection bias and to selective outcome reporting.
- **Effect estimates are widely dispersed and not all in the same direction.** Three of five point estimates lie above g = 0 with confidence intervals excluding zero in two cases; one estimate (study_1_R1) lies *below* zero with a confidence interval excluding zero, i.e. it is a statistically significant result in favour of the **control** condition for short-term function. The confidence intervals of study_1_R1 and study_3_R3 do not overlap at all, which is itself diagnostic of genuine heterogeneity rather than chance variation.
- **The two largest contributors are the two most null.** study_2_R2 (N = 131, g = 0.234) and study_6_R6 (N = 81, g = 0.126) — the first and third largest trials — both produce small, non-significant effects, while the two large positive effects come from the two smallest trials (study_3_R3, N = 43, g = 1.267; study_5_R5, N = 60, g = 1.001). This inverse pattern between sample size and effect magnitude is visible in the bubble plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) and is a classic small-study-effect signature, although the formal statistical tests for asymmetry were not significant (§8).
- **Clinical heterogeneity is intrinsic to the question as framed.** The comparator definition deliberately spans no treatment, sham, waiting list, educational material and co-intervention-alone designs, and the intervention definition spans exercise alone and exercise-plus-adjunct designs. An "exercise versus waiting list" contrast and an "exercise + taping versus taping alone" contrast are not expected to yield the same effect magnitude, and the outcome instruments differ across trials as well.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Individual effect sizes, expressed as Hedges' g on the SMD scale with positive values favouring exercise therapy, were:

- **study_1_R1:** g = −0.5198, 95% CI [−1.018, −0.022], n = 64. A moderate effect **favouring the control condition**, with the upper confidence limit falling just below zero.
- **study_2_R2:** g = 0.2337, 95% CI [−0.110, 0.577], n = 131. A small effect favouring exercise, compatible with no difference.
- **study_3_R3:** g = 1.2667, 95% CI [0.610, 1.923], n = 43. A large effect favouring exercise; the largest and least precise positive estimate in the set.
- **study_5_R5:** g = 1.0006, 95% CI [0.464, 1.537], n = 60. A large effect favouring exercise.
- **study_6_R6:** g = 0.1262, 95% CI [−0.310, 0.562], n = 81. A negligible effect, essentially null.

The spread from −0.520 to 1.267 spans 1.79 standard-deviation units — from a moderate benefit of control to a large benefit of exercise. The distribution of effects is displayed in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`, which shows a dispersed, arguably bimodal pattern (a cluster of near-null estimates around g ≈ 0.1–0.2, a pair of large positive estimates around g ≈ 1.0–1.3, and one negative outlier) rather than a tight distribution around a single central value.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

A single outcome/timepoint group was analysed (short-term, ≤3 months, self-reported knee function); no additional `meta_analysis_groups` were present, so one pooled estimate is reported.

> **Pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD), random-effects REML, k = 5, N = 379:**
> **SMD = 0.399, 95% CI −0.210 to 1.008, p = 0.199**
> **Direction: favours Exercise therapy (alone or exercise plus co-intervention).**

Interpretation requires care on three points.

**(1) Direction versus significance.** The point estimate is positive and, by the conventional Cohen benchmarks, of small-to-moderate magnitude (0.2 = small, 0.5 = moderate), and it favours exercise therapy. However, the 95% confidence interval comfortably includes zero and p = 0.199 falls well short of conventional significance. The interval is also strikingly wide (spanning 1.22 SD units), encompassing everything from a moderate *disadvantage* of exercise (SMD = −0.21) to a very large advantage (SMD = 1.01). The honest summary is therefore: **the data are directionally consistent with a benefit of exercise therapy on short-term knee function, but do not establish one, and are statistically compatible with no effect.**

**(2) Scale.** The reported statistic is a Standardized Mean Difference computed and pooled natively on the SMD scale, as specified. It expresses the between-arm difference in standard-deviation units of the pooled knee-function score, not in Kujala/FIQ/WOMAC/Lysholm points. Because source reports include at least one presenting raw mean differences, readers wishing to express the result in instrument-specific points would need to multiply the SMD by a relevant population SD for the chosen instrument — a conversion that introduces further assumptions and is not performed here.

**(3) Prediction versus confidence.** With τ = 0.646, the approximate 95% prediction interval for the effect in a new, exchangeable trial is far wider than the confidence interval around the mean — roughly −1.5 to +2.3 — meaning that a future trial of exercise therapy for short-term PFPS function could plausibly report a substantial benefit *or* a substantial disadvantage. This is the single most important quantitative statement in the review.

The forest plot showing individual and pooled estimates is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. The cumulative meta-analysis is at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; because the pooled estimate is dominated by a small number of divergent trials, the cumulative trajectory does not stabilise, reinforcing that the summary effect is not yet settled.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **substantial to considerable**:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 26.032 (df = 4) |
| p (Q) | < 0.001 |
| I² | 87.77% |
| τ² | 0.4175 |
| τ (SMD units) | 0.646 |
| τ² estimator | REML |

Three complementary readings follow. First, the Q test decisively rejects the null hypothesis of a common underlying effect (p < 0.001); the observed dispersion is far greater than sampling error alone would produce. Second, I² = 87.77% indicates that roughly seven-eighths of the observed variability in effect estimates reflects real between-study differences rather than chance — by Cochrane conventions this falls in the "considerable heterogeneity" band (75–100%). Third, and most interpretable clinically, τ = 0.646 means the standard deviation of true effects across trials is itself about two-thirds of a standard-deviation unit — larger than the pooled mean effect of 0.399. In other words, **the variation between trials exceeds the average effect being estimated.**

This degree of heterogeneity has a direct consequence: the pooled mean is a poor summary of the evidence. It is the average of qualitatively different findings — a significant benefit of control in one trial, two large benefits of exercise, and two near-nulls — rather than a stable central tendency from which individual trials deviate only by noise. It is also why the confidence interval is wide and the p-value non-significant despite three of five trials showing positive point estimates: the random-effects model appropriately inflates uncertainty to reflect the dispersion, and with only four degrees of freedom, τ² itself is estimated imprecisely.

Plausible sources of this heterogeneity, all present by design in this evidence base, include: (a) heterogeneity of comparator (no treatment and waiting-list controls generally produce larger apparent intervention effects than sham or co-intervention-alone controls, which control for attention, expectation and the adjunct itself); (b) heterogeneity of intervention (quadriceps-focused versus hip-focused versus mixed programmes; supervised versus home-based; differing dose and duration within the ≤3-month window); (c) heterogeneity of outcome instrument (Kujala, FIQ, WOMAC and Lysholm differ in content, responsiveness and ceiling behaviour, and standardization does not fully reconcile them); (d) heterogeneity of follow-up timing within the 0–3-month window; and (e) differing risk of bias, particularly regarding blinding of outcome assessment in trials where patient-reported outcomes cannot be blinded to the participant.

A graphical summary of the heterogeneity diagnostics is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 8. Moderators

With k = 5, formal meta-regression was **not performed**: the conventional minimum of approximately ten studies per covariate is not met, and fitting moderators to five effect sizes with τ² already large would produce estimates too unstable to support inference. Any subgroup analysis by comparator type, exercise focus, or outcome instrument would rest on one or two studies per cell and is therefore not reported, to avoid presenting spurious precision.

What can be stated descriptively, from the bubble plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`), is a clear **inverse association between study size and effect magnitude** in this sample: the two smallest trials (N = 43 and N = 60) produced the two largest positive effects (g = 1.267 and g = 1.001), while the two larger trials (N = 131 and N = 81) produced near-null effects (g = 0.234 and g = 0.126). The one trial favouring control (N = 64) is mid-sized. This pattern is consistent with several non-exclusive explanations: genuine small-study effects (bias or selective reporting in small trials), differences in design that happen to covary with size (small trials more often use no-treatment or waiting-list controls and less often blind assessors, both of which inflate effects), or chance, which cannot be excluded with five observations. Notably, the formal asymmetry tests in §9 did not detect this pattern as statistically significant — a discrepancy that is itself expected given their very low power at k = 5.

**The practical implication is that the heterogeneity in this review remains unexplained.** Explaining it is a task for future trials and for an updated synthesis with sufficient k, not something the present data can accomplish.

## 9. Publication Bias

Two complementary assessments were conducted:

- **Egger's regression test for funnel plot asymmetry:** intercept = −1.3535, p = 0.2071. No statistically significant asymmetry was detected.
- **Duval & Tweedie trim-and-fill:** k₀ = 0 studies imputed; the adjusted pooled estimate is unchanged at g = 0.3991.

Taken together, these indicate **no detectable small-study effects and a symmetric funnel**, and the overall assessed risk of publication bias is **low**. The trim-and-fill result in particular means the pooled estimate is unlikely to be materially inflated by a hidden stratum of unpublished negative or null findings, at least as far as these methods can discern.

Two caveats must accompany that conclusion, and they are substantial here:

1. **Power.** Egger's test and trim-and-fill are well known to be underpowered when fewer than about ten studies are pooled, and still further compromised when heterogeneity is high. With k = 5 and I² = 87.77%, both conditions obtain. The negative Egger result should therefore be read as "**no signal detected**" rather than "bias ruled out" — absence of evidence of bias is not evidence of absence of bias.
2. **Confounding of asymmetry by heterogeneity.** In the presence of considerable true between-study variance, the relationship between effect size and precision that Egger's test interrogates is obscured; the Egger intercept itself (−1.35) is negative, which if anything runs counter to the classic small-study-inflation pattern, but with p = 0.207 it carries no interpretive weight. Meanwhile, the descriptive inverse size–effect relationship noted in §8 remains visible in the data even though the formal test does not flag it. Readers should weigh the descriptive pattern alongside, not instead of, the formal test.

Two automated validation flags were raised during data preparation and are reported here in full, without mitigation:

## 11. Discussion

This meta-analysis of five controlled trials (N = 379) found a pooled Standardized Mean Difference of **0.399 (95% CI −0.210 to 1.008; p = 0.199)** for exercise therapy versus control on short-term self-reported knee function in patellofemoral pain syndrome. The point estimate **favours exercise therapy** and is of small-to-moderate magnitude, but the result is not statistically significant, the confidence interval spans clinically meaningful effects in both directions, and heterogeneity is considerable (I² = 87.77%, τ² = 0.4175, Q = 26.032, p < 0.001).

**How should this be read against prevailing clinical guidance?** Consensus statements and guidelines recommend exercise therapy as first-line care for PFPS, and this review does not contradict that recommendation — the central tendency of the evidence points in the direction of benefit, publication bias is not detectable, and exercise carries low risk, low cost and established benefits for pain and general health. What this review does do is temper confidence about the *magnitude* of short-term functional benefit. A pooled estimate whose confidence interval includes zero, derived from five small and mutually inconsistent trials, is not a strong quantitative warrant. It is better described as weak, directionally favourable evidence than as demonstrated efficacy for this specific outcome and timepoint.

**Why might the short-term functional signal be weak even if exercise works?** Several explanations are plausible and non-exclusive. First, timing: strength and neuromuscular adaptations, and the downstream functional gains they enable, may require longer than 12 weeks to translate into questionnaire-detectable change, so a ≤3-month window may systematically underestimate the eventual benefit. Second, comparator stringency: the pooled comparator set includes sham and co-intervention-alone arms, which control for attention, expectation and the adjunct itself and therefore shrink the apparent exercise-specific effect relative to no-treatment comparisons — and PFPS is known to respond to non-specific care and natural history in the early period. Third, instrument behaviour: Kujala, FIQ, WOMAC and Lysholm scores differ in content and responsiveness, and function questionnaires in mildly affected young active populations can show ceiling effects that compress detectable change. Fourth, intervention heterogeneity: "exercise therapy" is not one treatment. A supervised, progressive, hip-and-quadriceps programme and an unsupervised generic home programme are both captured by the inclusion criteria, and averaging them may dilute a real effect of the more intensive variants.

**The heterogeneity is the headline finding, not a nuisance parameter.** With τ exceeding the pooled mean effect and one trial significantly favouring control while two significantly favour exercise, the evidence is not merely imprecise — it is inconsistent. The clinically useful question shifts from "does exercise work on average?" to "for whom, with what prescription, against what comparator, and measured how?" Those questions require either individual-participant data or a substantially larger set of trials with standardized measurement, neither of which is available here.

**Comparison with the broader literature.** The validation gate identified at least one systematic review in this field appraising ten clinical trials, which indicates the primary literature is larger than the five trials pooled here. The present synthesis should accordingly be understood as a focused analysis of the trials that met these specific criteria and supplied extractable short-term function data — not as the definitive census of exercise therapy for PFPS. Larger syntheses that pool pain and function across timepoints have generally reported more favourable conclusions; the divergence here is attributable to the narrow outcome definition (short-term function only, one estimate per study), the small k, and the random-effects penalty for high τ².

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small number of pooled studies (k = 5).** This limits precision, destabilises the estimate of τ², precludes meta-regression and subgroup analysis, and severely limits the power of publication-bias diagnostics.
2. **Considerable unexplained heterogeneity (I² = 87.77%, τ = 0.646).** True effects vary more across trials than the pooled mean itself, so the summary estimate has limited generalisability and the prediction interval spans both benefit and harm.
3. **Non-significant pooled result.** The 95% CI (−0.210 to 1.008) includes no effect; the review cannot establish efficacy for this outcome, only a favourable direction.
4. **Small individual trials.** Analysed Ns of 43–131 (median 64) are small for patient-reported outcomes, and small trials are more vulnerable to bias and random high estimates.
5. **Incomplete outcome capture.** One of six included studies supplied no extractable short-term function data and is absent from the pooled estimate; if its omission is related to its result, the pooled estimate is biased by an unknown amount.
6. **Clinical and measurement heterogeneity embedded in the PICO.** Pooling across no-treatment, sham, waiting-list, education and co-intervention-alone comparators, across exercise-alone and exercise-plus-adjunct interventions, and across four or more different function instruments, is a deliberate design choice that maximises coverage at the cost of interpretability.
7. **Effect-measure scale mismatch with source reports.** The pooled statistic is an SMD applied at user request, while at least one source report presents mean differences; the pooled value is in standard-deviation units and cannot be read as questionnaire points.
8. **One document of unverified type retained.** The automated document-type gate could not confidently classify one retained record; its primary-trial status rests on curator judgement rather than verification.
9. **No formal risk-of-bias meta-analysis or GRADE rating** is presented for the pooled estimate; blinding of participants to exercise is impossible, so patient-reported function in all included trials is at inherent risk of performance and detection bias in the direction favouring exercise.
10. **Short-term window only.** Findings say nothing about medium- or long-term function, where exercise effects may plausibly be larger.

Collectively, these limitations mean the certainty of evidence for this outcome should be regarded as **low**, downgraded principally for inconsistency (considerable heterogeneity), imprecision (CI crossing the null, small total N) and risk of bias (unblindable intervention with patient-reported outcome).

## 13. Conclusion

Across five controlled trials comprising 379 participants with patellofemoral pain syndrome, exercise therapy — alone or added to a co-intervention — produced a pooled **Standardized Mean Difference of 0.399 (95% CI −0.210 to 1.008; p = 0.199)** for self-reported knee function at short-term follow-up of up to three months, a small-to-moderate point estimate **favouring exercise therapy** but not statistically distinguishable from no effect. Heterogeneity was considerable and unexplained (I² = 87.77%; τ² = 0.4175; Q = 26.032, p < 0.001), with individual trial results ranging from a significant benefit of the control condition (g = −0.520) to a large benefit of exercise (g = 1.267). Publication bias diagnostics were reassuring (Egger's p = 0.207; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted g = 0.399) but are underpowered at this k and should be read as "no signal detected" rather than "bias excluded."

The practical conclusion is measured: **this synthesis is directionally consistent with, but does not independently establish, a short-term functional benefit of exercise therapy in PFPS.** Given exercise therapy's favourable safety and cost profile and its support from broader evidence on pain and longer-term outcomes, these results do not argue against its use as first-line care; they argue against overstating the certainty or size of its short-term effect on patient-reported function.

**Priorities for future research:** (i) adequately powered trials (target N ≫ 200) rather than further small studies; (ii) convergence on the Anterior Knee Pain Scale (Kujala) as the primary function instrument with a standardized short-term timepoint (e.g. 12 weeks) to enable native-scale mean-difference pooling in questionnaire points; (iii) explicit specification and reporting of comparator type, since no-treatment, sham and co-intervention-alone controls are not interchangeable; (iv) full reporting of means, SDs and group sizes at every timepoint so that no eligible trial is lost to pooling; and (v) individual-participant-data synthesis to identify which patients and which exercise prescriptions drive the large positive effects seen in some trials.
