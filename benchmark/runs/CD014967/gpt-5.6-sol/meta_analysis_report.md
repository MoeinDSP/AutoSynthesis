# Phenobarbital versus levetiracetam as first-line treatment for neonatal seizures and all-cause mortality before initial hospital discharge: a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis

## Abstract

## Abstract

**Background:** Phenobarbital (phenobarbitone) and levetiracetam are used as first-line anti-seizure medications in neonates, but their comparative effect on mortality during the initial hospital admission is uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of assignment to phenobarbital, compared with levetiracetam, as first-line treatment for neonatal seizures on all-cause mortality from randomisation through discharge from the initial hospital or neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) stay.

**Methods:** Eligible participants were term or preterm neonates up to 28 days of age with clinically or electrographically diagnosed seizures. The prespecified outcome was death from any cause between randomisation and initial hospital/NICU discharge. Infants were analysed according to original treatment assignment, including in trials permitting crossover, rescue switching, or add-on therapy. Six studies were represented in the supplied evidence base; four studies, comprising 340 infants, contributed to the mortality meta-analysis. Risk ratios (RRs) were pooled with a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model.

**Results:** Across four studies, the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.7785633512054775 (95% CI 0.337 to 1.797; p=0.5574718402520402). The supplied result classified the effect as favouring neither treatment. Heterogeneity was low to moderate (I²=23.25%; τ²=0.182852; Cochran’s Q=2.928, p=0.4029). Egger’s intercept was −1.1324 (p=0.7191), providing no statistically significant evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry, although this test was severely underpowered with only four studies. Trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study; the reported adjusted standardized estimate was −0.2421, which was not on the RR scale and therefore cannot be interpreted as an adjusted mortality RR. Publication-bias risk was classified as moderate.

**Conclusions:** The available evidence does not establish a difference in all-cause mortality before initial hospital/NICU discharge between first-line phenobarbital and levetiracetam. The confidence interval is wide and remains compatible with substantial benefit or harm. Larger, rigorously reported randomised trials with explicit discharge-mortality data are required.

## Introduction

Neonatal seizures are neurological emergencies associated with acute brain injury, adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, and death. Phenobarbital (phenobarbitone) remains widely used as a first-line anti-seizure medication, while levetiracetam has increasingly been adopted because of its pharmacological profile and perceived tolerability. Comparative treatment decisions should consider not only seizure control but also clinically critical safety outcomes, including death during the initial admission.

This review addressed the following question: among term or preterm neonates up to 28 days of age with clinically or electrographically diagnosed seizures, what is the effect of phenobarbital, compared with levetiracetam, when assigned as first-line anti-seizure medication, on all-cause mortality from randomisation through discharge from the initial hospital or neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) stay?

The treatment contrast was assignment to phenobarbital versus assignment to levetiracetam. The outcome was the proportion of randomised or analysed infants who died from any cause before discharge. Deaths occurring only after discharge or reported at 3, 6, or 12 months were outside the defined outcome window.

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for this outcome if they:

1. enrolled term or preterm neonates no older than 28 days with clinically or electrographically diagnosed seizures;
2. assigned phenobarbital or levetiracetam as first-line anti-seizure medication;
3. included directly comparable phenobarbital and levetiracetam groups; and
4. explicitly reported all-cause mortality between randomisation and discharge from the initial hospital or NICU stay, including an explicit report of zero deaths.

For multi-arm trials, only the phenobarbital and levetiracetam groups were eligible. Original randomised assignment was to be preserved in trials permitting crossover, rescue switching, or add-on therapy after nonresponse. The number randomised was the preferred denominator; the number analysed was used only when the randomised denominator was unavailable. Studies not reporting mortality in the eligible period were excluded from this outcome rather than treated as having zero deaths.

### Information sources and selection process

The supplied dataset contained six study records and four effect-size records. Four studies contributed eligible discharge-mortality data to the quantitative synthesis, while two did not contribute to this outcome. The materials provided for this report did not specify database names, search dates, complete search strings, reviewer procedures, or record-level reasons for the two noncontributing studies. Thus, all four included studies contributed to the same outcome and time window: all-cause mortality from randomisation through initial hospital/NICU discharge.

## Effect Sizes

The effect measure was the Risk Ratio (RR), calculated conceptually as:

\[
RR=\frac{\text{risk of in-hospital death with phenobarbital}}{\text{risk of in-hospital death with levetiracetam}}.
\]

Under the prespecified direction, values greater than 1 indicate more deaths with phenobarbital, whereas values less than 1 indicate fewer deaths with phenobarbital. For interpretation of the synthesized result, however, the supplied meta-analysis categorisation was followed exactly: the result was classified as favouring **neither treatment** (“essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted”).

Study-specific mortality RRs and their confidence intervals were not included in the supplied results. Accordingly, exact individual-study RRs cannot be reported or independently recalculated. ## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled four studies and 340 infants. The pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** was **0.7785633512054775** (95% CI **0.337 to 1.797**; p=**0.5574718402520402**). The supplied result classified this estimate as favouring **neither phenobarbital nor levetiracetam**.

The confidence interval crossed the null value of 1 and was wide. Therefore, the analysis did not show a statistically significant difference in mortality before discharge. It also lacked precision: the interval remained compatible with materially fewer or materially more deaths under phenobarbital assignment relative to levetiracetam assignment. The absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as proof of equivalence or noninferiority.

## Heterogeneity

Observed statistical heterogeneity was low to moderate:

- **I²:** 23.25%
- **τ²:** 0.182852
- **Cochran’s Q:** 2.928
- **Q-test p value:** 0.4029

The Q test did not detect statistically significant heterogeneity. Nevertheless, with only four studies, both Q and I² are imprecise, and the absence of a significant Q test does not establish homogeneity. The estimated between-study variance was nonzero, supporting use of a random-effects model. A heterogeneity summary figure is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

Potential clinical sources of heterogeneity could include gestational age, seizure aetiology, clinical versus electrographic ascertainment, medication loading and escalation protocols, baseline illness severity, and rescue or crossover practices. The supplied study-level data were insufficient to evaluate these factors.

## Moderators

No moderator or subgroup results were supplied. Meta-regression was not supportable with only four contributing studies and without study-level covariate data. Consequently, there is no reliable evidence from this analysis that the mortality association differs according to prematurity, seizure-diagnosis method, seizure aetiology, dosing strategy, study size, or other design or clinical characteristics.

A bubble chart was generated at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, but the moderator represented, model coefficient, uncertainty, and p value were not provided. It should therefore be regarded as descriptive only and should not be used to infer effect modification.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−1.1324** with p=**0.7191**. This does not provide statistically significant evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry. However, an analysis containing only four studies has very low power to detect asymmetry, and Egger’s test is generally unreliable with such a small evidence base.

Trim-and-fill estimated **one** potentially missing study and reported an adjusted standardized estimate of **−0.2421**. This adjusted value was supplied on a standardized scale rather than the native RR scale, and no confidence interval was reported. It therefore cannot be interpreted as an adjusted mortality RR or directly compared numerically with the primary pooled RR of 0.7785633512054775.

Taking the nonsignificant Egger test, the trim-and-fill result, the very small number of studies, and possible selective nonreporting of mortality together, the supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **moderate**. This judgement is appropriately cautious rather than reassuring.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This meta-analysis synthesized four studies involving 340 neonates. First-line phenobarbital compared with first-line levetiracetam yielded a pooled Risk Ratio (RR) of 0.7785633512054775 (95% CI 0.337 to 1.797; p=0.5574718402520402) for all-cause mortality before discharge from the initial hospital or NICU stay. The supplied analysis classified the result as favouring neither treatment. Statistical heterogeneity was limited (I²=23.25%), but uncertainty around both the treatment effect and heterogeneity was substantial because the evidence base was small.

The result should be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence that the treatments have identical mortality effects. The confidence interval spans potentially important effects in both directions. Mortality in neonates with seizures is also strongly influenced by underlying neurological and systemic disease, gestational age, treatment limitations, and illness severity. Randomisation can balance these factors within adequately sized trials, but small trials may still yield unstable estimates.

### Clinical interpretation

On the available evidence, mortality before discharge does not provide a secure basis for preferring phenobarbital or levetiracetam as first-line therapy. Treatment choice should therefore also consider evidence on seizure cessation, electrographic seizure burden, adverse effects, cardiorespiratory stability, pharmacokinetics, neurodevelopment, and certainty of evidence for those outcomes. Those endpoints were not evaluated in this mortality-focused synthesis.

The analysis preserved the intended first-line treatment contrast conceptually. This is important because rescue switching or addition of a second medication after nonresponse can otherwise create post-randomisation bias. Nonetheless, the source materials did not provide sufficient trial-level detail to verify adherence to intention-to-treat denominators in every study.

### Implications for research

Future comparative trials should:

- report deaths by randomised arm through initial hospital/NICU discharge, including explicit zero-event reporting;
- distinguish in-hospital mortality from later mortality;
- retain all randomised infants in arm-specific denominators;
- document crossover, rescue therapy, and add-on treatment without reassigning outcomes by treatment received;
- stratify or adjust prospectively for major prognostic variables where appropriate;
- provide complete binary event data to permit independent RR calculation; and
- register protocols and prespecify mortality analyses to reduce selective outcome reporting.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

No formal `validation_flags` field was supplied. Nevertheless, several data-quality issues are evident from the provided results:

1. Six study records were present, but only four contributed effect sizes for this outcome; record-level reasons for the two noncontributing studies were not provided.
2. The primary meta-analysis was explicitly identified as native pooling of Risk Ratios, but the accompanying study-level records were labelled “Hedges’ g.” Those standardized records were not treated as mortality RRs.
3. The trim-and-fill adjusted value was also reported on a standardized scale rather than the RR scale and therefore could not be used as an adjusted native-scale mortality estimate.
4. Arm-level deaths and denominators were unavailable, preventing independent verification of zero-event handling, denominator selection, individual-study RRs, and adherence to original randomised assignment.
5. Bibliographic details and risk-of-bias judgements were not provided, limiting verification of study identity, duplicate reporting, and methodological quality.

## Limitations

This analysis has several important limitations.

First, only four studies and 340 infants contributed to the outcome, producing a wide confidence interval and limiting the ability to detect either a treatment effect or heterogeneity. Second, individual arm-level death counts and denominators were unavailable, so the extracted effects and eligibility of the mortality time point could not be independently audited. Third, two of the six represented studies did not contribute to the mortality analysis, but the available data did not clarify whether mortality was unreported, reported outside the eligible period, or otherwise unusable; outcome-reporting bias is therefore possible.

Fourth, no study-level risk-of-bias information was supplied. The certainty of the evidence could not be formally graded because domains needed for a GRADE assessment—risk of bias, detailed indirectness, event-level imprecision, inconsistency, and publication bias—were incompletely documented. Fifth, the source output contained a scale inconsistency between the native RR meta-analysis and ancillary standardized effect records. Sixth, publication-bias analyses are not dependable with four studies, and trim-and-fill relies on assumptions that may not hold when heterogeneity or selective outcome reporting is present.

Finally, the report could not reproduce a full search strategy or PRISMA flow because databases, dates, search terms, duplicate-removal counts, screening counts, and exclusion reasons were not provided. These reporting omissions reduce reproducibility.

## Conclusion

Among neonates with clinically or electrographically diagnosed seizures, the available comparison of first-line phenobarbital with first-line levetiracetam did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in all-cause mortality before discharge from the initial hospital or NICU stay. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.7785633512054775 (95% CI 0.337 to 1.797; p=0.5574718402520402), and the supplied analysis classified the result as favouring neither treatment. Heterogeneity was low to moderate, but the estimate was imprecise and publication-bias risk was considered moderate. The evidence is insufficient to conclude equivalence, benefit, or harm; larger and more transparently reported randomised trials are needed.
