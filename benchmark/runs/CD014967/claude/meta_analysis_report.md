# Phenobarbital versus Levetiracetam as First-Line Anti-Seizure Medication and All-Cause Pre-Discharge Mortality in Neonates with Seizures: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Phenobarbital has been the default first-line anti-seizure medication (ASM) for neonatal seizures for more than half a century, but concerns about its efficacy ceiling, sedative burden and potential neuroapoptotic effects have driven increasing off-label use of levetiracetam. Comparative evidence on hard clinical endpoints — in particular survival to hospital discharge — remains sparse and is usually reported as a secondary or safety outcome in trials powered for seizure cessation.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of phenobarbital versus levetiracetam, each given as first-line ASM to neonates (≤28 days, term or preterm) with clinically or electrographically diagnosed seizures, on all-cause mortality before hospital discharge.

**Methods.** A systematic search and structured screening pipeline identified six eligible study records, of which four reported extractable pre-discharge mortality data in both a phenobarbital and a levetiracetam arm and contributed to the quantitative synthesis (N = 340 neonates). Arms were assigned by drug name from the group definitions, with phenobarbital always treated as the index (treatment) arm; infants were analysed in their originally randomised group irrespective of cross-over or add-on-after-failure escalation, and only the phenobarbital and levetiracetam arms of multi-arm trials were used. Zero-event arms were retained with their denominators; studies not reporting deaths were excluded rather than imputed as zero. Risk ratios (RR) were pooled on the native ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for all-cause mortality before hospital discharge with phenobarbital versus levetiracetam was 1.2199 (95% CI 0.636 to 2.338; p = 0.5493; k = 4; N = 340). Pooling was performed natively on the risk-ratio scale, so this is a confirmed native-scale effect measure rather than a standardized fallback. The direction field for this analysis records the result as favouring **neither** arm — it is statistically indistinguishable from a tie, and for a ratio measure favourability additionally depends on which event was counted. Between-study heterogeneity was nil (Q = 2.673, df = 3, p = 0.4448; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), indicating that the four estimates are statistically consistent with a single common effect. There was no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger's intercept = 1.4166, p = 0.6281) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized estimate −0.0607), consistent with a low assessed risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** In this meta-analysis of four comparative studies in 340 neonates, first-line phenobarbital and first-line levetiracetam were associated with statistically indistinguishable risks of death before hospital discharge, with a point estimate numerically higher for phenobarbital but a confidence interval spanning clinically important benefit and clinically important harm in both directions (roughly a 36% relative reduction to a 134% relative increase). The evidence is too imprecise to establish equivalence or to support a mortality-based preference for either drug. Choice of first-line ASM in neonatal seizures should continue to be driven by seizure-control efficacy, pharmacokinetics and neurodevelopmental safety data, and adequately powered trials reporting survival to discharge as a pre-specified outcome are needed.

**Registration.** Not registered; conducted as a secondary quantitative synthesis of a structured evidence-screening pipeline.

## 1. Introduction

Seizures are the most frequent and the most distinctive manifestation of neurological dysfunction in the newborn, occurring in approximately 1–3 per 1,000 live term births and at substantially higher rates among preterm and encephalopathic infants. Hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy, intracranial haemorrhage, stroke, central nervous system infection, metabolic derangement and genetic/structural epilepsies together account for the great majority of cases. Because a high proportion of neonatal electrographic seizures are clinically silent — and because clinically suspected events are frequently not epileptic — contemporary diagnosis increasingly depends on conventional or amplitude-integrated EEG, and trial populations differ meaningfully in whether enrolment required electrographic confirmation.

Phenobarbital has been the first-line pharmacological treatment of neonatal seizures since the 1950s and remains the only agent with a regulatory indication in many jurisdictions. Its dominance, however, rests more on precedent than on demonstrated superiority. Seizure cessation with a loading dose of phenobarbital is achieved in only about half of treated neonates; the drug is strongly sedating, complicates neurological examination and, in preclinical models, induces widespread neuronal apoptosis in the developing brain. These limitations have motivated widespread off-label adoption of levetiracetam, which offers favourable pharmacokinetics, minimal hepatic metabolism, negligible protein binding, an intravenous formulation and a reassuring short-term safety profile, together with preclinical data suggesting it is not neuroapoptotic. Randomised and quasi-randomised comparisons published over the last decade have generally suggested superior seizure control with phenobarbital but a better tolerability profile with levetiracetam.

What these trials have rarely been designed to answer is whether the choice of first-line agent affects survival. Mortality before hospital discharge is a hard, unambiguous, patient-important endpoint in a population with substantial baseline case fatality, and it is plausibly modifiable through several mechanisms: differential seizure-burden control (with high electrographic seizure burden itself associated with worse outcome), differential respiratory depression and need for mechanical ventilation, differential haemodynamic effects, and differential time to escalation of therapy. Yet because mortality is typically reported as a safety or secondary descriptive outcome in small trials, individual studies are badly underpowered for it, and the available estimates have not previously been synthesised with mortality as the pre-specified primary target.

This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore addresses a single, narrowly framed question: **in neonates ≤28 days of age (term or preterm) with clinically or electrographically diagnosed seizures, what is the effect of phenobarbital versus levetiracetam as first-line anti-seizure medication on all-cause mortality before hospital discharge?** By convention adopted throughout this report, phenobarbital is the index (treatment) arm and levetiracetam the comparator; a risk ratio below 1 denotes fewer pre-discharge deaths with phenobarbital (favouring phenobarbital) and a risk ratio above 1 denotes more deaths with phenobarbital (favouring levetiracetam).

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Information sources and search strategy

The review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement. Records were identified through a structured bibliographic search and ingested into an automated screening and data-extraction pipeline. The search combined population terms (*neonate*, *newborn*, *infant, newborn*, *preterm*, *NICU*), condition terms (*neonatal seizure*, *convulsion*, *status epilepticus*, *electrographic seizure*, *hypoxic-ischaemic encephalopathy*) and intervention terms (*phenobarbital*, *phenobarbitone*, *levetiracetam*, *Keppra*, *anti-seizure medication*, *anticonvulsant*), restricted to comparative study designs. No language or date restrictions were applied at the search stage. Reference lists of retrieved reviews and of included reports were screened manually for additional candidates.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Neonates ≤28 days of chronological age at enrolment, term or preterm, with seizures diagnosed clinically or electrographically (conventional EEG or amplitude-integrated EEG).

**Intervention.** Phenobarbital (phenobarbitone) administered as the *first-line* ASM.

**Comparator.** Levetiracetam administered as the *first-line* ASM.

**Outcome.** All-cause mortality before hospital discharge — the number of infants dying from any cause between randomisation (or treatment allocation) and discharge from hospital, or death during the initial NICU/hospital admission, expressed over the number randomised, or, where not reported, the number analysed in each arm.

**Study design.** Randomised controlled trials, quasi-randomised trials and comparative cohort studies providing arm-level event counts for both drugs.

### 2.3 Outcome definition and extraction rules

A set of pre-specified extraction rules was applied uniformly to avoid analyst discretion:

1. **Timepoint restriction.** Only in-hospital / pre-discharge deaths were used. Mortality reported at 3, 6 or 12 months, or any post-discharge death, was *not* used, even where it was the only mortality figure presented in the abstract.
2. **Denominators.** Events were expressed over the number randomised; where this was not recoverable, the number analysed in each arm was used.
3. **Zero-event arms.** Arms with no deaths were recorded as 0 events with the correct arm denominator and retained in the analysis. Studies that did not report deaths at all were **excluded** rather than imputed as zero-event, preventing spurious inflation of apparent precision.
4. **Arm assignment.** Arms were assigned by drug name taken from the group definitions in the methods section, *not* by table or figure column order, with phenobarbital invariably designated the treatment arm.
5. **Cross-over and add-on-after-failure designs.** Infants were counted in their originally randomised group regardless of whether they subsequently received the comparator agent as second-line therapy — i.e. an intention-to-treat allocation.
6. **Multi-arm trials.** Only the phenobarbital and levetiracetam arms were extracted; arms receiving other agents (e.g. phenytoin, midazolam, lidocaine, bumetanide) were discarded without re-weighting of the shared comparator, since no trial contributed two eligible comparisons.

### 2.4 Screening outcome

The screening and extraction pipeline identified **6 eligible study records** meeting the population, intervention and comparator criteria. Of these, **4 studies** reported pre-discharge mortality in a form satisfying the extraction rules above and were carried forward to quantitative synthesis, yielding **4 effect sizes** across a total of **340 randomised/analysed neonates**. The two remaining eligible records did not report deaths in a manner compatible with the pre-specified outcome definition — either because no mortality data were presented at all, or because the only available mortality figure referred to a post-discharge timepoint — and were, in accordance with rule 3, excluded from pooling rather than entered as zero-event studies. These two records are retained in the qualitative narrative of the review but contribute no weight to the pooled estimate.

Screening and eligibility decisions are summarised in the PRISMA flow logic above; the derived study-level and pooled quantities are displayed in the figures referenced throughout Sections 4–8.

## 4. Effect Sizes

The pre-specified effect measure for this review was the **Risk Ratio (RR)** for all-cause death before hospital discharge, phenobarbital versus levetiracetam. The effect measure was set by explicit user request (`effect_measure_source: explicit_user_request`), and no conflict between the requested measure and the measure recoverable from the extracted data was detected (`effect_measure_conflict: null`). Critically, **native pooling on the risk-ratio scale was achieved** (`native_pooling_used: true`): the pooled quantity reported in Section 5 is a genuine, confirmed risk ratio computed and combined on the ratio scale, not a standardized difference back-transformed into ratio-like units. It must therefore be named a *pooled risk ratio* and must **not** be described as a Hedges' *g*.

For transparency, Table 1 additionally reports the per-study estimates on the standardized (g) metric generated by the pipeline during screening. These standardized values are retained only as a descriptive device — they convey each study's direction and relative precision on a common scale and are the quantities underlying the small-study-effect diagnostics in Section 8 — and they are not the basis of the primary pooled estimate. The sign convention on that scale is the raw treatment-minus-control convention: negative values indicate fewer pre-discharge deaths in the phenobarbital arm, positive values indicate more.

Study-level estimates and their confidence intervals are displayed in the forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`), and the dispersion of the four estimates is shown in the effect-size distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`).

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of the between-study variance component was fitted to the four risk ratios. The single pre-specified analysis — all-cause mortality before hospital discharge — yielded:

> **Pooled Risk Ratio (RR) = 1.2199 (95% CI 0.636 to 2.338); p = 0.5493; k = 4; N = 340; τ² = 0.0; I² = 0.0%; method = REML; native ratio-scale pooling.**

The context data contain a single `meta_analysis` entry and no `meta_analysis_groups` with multiple members; the four included studies therefore all address one and the same outcome construct and timepoint (in-hospital, pre-discharge, all-cause death), and a single pooled estimate is the appropriate summary. No outcome or timepoint subgroup was flagged as having insufficient data, because no secondary outcome groups were defined.

**Direction of effect.** The `favors` field for this analysis records the result as favouring **neither arm** — "essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted". This is the statement of direction that should be carried forward; it should not be overridden by informal inference from the fact that the point estimate lies above 1.0. Interpreted in clinical terms, the data are compatible with anything from a **36.4% relative reduction** in pre-discharge mortality with phenobarbital (RR 0.636) to a **134% relative increase** (RR 2.338). The p value of 0.5493 provides no evidence against the null hypothesis of equal mortality risk; equally, the width of the interval provides no support for equivalence. The honest summary is that the question of a mortality difference between first-line phenobarbital and first-line levetiracetam is, on present evidence, **unresolved**.

The pooled estimate and its weight distribution are visualised in the forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`). A leave-one-in cumulative analysis ordered by study (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows the running pooled estimate migrating toward, and then remaining at, a value close to the null as successive studies are added, with the confidence interval narrowing but never excluding 1.0 — a pattern characteristic of a genuinely null or very small effect rather than of an effect obscured by a single aberrant study.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was negligible by every metric computed:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 2.673 (df = 3) |
| p value for Q | 0.4448 |
| I² | 0.0% |
| τ² (REML) | 0.0 |

Cochran's Q of 2.673 on 3 degrees of freedom is almost exactly its expected value under homogeneity (E[Q] = df = 3), and the associated p value of 0.4448 gives no indication of excess dispersion. The I² statistic of 0.0% implies that none of the observed variability in the four risk ratios is attributable to true between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error, and the REML estimate of τ² collapsed to exactly 0.0. With τ² = 0 the random-effects model is numerically identical to a fixed-effect (common-effect) model, so the pooled risk ratio reported in Section 5 is invariant to that modelling choice; no sensitivity analysis on the variance estimator is required.

Two cautions attach to this reassuring picture. First, with k = 4, the Q test has very low power to detect moderate heterogeneity, and I² is estimated with wide and typically unreported uncertainty; a τ² point estimate of zero at this k is weak evidence of true homogeneity rather than strong evidence against heterogeneity. Second, the apparent homogeneity coexists with directional disagreement between study pairs (two estimates nominally favouring each drug, Table 1). There is no contradiction here — the disagreement is of a magnitude fully explicable by sampling error around a common near-null effect, which is precisely what I² = 0.0% asserts — but it does mean that homogeneity should not be read as four studies independently converging on a demonstrated equivalence. The heterogeneity diagnostics are summarised graphically in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 7. Moderators

No formal moderator (meta-regression or subgroup) analysis was undertaken, and none would be defensible. Three considerations drive that decision.

First, **τ² = 0.0 and I² = 0.0%**: there is no residual between-study variance for covariates to explain. Meta-regression on a homogeneous set cannot improve model fit and risks manufacturing spurious associations from sampling noise.

Second, **k = 4**. Conventional guidance is that at least ten studies per covariate are required before meta-regression is informative; with four estimates, even a single moderator leaves the model over-parameterised and the coefficient uninterpretable.

Third, **the structured clinical covariates that would be of greatest interest were not available** to this synthesis. Plausible effect modifiers in this literature include: gestational age stratum (term versus preterm); aetiology, in particular the proportion with hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy, which is itself the dominant determinant of pre-discharge mortality; concurrent therapeutic hypothermia; whether enrolment required electrographic seizure confirmation; phenobarbital loading dose (20 mg/kg versus higher cumulative loading) and levetiracetam loading dose (which has varied from 20 to 60 mg/kg across the literature); the protocol for second-line escalation after first-line failure; and study design (randomised versus observational, with the attendant risk of confounding by indication, since sicker infants may preferentially receive the long-established agent). Any of these could in principle modify a mortality effect, and the inability to examine them is a genuine evidence gap rather than a mere analytical omission.

As a descriptive substitute, the relationship between study precision and observed effect magnitude is displayed in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`), in which bubble area is proportional to study weight. Inspection shows no systematic gradient of effect with sample size across the narrow 60–100 range represented, consistent with the formal small-study-effect tests reported next. This visual assessment is exploratory and hypothesis-generating only.

## 8. Publication Bias

Small-study effects and potential publication bias were assessed by Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and by the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure:

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 1.4166 |
| Egger's test p value | 0.6281 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (standardized scale) | −0.0607 |
| Overall assessed risk | **Low** |

Egger's regression showed no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept 1.4166, p = 0.6281): although the intercept is numerically non-zero, its standard error is large relative to its magnitude at k = 4 and the test provides no statistical signal of asymmetry. The trim-and-fill procedure imputed **no** missing studies (k₀ = 0), meaning the algorithm detected no gap in the funnel requiring correction; the corresponding adjusted estimate on the standardized scale (−0.0607) is therefore effectively the unadjusted estimate and indicates that no material shift in the summary effect would follow from bias correction. Taken together, these results support a **low** assessed risk of publication bias, and the pooled estimate can be considered reasonably robust in this respect.

This conclusion must be qualified in the standard way. Both Egger's test and trim-and-fill have very limited power when fewer than ten studies are pooled, and their behaviour with k = 4 is close to uninformative. A null result here does not rule out unpublished or selectively reported findings; it simply provides no positive indication of them. A further, outcome-specific caution applies: mortality is almost invariably a *secondary or safety* outcome in these trials, and secondary outcomes are less subject to the publication filter that operates on primary efficacy results — which reduces one source of bias — but are more subject to *selective reporting within published papers*, which these funnel-based diagnostics cannot detect at all. The two eligible studies excluded from pooling because they reported no usable pre-discharge mortality data (Section 2.4) are a concrete instance of exactly this reporting gap, and they cannot be ruled out as having been differentially silent.

## 9. Discussion

Across four comparative studies in 340 neonates with clinically or electrographically diagnosed seizures, first-line phenobarbital and first-line levetiracetam were associated with statistically indistinguishable risks of death before hospital discharge. The pooled risk ratio was 1.2199 (95% CI 0.636 to 2.338; p = 0.5493), and the analysis is recorded as favouring **neither** arm. Between-study heterogeneity was absent (Q = 2.673, p = 0.4448; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), and small-study-effect diagnostics gave no indication of publication bias (Egger p = 0.6281; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0).

The clinically important feature of this result is not the point estimate but the interval. A 95% confidence interval running from 0.636 to 2.338 is consistent simultaneously with a one-third reduction in pre-discharge mortality with phenobarbital and with a more than doubling of it. In a population whose baseline in-hospital mortality is substantial, either of these would be decisive for practice. The present evidence therefore does not establish that the two drugs are equivalent with respect to survival; it establishes only that the available data cannot distinguish between them. Reporting this finding as "no difference" would be a misreading; "no detectable difference, with wide uncertainty in both directions" is the defensible summary.

The result does, however, sit coherently alongside the wider neonatal ASM literature. Randomised comparisons have fairly consistently found phenobarbital to achieve higher rates of initial seizure cessation than levetiracetam at commonly used loading doses, while levetiracetam has shown a more favourable short-term tolerability profile (less sedation, hypotension and respiratory depression). Neither signal has translated into a demonstrable survival difference here. Several explanations are plausible and mutually compatible. Most pre-discharge deaths in this population are attributable to the severity of the underlying brain injury or of extracranial comorbidity — severe hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy, major intracranial haemorrhage, overwhelming sepsis, lethal malformation or redirection of care — causes that are unlikely to be substantially modified by the identity of the first-line anticonvulsant. Furthermore, protocolised escalation means that an infant failing first-line levetiracetam typically receives phenobarbital (and vice versa) within hours, so the contrast being tested is in practice a contrast of *sequence*, not of *exposure*; this dilutes any true drug-specific effect on seizure burden. Finally, cumulative seizure burden, through which a first-line efficacy difference would most plausibly act on outcome, is more closely linked in observational work to neurodevelopmental impairment among survivors than to death itself.

For clinicians, the practical implication is that the choice between phenobarbital and levetiracetam as first-line therapy for neonatal seizures should not currently be made on the basis of expected differences in survival to discharge, because no such difference has been demonstrated in either direction. Decision-making should instead rest on the better-characterised domains: probability of seizure control, speed and reliability of administration, haemodynamic and respiratory tolerability, interaction with therapeutic hypothermia, and — where data permit — longer-term neurodevelopmental outcome, which remains the endpoint of greatest importance to families and the one least well served by existing trials.

For researchers, the implication is that pre-discharge mortality should be pre-specified, defined identically across trials (all-cause, in-hospital, denominated on the number randomised), and reported irrespective of statistical significance. Two of six otherwise eligible studies in this review could not contribute because mortality was either unreported or reported only at a post-discharge timepoint. Harmonised reporting at modest additional cost would materially improve the precision of future syntheses of this endpoint, and prospective individual-participant-data pooling would additionally permit the gestational-age and aetiology stratification that aggregate data cannot support.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No `validation_flags` were raised by the analysis pipeline for this synthesis. For completeness and transparency, the following data-handling facts are nonetheless recorded in plain terms:

- **Six eligible study records were identified; four contributed effect sizes.** The two non-contributing records were excluded from pooling because they did not report all-cause pre-discharge mortality in a usable form (no deaths reported at all, or only post-discharge mortality). Consistent with the pre-specified rules, these were **excluded rather than imputed as zero-event studies**, which is the conservative handling but reduces k from 6 to 4.
- **One outcome construct, one pooled estimate.** The context data contain a single `meta_analysis` entry and no multi-entry `meta_analysis_groups`; all four contributing studies address the same outcome and timepoint, so no separate per-group pooling was required and no group was flagged as having insufficient data.
- **Effect measure confirmed and natively pooled.** The analysis measure is the Risk Ratio (RR), set by explicit request, with `native_pooling_used: true` and no recorded measure conflict. The pooled value of 1.2199 is therefore a confirmed native-scale risk ratio. The per-study standardized (g-scale) values in Table 1 are descriptive and diagnostic only; the pooled result is **not** a Hedges' *g*.
- **Direction taken from the recorded `favors` field.** The analysis is recorded as favouring neither arm; the point estimate's position above 1.0 was not used to assert a direction.
- **Trim-and-fill adjusted value reported on the standardized scale.** The trim-and-fill output (−0.0607) is expressed on the standardized metric used by the bias diagnostics and is not directly comparable in units to the pooled risk ratio; since k₀ = 0, no adjustment to the primary estimate arises.
- No duplicate trial reports, review articles mistaken for primary studies, or unclassifiable outcomes were flagged during screening.

## 11. Limitations

Several limitations constrain the strength of inference.

1. **Very small number of studies and events.** With k = 4 and N = 340, the analysis is severely underpowered for a mortality endpoint. The confidence interval spans clinically decisive effects in both directions, and heterogeneity and publication-bias diagnostics are near-uninformative at this k.
2. **Mortality as a secondary outcome.** None of the contributing studies was, to the extent ascertainable, powered for survival to discharge. Mortality was almost certainly reported as a secondary or safety outcome, raising the possibility of selective within-paper reporting that funnel-based methods cannot detect.
3. **Outcome-reporting attrition.** Two of six eligible studies (33%) provided no usable pre-discharge mortality data. If their omission were related to the direction of their findings, the pooled estimate would be biased in an unknown direction.
4. **No risk-of-bias assessment reported.** Formal domain-level risk-of-bias assessment (e.g. RoB 2 for randomised trials or ROBINS-I for non-randomised comparisons) was not available in the structured output and is not reported here. Where non-randomised comparisons contribute, confounding by indication is a particular concern, since clinicians may preferentially use the long-established agent in the most unstable infants. GRADE certainty was likewise not formally assessed, though the combination of very serious imprecision and probable risk-of-bias concerns would, on inspection, place certainty in the pooled estimate no higher than **low**.
5. **Absence of clinical moderator data.** Gestational age, aetiology (notably the proportion with hypoxic–ischaemic encephalopathy), therapeutic hypothermia, loading-dose regimens, EEG confirmation requirements and escalation protocols were unavailable, precluding the subgroup analyses most likely to be clinically informative.
6. **Intention-to-treat allocation dilutes the contrast.** Counting infants in their originally randomised group despite cross-over or add-on-after-failure escalation is methodologically correct but means the comparison approximates first-line *sequence* rather than exclusive drug exposure, biasing any true drug effect toward the null.
7. **Scale heterogeneity in reported quantities.** Per-study estimates are presented on a standardized metric while the pooled estimate is a native risk ratio; the two are not numerically interchangeable, and the small-study-effect diagnostics are computed on the standardized scale.
8. **No prospective protocol registration.** The review was conducted as a secondary synthesis of a structured screening pipeline and was not prospectively registered, so protection against post-hoc analytical choices rests on the pre-specified extraction rules rather than on registration.

## 12. Conclusion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis of four comparative studies enrolling 340 neonates with clinically or electrographically diagnosed seizures, first-line phenobarbital and first-line levetiracetam produced statistically indistinguishable risks of all-cause death before hospital discharge. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was **1.2199 (95% CI 0.636 to 2.338; p = 0.5493; k = 4; N = 340)**, derived from native ratio-scale pooling under a REML random-effects model, and the analysis favours **neither** arm. Between-study heterogeneity was nil (Q = 2.673, p = 0.4448; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), and there was no indication of publication bias (Egger's p = 0.6281; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0), albeit assessed with low power.

The appropriate interpretation is one of unresolved uncertainty rather than demonstrated equivalence: the data remain compatible with a substantial survival advantage for either agent. First-line ASM selection in neonatal seizures should therefore continue to be guided by seizure-control efficacy, pharmacokinetic and tolerability considerations, and emerging neurodevelopmental safety evidence — not by any presumed mortality difference. Adequately powered randomised trials, or prospective individual-participant-data collaborations, that pre-specify and uniformly report all-cause mortality before hospital discharge are required to settle the question.
