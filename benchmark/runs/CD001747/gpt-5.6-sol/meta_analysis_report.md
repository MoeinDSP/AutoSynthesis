# Regular-release galantamine 16–24 mg/day versus placebo for 6-month cognitive change in Alzheimer’s disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Galantamine is used to treat cognitive symptoms of Alzheimer’s disease, but its effect depends on formulation, dose, and follow-up duration. This review evaluated regular-release galantamine 16–24 mg/day (8–12 mg twice daily) versus placebo for change from baseline in Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-cog) score at 6 months.

**Methods:** Only regular-release 16–24 mg/day arms and their corresponding placebo groups were eligible. Arms receiving 8 mg/day, 32 mg/day, or prolonged-release galantamine were excluded. Five studies were represented in the supplied evidence set, but only three eligible effect sizes were available for quantitative synthesis. A random-effects model estimated the pooled Mean Difference (MD) in ADAS-cog change using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The statistical convention in the supplied analysis was treatment minus placebo; therefore, a negative MD denotes a lower (more favorable) ADAS-cog change score with galantamine. Heterogeneity was evaluated with Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were explored with Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Three comparisons included 1,090 participants. The pooled Mean Difference was −3.318534153330484 ADAS-cog points (95% CI −4.023 to −2.614; p = 2.6294025011047218 × 10⁻²⁰), favoring regular-release galantamine 16–24 mg/day. Equivalently, galantamine conferred a benefit of approximately 3.319 points when the result is expressed in the prespecified improvement-positive direction. Statistical heterogeneity was absent (Q = 0.984, p = 0.6115; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). Egger’s intercept was −0.1442 (p = 0.4521), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0). These publication-bias analyses are highly underpowered with only three studies.

**Conclusions:** At 6 months, regular-release galantamine 16–24 mg/day improved ADAS-cog change relative to placebo by a pooled mean of approximately 3.32 points. The estimate was statistically precise and showed no observed between-study heterogeneity, but confidence in robustness is limited by the small number of pooled comparisons and the absence of study-level risk-of-bias and screening details in the supplied results.

## Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease is characterized by progressive cognitive decline. The Alzheimer’s Disease Assessment Scale–Cognitive Subscale (ADAS-cog) is commonly used to quantify cognitive change in clinical trials, with lower scores indicating better cognitive performance. Because efficacy may vary by formulation and dose, this review addressed a narrowly specified question: among people with Alzheimer’s disease, what is the effect at 6 months of **regular-release galantamine 16–24 mg/day (8–12 mg twice daily)**, compared with placebo, on change from baseline in ADAS-cog?

The eligible intervention was restricted to regular-release galantamine within the 16–24 mg/day range. Data from 8 mg/day, 32 mg/day, and prolonged-release 16–24 mg/day arms were not eligible. In multi-arm trials, only the eligible 16–24 mg/day arm and the shared placebo group were to be compared; other active-dose arms were not to be included.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood included **k = 3** comparisons and **n = 1,090** participants. The pooled **Mean Difference (MD)** was:

> **MD = −3.318534153330484 ADAS-cog points (95% CI −4.023 to −2.614; p = 2.6294025011047218 × 10⁻²⁰).**

This result favors **regular-release galantamine 16–24 mg/day (8–12 mg twice daily)**. In benefit-oriented terms, it corresponds to approximately **3.318534 ADAS-cog points less deterioration or greater improvement** at 6 months relative to placebo, with the transformed confidence limits indicating a benefit of 2.614 to 4.023 points.

The confidence interval excluded the null value by a substantial margin, providing strong statistical evidence of a treatment effect in the analyzed trials. The cumulative meta-analysis plot is available at:

## Heterogeneity

No statistical heterogeneity was detected:

- **Cochran’s Q = 0.984**
- **Q-test p = 0.6115**
- **I² = 0.0%**
- **τ² = 0.0**

These values indicate that the observed dispersion was compatible with sampling error under the fitted model. Nevertheless, I² = 0% does not establish that the studies were clinically or methodologically identical. With only three comparisons, Q has low power to detect heterogeneity, and τ² is estimated imprecisely. The absence of observed heterogeneity should therefore be interpreted as a property of this small dataset rather than proof that the treatment effect is invariant across patient populations or study designs.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−0.1442** with **p = 0.4521**. Thus, the test did not detect funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies, and the supplied trim-and-fill standardized estimate was **−0.5557**. The pipeline classified publication-bias risk as low.

The trim-and-fill value is on a standardized-effect scale and is not directly comparable with the principal native-scale pooled MD of −3.318534153330484 ADAS-cog points. More importantly, both Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill are unreliable with only three studies. The non-significant Egger test should therefore be interpreted as **no detected asymmetry**, not evidence that reporting bias is absent. Formal funnel-plot diagnostics are generally most informative when substantially more studies are available.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This meta-analysis found that regular-release galantamine 16–24 mg/day improved 6-month cognitive outcomes compared with placebo among people with Alzheimer’s disease. The pooled Mean Difference was −3.318534153330484 ADAS-cog points under the treatment-minus-placebo convention, equivalent to an approximately 3.32-point advantage for galantamine. The 95% confidence interval, −4.023 to −2.614, excluded no effect, and the associated p value was 2.6294025011047218 × 10⁻²⁰.

The three study-level standardized effects were similar in direction and magnitude: −0.516447, −0.526877, and −0.655489. This consistency accords with the heterogeneity estimates of I² = 0.0% and τ² = 0.0. The result is therefore statistically coherent across the included comparisons, although the small number of studies limits the sensitivity of heterogeneity tests.

### Clinical interpretation

The analysis addresses a specific regimen and should not be generalized automatically to lower-dose galantamine, 32 mg/day dosing, or prolonged-release formulations. It also pertains specifically to change from baseline on ADAS-cog at 6 months. Outcomes such as global clinical impression, functional ability, behavior, institutionalization, caregiver burden, adverse events, treatment discontinuation, and longer-term cognitive trajectories were outside the supplied analysis.

The pooled estimate reflects average between-group separation on the ADAS-cog. Whether an approximately 3.32-point mean difference constitutes a clinically important benefit for an individual patient cannot be established from these aggregate results alone. Clinical interpretation would also require information about baseline severity, responder distributions, adverse effects, adherence, attrition, and patient- or caregiver-valued outcomes.

### Strength and certainty of the evidence

Strengths include the narrowly defined intervention, explicit exclusion of ineligible dose and formulation arms, use of corresponding placebo groups, native-scale pooling, and consistency across the three comparisons. Restricting multi-dose trials to the eligible arm reduces contamination by doses outside the review question.

Certainty cannot be graded comprehensively from the supplied information. Risk-of-bias assessments, allocation and masking methods, outcome-data completeness, selective-reporting evaluations, and protocol concordance were not available. Statistical precision and consistency support confidence in the existence of an effect within the analyzed evidence set, but the small number of studies and missing methodological detail constrain conclusions about the overall certainty and generalizability of the evidence.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

No explicit `validation_flags` were included in the supplied context. Nonetheless, the following data constraints are evident and should be kept in view:

1. The pipeline represented five studies but supplied only three eligible effect sizes; reasons for the two non-contributing studies were not reported.
2. The main synthesis used the native-scale Mean Difference, whereas the listed study-level effects and trim-and-fill estimate were standardized. These statistics should not be numerically compared as if they shared a common unit.
3. Instrument-range information was available for only two of three rows. Accordingly, no 0–100 transformation was applied, and the MD was pooled in the papers’ raw units.
4. Bibliographic study identities were replaced by dataset labels, preventing verification of trial characteristics and complete trial-level citation.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations:

- Only three comparisons were available for pooling, limiting inference about heterogeneity, small-study effects, and effect modifiers.
- Full search methods and record-flow counts were unavailable, so search completeness and the detailed screening process could not be independently evaluated.
- Reasons that two of the five represented studies did not yield pooled effects were not supplied.
- Trial-level risk-of-bias information was unavailable, precluding domain-based appraisal and sensitivity analysis restricted to low-risk studies.
- Participant and design characteristics were not provided, limiting assessment of clinical applicability and cross-study similarity.
- The study-level effects were supplied on a standardized scale, whereas the principal synthesis used native ADAS-cog MDs; the underlying arm means, standard deviations, and group-specific sample sizes were not supplied for independent recalculation.
- Instrument-range metadata were incomplete, although the analysis appropriately avoided unsupported rescaling.
- Publication-bias procedures were severely underpowered at k = 3; their apparently reassuring results should not be overinterpreted.
- No adverse-event, discontinuation, global, behavioral, functional, quality-of-life, or long-term outcomes were included, so the benefit–harm balance cannot be determined.
- The analysis is limited to the 6-month endpoint and should not be extrapolated to other follow-up durations.

## Conclusion

Among people with Alzheimer’s disease, regular-release galantamine 16–24 mg/day (8–12 mg twice daily) produced better 6-month ADAS-cog change than placebo. The REML random-effects pooled **Mean Difference was −3.318534153330484 points (95% CI −4.023 to −2.614; p = 2.6294025011047218 × 10⁻²⁰)** under the treatment-minus-placebo convention, favoring galantamine and corresponding to an approximately 3.32-point benefit. No statistical heterogeneity was observed (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0; Q = 0.984, p = 0.6115). Although no publication-bias signal was detected, the evidence base of three comparisons is too small for reliable bias diagnostics. The findings support a cognitive benefit at 6 months for the specified regular-release dose, but interpretation should account for missing risk-of-bias information, incomplete study-level reporting, and the absence of safety and longer-term outcomes.
