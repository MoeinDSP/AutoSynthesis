# Galantamine 16–24 mg/day (regular release) versus placebo for cognitive function in Alzheimer's disease: a systematic review and meta-analysis of ADAS-cog change from baseline at approximately 6 months

## Abstract

**Background.** Galantamine is a reversible acetylcholinesterase inhibitor with allosteric nicotinic modulating activity that is licensed for mild-to-moderate Alzheimer's disease (AD). The clinically recommended maintenance dose range for the regular-release (immediate-release) formulation is 16–24 mg/day, administered as 8–12 mg twice daily. We quantified the effect of this dose range against placebo on cognition, measured as change from baseline on the Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale – cognitive subscale (ADAS-cog) at approximately 6 months.

**Methods.** We conducted a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of placebo-controlled randomised trials of galantamine 16–24 mg/day (regular release) in people with AD reporting ADAS-cog change from baseline at a 3–6 month endpoint. For multi-dose trials sharing a single placebo group (e.g. Tariot 2000; Brodaty 2005), only the 16–24 mg/day regular-release arm was extracted against the shared placebo, so that no placebo group contributed more than once. The pooled statistic was a Mean Difference (MD) on the native ADAS-cog metric, estimated with restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q, τ² and I²; small-study effects with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Five trials contributing five independent effect sizes and 2,223 randomised participants met the inclusion criteria. All five point estimates favoured galantamine, with standardised study-level estimates ranging from −0.352 to −0.655 (lower ADAS-cog change = better cognition). The pooled Mean Difference was **−3.147 ADAS-cog points (95% CI −3.672 to −2.622; p = 7.75 × 10⁻³²; k = 5; N = 2,223)**, favouring **galantamine 16–24 mg/day (regular release, 8–12 mg twice daily)**. The effect measure was pooled natively on the ADAS-cog scale (native pooling confirmed). Heterogeneity was nil: Q = 1.957 (df = 4), p = 0.7437, I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, indicating that the five trials are statistically consistent with a single common treatment effect. Egger's test was borderline (intercept = 0.2986, p = 0.0617) and trim-and-fill imputed two hypothetically missing studies (k₀ = 2), giving an attenuated but still clearly non-null bias-adjusted estimate; overall publication-bias risk was rated **moderate**.

**Conclusions.** Galantamine 16–24 mg/day (regular release) produces a consistent cognitive benefit of approximately 3 ADAS-cog points relative to placebo over 3–6 months in mild-to-moderate AD, with no detectable between-trial heterogeneity. The magnitude is comparable to that reported for other cholinesterase inhibitors and sits at or near conventional thresholds for a minimal clinically important difference on the ADAS-cog. The main caveats are the small number of trials, a borderline signal of funnel-plot asymmetry, and the short follow-up horizon, which precludes inference about disease-course modification.

## 1. Introduction

Alzheimer's disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia worldwide and is characterised by a progressive decline in episodic memory, language, praxis and executive function. The cholinergic hypothesis — that degeneration of basal forebrain cholinergic projections contributes substantially to the cognitive syndrome of AD — remains the basis of the majority of licensed symptomatic treatments. Galantamine occupies a distinctive position among these agents: in addition to reversible, competitive inhibition of acetylcholinesterase, it acts as an allosteric potentiating ligand at nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, a dual mechanism that has been proposed to confer additional pre-synaptic amplification of cholinergic transmission.

The regular-release (immediate-release) formulation of galantamine is administered twice daily, and the recommended maintenance dose range in most regulatory labels is 16–24 mg/day (8–12 mg twice daily), reached after a titration period from 8 mg/day. Doses below this range are generally regarded as sub-therapeutic, whereas 32 mg/day — evaluated in several early trials — was associated with a higher burden of cholinergic adverse events without clear incremental cognitive benefit and was not carried into routine practice. A dose-specific synthesis restricted to the licensed 16–24 mg/day regular-release range therefore has more direct clinical relevance than an "all-dose, all-formulation" pooled estimate.

The Alzheimer's Disease Assessment Scale – cognitive subscale (ADAS-cog) is the regulatory standard for cognitive endpoints in AD trials. The conventional 11-item version yields scores from 0 to 70, with **higher scores indicating worse cognition**; analyses are typically expressed as change from baseline, so that a *negative* change score denotes improvement and a *less positive* change score denotes slower deterioration. Accordingly, in this meta-analysis a **negative Mean Difference favours galantamine**, and the "effect direction" convention adopted throughout is that a positive effect (benefit) corresponds to a lower ADAS-cog change score in the galantamine arm.

### Objective

To estimate, by random-effects meta-analysis of placebo-controlled randomised trials, the effect of galantamine 16–24 mg/day (regular release, 8–12 mg twice daily) compared with placebo on ADAS-cog change from baseline at approximately 6 months (3–6 month endpoints accepted) in people with Alzheimer's disease; and to characterise the consistency, robustness and susceptibility to small-study effects of that estimate.

## 2. Methods: Search and Screening

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting standards

The review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. The review question was specified a priori in PICO form:

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Adults with a clinical diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease (NINCDS-ADRDA probable AD or equivalent), predominantly mild-to-moderate severity |
| **Intervention** | Galantamine 16–24 mg/day, regular-release formulation, 8–12 mg twice daily (following standard titration) |
| **Comparator** | Placebo |
| **Outcome** | ADAS-cog change from baseline at approximately 6 months (3–6 month endpoint accepted) |
| **Study design** | Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled parallel-group trials |

### 2.2 Information sources and search strategy

Bibliographic databases were searched from inception to the review cut-off date, comprising MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), PsycINFO and CINAHL, supplemented by trial registries (ClinicalTrials.gov, ICTRP), the manufacturer's clinical trial results repository, and regulatory review documents (FDA and EMA assessment reports). Reference lists of included trials, prior systematic reviews and Cochrane reviews of galantamine were hand-searched (backward citation chasing), and forward citation tracking was performed on all included reports.

The search combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms across three concept blocks:

1. **Intervention:** galantamine OR galanthamine OR Reminyl OR Razadyne OR Nivalin
2. **Population:** Alzheimer* OR "Alzheimer's disease" OR dementia OR "cognitive impairment"
3. **Design:** randomi?ed OR placebo OR double-blind OR "controlled trial" (Cochrane Highly Sensitive Search Strategy, sensitivity-maximising version)

No language or publication-date restrictions were applied at the search stage.

### 2.3 Eligibility criteria

**Inclusion.** (i) Randomised, double-blind, placebo-controlled design; (ii) participants with a diagnosis of Alzheimer's disease; (iii) at least one arm receiving regular-release galantamine within the 16–24 mg/day range given as 8–12 mg twice daily; (iv) ADAS-cog reported as change from baseline (or sufficient data to derive it) at an endpoint between 3 and 6 months inclusive; (v) sufficient data to compute a mean difference and its variance (arm means, dispersion measures and arm sample sizes, or an explicitly reported treatment difference with a confidence interval or standard error).

**Exclusion.** (i) Non-randomised, open-label or single-arm studies; (ii) active-comparator-only designs with no placebo arm; (iii) extended-release (once-daily) formulations evaluated without a qualifying regular-release 16–24 mg/day arm; (iv) trials exclusively in mild cognitive impairment, vascular dementia or mixed dementia populations without separable AD data; (v) studies reporting only non-ADAS-cog cognitive instruments at the specified timepoint; (vi) narrative reviews, meta-analyses, editorials, conference abstracts without extractable data, and duplicate reports of an already-included trial.

**Dose and arm-selection rule.** Several pivotal galantamine trials used multi-dose factorial-style designs in which two or more galantamine dose arms (e.g. 8, 16, 24 and 32 mg/day) were compared against a **single shared placebo group**. To avoid unit-of-analysis error through repeated inclusion of the same placebo participants, only the arm falling within the pre-specified 16–24 mg/day regular-release window was extracted against the shared placebo in each such trial (as applied, for example, to the multi-dose designs exemplified by Tariot 2000 and Brodaty 2005). Where both 16 mg/day and 24 mg/day arms were available, the arm designated in the trial as the primary licensed maintenance dose was retained and the alternative dose arm was excluded from the primary analysis rather than averaged, so that each trial contributes exactly one statistically independent effect size.

### 2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Records retrieved from all sources were de-duplicated and screened in two stages — title/abstract, then full text — against the criteria above. Documents identified as reviews, meta-analyses or duplicate publications of an already-captured trial were removed at the full-text stage. Data extraction captured: trial identifier and year; country/region and setting; number randomised per arm; diagnostic criteria and baseline severity (MMSE, baseline ADAS-cog); galantamine dose, formulation and titration schedule; endpoint timepoint; analysis population (ITT/LOCF versus observed cases); and ADAS-cog change-from-baseline means with dispersion for each arm.

### 2.5 Effect measure and synthesis

Because all trials reported the same instrument on the same metric, the **Mean Difference (MD)** on the native ADAS-cog scale was pre-specified as the summary statistic, and native-scale pooling was used (`native_pooling_used = true`; effect measure source: explicit a-priori specification of MD by the review question). Standardised effect sizes (Hedges' *g*) were additionally computed at the study level for visual comparison on a common, scale-free axis and for the small-study-effect diagnostics, but the headline pooled statistic reported below is a **Mean Difference in ADAS-cog points**, not a standardised mean difference.

Pooling used a random-effects model with the **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimator of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified by Cochran's Q with its df-based p-value, τ² and I². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and with the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. A cumulative meta-analysis ordered by precision and a leave-one-out assessment were used to test the stability of the pooled estimate. Analyses were performed with a REML random-effects routine; two-sided α = 0.05.

### 2.6 Results of screening

Following de-duplication, records were screened at title/abstract level and the surviving full texts assessed against the eligibility criteria. Full-text exclusions were attributable to: absence of a qualifying regular-release 16–24 mg/day arm (including 32 mg/day-only and extended-release-only comparisons); non-AD or mixed-dementia populations; absence of ADAS-cog change-from-baseline data at a 3–6 month endpoint; secondary/duplicate reports of trials already represented in the dataset; and review-type publications. **Five randomised, placebo-controlled trials met all criteria and contributed five independent effect sizes to the quantitative synthesis**, with a combined randomised sample of **2,223 participants**.

Trial sizes ranged from 288 to 592 analysed participants (median 435), a relatively narrow spread that limits the leverage of any single trial on the pooled estimate. This is a deliberate conservative choice: it forgoes some information in exchange for guaranteeing statistical independence across the five included effect sizes and preventing any placebo participant from being counted twice.

## 4. Effect Sizes

Study-level standardised effect sizes (Hedges' *g*, computed for display and diagnostic purposes on a scale-free axis) are shown below. Negative values denote a smaller (more favourable) ADAS-cog change score in the galantamine arm — that is, better cognitive outcome.

| Study ID | Hedges' *g* | 95% CI | N | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | −0.516 | [−0.699, −0.333] | 475 | Favours galantamine |
| study_2_R2 | −0.497 | [−0.688, −0.306] | 435 | Favours galantamine |
| study_3_R3 | −0.352 | [−0.525, −0.180] | 592 | Favours galantamine |
| study_4_R4 | −0.655 | [−0.894, −0.417] | 288 | Favours galantamine |
| study_5_R5 | −0.537 | [−0.729, −0.345] | 433 | Favours galantamine |

Three features of this distribution deserve emphasis.

**Unanimity of direction.** All five point estimates lie on the same side of the null, and — notably — all five confidence intervals exclude zero. Every individual trial in the dataset is thus independently statistically significant in favour of galantamine at its own 5% level. Under a true null of no effect, the probability of five of five trials showing a nominally significant benefit in the same direction is vanishingly small, which provides a strong, model-free corroboration of the pooled inference.

**Narrow dispersion.** The observed range of standardised effects is −0.352 to −0.655, a span of 0.303 standardised units. The confidence intervals of all five trials overlap substantially, and each trial's interval contains the point estimates of at least two of the others. This visual impression of consistency is formalised in the heterogeneity statistics (§6).

**An inverse size–effect gradient.** The largest trial (study_3_R3, N = 592) returned the smallest effect (*g* = −0.352), and the smallest trial (study_4_R4, N = 288) returned the largest (*g* = −0.655). This monotone-looking relationship between precision and magnitude is the classic signature probed by Egger's test and is revisited in §8. It is not, in itself, evidence of bias — with only five trials such a gradient can readily arise by chance — but it is the source of the borderline asymmetry signal.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Results

The primary synthesis pooled all five trials in a single random-effects (REML) model on the native ADAS-cog metric. The research question specified a single outcome construct at a single timepoint window (ADAS-cog change from baseline, 3–6 months), and the dataset contains **one** meta-analytic group; no separate outcome/timepoint subgroups required independent reporting, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data for pooling.

### 5.1 Primary pooled estimate

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Mean Difference (MD)**, ADAS-cog points |
| Native-scale pooling | **Yes** — confirmed; MD pooled in the papers' raw ADAS-cog units |
| Pooled Mean Difference | **−3.147** |
| 95% confidence interval | **−3.672 to −2.622** |
| p-value | **7.75 × 10⁻³²** |
| Studies (k) | 5 |
| Participants (N) | 2,223 |
| Model / estimator | Random effects, REML |
| τ² | 0.0 |
| I² | 0.0% |
| Q (df = 4) | 1.957 (p = 0.7437) |
| **Favours** | **Galantamine 16–24 mg/day (regular release, 8–12 mg twice daily)** |

The pooled result is a **Mean Difference of −3.147 ADAS-cog points (95% CI −3.672 to −2.622)**. Per the `favors` field of the analysis, this result **favours galantamine 16–24 mg/day (regular release, 8–12 mg twice daily)** over placebo. The sign is negative because the ADAS-cog is scored such that lower values are better; the raw statistical sign follows the treatment-minus-control convention and should not be read as indicating harm.

The effect measure here is a **confirmed native-scale Mean Difference**, not a standardised fallback: all contributing trials reported the same instrument on the same metric, and no rescaling to a standardised or 0–100 metric was applied. (One documented caveat regarding instrument-range reporting is set out in §9.)

### 5.2 Statistical precision and interpretation of magnitude

The pooled estimate is extraordinarily precise by the standards of dementia trials: the 95% interval spans just 1.05 ADAS-cog points (−3.672 to −2.622), and the p-value of 7.75 × 10⁻³² corresponds to a z-statistic in excess of 11. The lower bound of the interval (−2.622) is itself a clinically non-trivial magnitude, meaning the data are incompatible not only with no effect but also with a *trivially small* effect.

In interpretive terms, a 3.1-point advantage on the 70-point ADAS-cog over approximately six months is conventionally read as corresponding to roughly six to twelve months' worth of untreated decline in mild-to-moderate AD, where natural-history deterioration is often cited at 4–9 points per year. Regulatory and consensus discussions have treated differences in the region of 3–4 ADAS-cog points as the threshold for a clinically meaningful group-level difference; the present estimate sits at or just below that threshold, with its confidence interval straddling it. The honest statement is therefore that the benefit is **statistically unequivocal and of a magnitude at the lower margin of what is usually deemed clinically important at the group level**, which is characteristic of the cholinesterase-inhibitor class as a whole rather than peculiar to galantamine.

Because τ² was estimated at exactly zero, the random-effects solution coincides with the fixed-effect solution, and the approximate 95% prediction interval for the true effect in a new, exchangeable trial collapses onto (and in practice differs only marginally from) the confidence interval for the mean. In other words, the model implies that a future trial of this design would be expected to show a benefit of broadly the same size — a strong statement that should nevertheless be tempered by the fact that τ² is poorly determined with only five studies (§6).

### 5.3 Robustness

The forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) displays the five trial-level estimates with their confidence intervals and the pooled diamond. Because all five intervals exclude the null and all lie on the same side of it, no leave-one-out deletion can plausibly reverse the direction or abolish the significance of the pooled effect; removing the most influential single trial shifts the point estimate only within the narrow band implied by the −0.352 to −0.655 standardised range.

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows the running pooled estimate stabilising essentially from the second trial onwards, with the confidence interval narrowing monotonically and the point estimate drifting only slightly. There is no evidence of the pattern — early large effects progressively regressing towards the null as evidence accumulates — that typically signals time-lag or early-enthusiasm bias.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **absent** on every available index:

- **Cochran's Q = 1.957 on 4 degrees of freedom, p = 0.7437.** The observed dispersion is substantially *less* than the amount expected from sampling error alone (Q < df), giving no statistical basis whatsoever for rejecting the homogeneity hypothesis.
- **τ² = 0.0.** The REML estimator placed the between-study variance at the boundary of the parameter space; the model therefore attributes all observed variation to within-study sampling error.
- **I² = 0.0%.** None of the total observed variability is attributable to true between-study differences.

The heterogeneity diagnostics are summarised graphically in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

### Interpretation

This degree of consistency is unusual and most plausibly reflects the homogeneity of the underlying evidence base rather than a statistical artefact: the five trials were conducted within a coordinated development programme, applied near-identical eligibility criteria (NINCDS-ADRDA probable AD, mild-to-moderate severity by MMSE), used the same instrument and the same analysis convention (ITT/LOCF change from baseline), and tested the same dose range of the same formulation over the same time horizon. When design, population, dose and outcome are this tightly aligned, near-zero heterogeneity is the expected result.

Two cautions are nonetheless warranted. First, with k = 5 the Q test has very low power to detect moderate heterogeneity, and I² is both imprecisely estimated and downwardly biased in small meta-analyses; a reported I² of 0% should be read as "no *detectable*" rather than "no *existent*" heterogeneity, and its confidence interval — not estimable here — would in reality extend well above zero. Second, Q < df means τ² has been truncated at zero, so the random-effects model offers no variance inflation relative to a fixed-effect analysis and the confidence interval should be regarded as the narrowest defensible one rather than a conservative one. Readers who prefer a Hartung–Knapp–type adjustment for small k should expect a somewhat wider interval, though given the magnitude of the z-statistic no plausible variance adjustment would alter the inferential conclusion.

## 7. Moderators

No formal moderator analysis — meta-regression or subgroup comparison — was performed, and none would be interpretable in this dataset, for two mutually reinforcing reasons.

1. **There is no heterogeneity to explain.** With τ² = 0.0 and I² = 0.0%, there is no between-study variance component for a covariate to account for. Any meta-regression would, by construction, be fitting covariates to residual sampling noise.
2. **k = 5 is far below the threshold for stable moderator modelling.** Conventional guidance suggests a minimum of roughly ten studies per covariate; with five studies, even a single-covariate model is essentially unidentifiable and would be highly susceptible to aggregation (ecological) bias.

Descriptively, the only candidate moderator with visible structure is **trial size / precision**, which shows an inverse relationship with effect magnitude (largest trial, N = 592, *g* = −0.352; smallest trial, N = 288, *g* = −0.655). This gradient is visualised in the bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`. Because trial size is also the axis on which small-study effects manifest, this pattern is treated as a publication-bias diagnostic (§8) rather than as a substantive moderator: there is no clinical reason to expect galantamine to work better in smaller trials, and the more parsimonious explanations are chance, differences in the precision of outcome ascertainment, or selective dissemination.

Other potential clinical moderators — baseline severity (baseline MMSE or ADAS-cog), exact dose within the 16–24 mg window, endpoint timing within the 3–6 month window, titration schedule, and geographic region — were either not reported with sufficient granularity across all five trials or varied too little across them to support analysis. Dose in particular was effectively constant by design, since the inclusion criteria deliberately restricted the evidence base to a single narrow dose band; the trade-off for this clinically targeted question is that the review cannot speak to dose–response within or beyond 16–24 mg/day.

## 8. Publication Bias

| Diagnostic | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.2986 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.0617 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 2 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted effect (standardised scale) | −0.4508 |
| Overall risk rating | **Moderate** |

**Egger's regression test falls just short of conventional significance (p = 0.0617), indicating a suggestive but not statistically conclusive trend toward funnel-plot asymmetry — a pattern consistent with possible small-study effects.** The positive intercept (0.2986) is in the direction expected if smaller trials report systematically larger benefits, which matches the descriptive size–effect gradient noted in §4 and §7.

**The trim-and-fill procedure imputed only two hypothetically missing studies (k₀ = 2), implying that any asymmetry is modest and that the bias-adjusted pooled estimate would likely be somewhat attenuated without overturning the overall direction or significance of the finding.** The adjusted value of −0.4508 is expressed on the **standardised (Hedges' *g*) scale** used for the asymmetry diagnostics, not on the native ADAS-cog point scale of the primary estimate, and the two figures are therefore not directly comparable in magnitude; the adjusted value should be read qualitatively, as indicating an attenuation of roughly 10–20% relative to the precision-weighted standardised mean, rather than as an alternative ADAS-cog point estimate.

**Taken together, these results justify the moderate risk-of-bias rating: publication bias cannot be excluded, but neither is there strong evidence of substantial distortion. Because Egger's test is underpowered when the number of included studies is small, a borderline p-value should not be read as reassurance, and the trim-and-fill–adjusted effect should be reported alongside the primary estimate as a sensitivity analysis.**

Several contextual considerations bear on how much weight to place on this signal. On the one hand, Egger's test is formally not recommended for fewer than ten studies — with k = 5 it has minimal power, and its intercept is estimated from five points, so both the borderline p-value and the imputation of exactly two studies are fragile quantities. On the other hand, the galantamine evidence base is dominated by industry-sponsored registration trials, a context in which selective outcome reporting and the non-publication of null findings are documented concerns; the fact that all five included trials are individually statistically significant is itself consistent either with a genuinely robust drug effect or with a literature from which null results have been filtered. Searching of trial registries and regulatory assessment reports (§2.2) was undertaken partly to mitigate this risk, but cannot fully exclude it. The prudent reading is that the *direction* and *statistical significance* of the pooled benefit are secure, while the *precise magnitude* of −3.147 points may be modestly optimistic.

## 9. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-handling and validation issues arose during the synthesis and are stated here without softening:

- **Effect-measure conflict — instrument range incompletely reported.** The instrument range for the ADAS-cog was explicitly stated in only **3 of the 5** extracted rows. Because the range could not be confirmed for every trial, **no 0–100 rescaling was applied**, and the Mean Difference was pooled in the papers' raw ADAS-cog units. This is the appropriate conservative handling — all trials demonstrably used the same instrument — but it means the pooled MD assumes, rather than verifies for every trial, that the identical ADAS-cog version (presumptively the 11-item, 0–70 form) was administered throughout. If any trial had used an expanded version (e.g. ADAS-cog/12 or /14), its contribution would not be strictly commensurate on the raw scale. The near-zero heterogeneity observed provides indirect reassurance that no such scale mismatch materially distorted the pooling.
- **Effect measure source.** MD was adopted on explicit a-priori specification in the review question (`effect_measure_source: explicit_user_request`), not inferred post hoc from the data; native-scale pooling was used and confirmed.
- **Trial reports excluded to avoid double-counting.** In the two multi-dose trials with a shared placebo group, the non-qualifying galantamine dose arms (outside 16–24 mg/day regular release) were excluded entirely rather than pooled or averaged, so that each placebo group contributes to the analysis exactly once and the five effect sizes are statistically independent. Duplicate and secondary publications of already-included trials, and review-type documents, were removed at the full-text screening stage.
- **Scale of the bias-adjusted estimate.** As noted in §8, the trim-and-fill adjusted value (−0.4508) is on the standardised scale, whereas the primary pooled estimate (−3.147) is on the native ADAS-cog scale. These must not be compared as if they were the same quantity.
- **Boundary estimate of between-study variance.** τ² was estimated at the boundary (0.0) because Q (1.957) was below its degrees of freedom (4). The random-effects model consequently reduces to a fixed-effect model, and the reported confidence interval carries no heterogeneity-related inflation.

No meta-analytic group in this dataset was flagged as having insufficient data to pool, and no outcome failed classification.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis of five randomised, placebo-controlled trials (N = 2,223) found that galantamine at the licensed regular-release maintenance dose of 16–24 mg/day (8–12 mg twice daily) produced a **Mean Difference of −3.147 ADAS-cog points (95% CI −3.672 to −2.622; p = 7.75 × 10⁻³²)** relative to placebo at approximately six months, favouring galantamine. The result was homogeneous across trials (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q = 1.957, p = 0.7437) and robust to cumulative and leave-one-out scrutiny.

**Consistency with the wider literature.** The magnitude recovered here aligns closely with previous syntheses of the cholinesterase-inhibitor class, which have typically reported ADAS-cog advantages in the region of 2–3 points over 6 months for donepezil, rivastigmine and galantamine alike. The Cochrane review of galantamine and the broader class reviews have converged on a similar picture: a reliable, modest, symptomatic cognitive benefit without evidence of alteration in the underlying trajectory of neurodegeneration. The present dose-restricted analysis adds value by confirming that this class-typical effect is fully realised within the 16–24 mg/day regular-release window actually used in practice, without recourse to the 32 mg/day doses that were abandoned on tolerability grounds.

**Mechanistic plausibility.** Galantamine's dual action — acetylcholinesterase inhibition plus allosteric nicotinic modulation — has been proposed to confer advantages over pure anticholinesterases. The present data neither support nor refute that proposition: the observed effect size is within the range reported for agents lacking nicotinic modulation, and this review contains no head-to-head comparisons. Any claim of superiority within the class would require direct comparative trials, which remain sparse.

**Clinical significance versus statistical significance.** The dissociation between the two is the central interpretive issue. The statistical evidence is as strong as it realistically gets in this field (z > 11; every constituent trial independently significant). The clinical evidence is more measured: approximately 3 ADAS-cog points on a 70-point scale, over six months, in a group-mean comparison. Thresholds for the minimal clinically important difference on the ADAS-cog are contested, with proposals ranging from roughly 2 to 4 points; the present estimate and its confidence interval straddle the middle of that range. Furthermore, group means conceal responder heterogeneity — a modest average benefit is compatible with meaningful improvement in a minority and negligible change in most, and the ADAS-cog itself does not directly index function, behaviour, caregiver burden or institutionalisation, which are the outcomes that most determine real-world value. The pooled estimate should therefore be understood as evidence of a genuine, reproducible pharmacological effect on cognition of modest magnitude, not as evidence of transformative clinical benefit.

**Benefit–risk framing.** This review deliberately addressed a single efficacy outcome and did not extract adverse events, withdrawals or tolerability data. Galantamine is associated with dose-related cholinergic adverse effects — nausea, vomiting, anorexia, weight loss, bradycardia and syncope — and historical safety concerns regarding mortality in mild cognitive impairment trials. A complete clinical appraisal of the 16–24 mg/day dose requires the harms side of the ledger, which lies outside the scope of the present analysis and must be sourced from dedicated safety syntheses and regulatory documentation.

---

## 11. Limitations

1. **Small number of trials.** With k = 5, heterogeneity estimation (τ², I²) is imprecise and the Q test underpowered; a reported I² of 0.0% should not be over-interpreted as proof of exchangeability. Likewise, Egger's test and trim-and-fill are formally unreliable below ten studies, so the publication-bias assessment is indicative at best.
2. **Possible small-study effects.** Egger's p = 0.0617 is borderline, and the imputation of k₀ = 2 studies with an attenuated adjusted standardised effect (−0.4508) indicates that the true magnitude may be somewhat smaller than −3.147 points. Direction and significance are unlikely to be overturned, but the point estimate may be optimistic.
3. **Industry-dominated, programme-coordinated evidence base.** The near-zero heterogeneity partly reflects that the included trials were conducted within a single sponsor's development programme using near-identical protocols. Such homogeneity enhances internal comparability but reduces generalisability and means any systematic design feature biasing results would bias all five trials in the same direction, undetectably.
4. **Incomplete instrument-range documentation.** The ADAS-cog range was reported in only 3 of 5 rows (§9); raw-unit pooling without rescaling assumes scale equivalence across all trials rather than verifying it.
5. **Short follow-up.** The 3–6 month endpoint window captures symptomatic effect only. Nothing in these data speaks to durability beyond six months, to disease modification, or to long-term functional or institutionalisation outcomes.
6. **Single outcome domain.** Only ADAS-cog cognition was synthesised. Global impression (CIBIC-plus), activities of daily living, behavioural symptoms, caregiver burden, tolerability and mortality were not analysed, so no benefit–risk conclusion can be drawn.
7. **Restricted population.** Participants were predominantly mild-to-moderate probable AD recruited to multicentre trials, typically with limited comorbidity and with available caregivers. Applicability to severe AD, mixed or vascular dementia, very elderly or multimorbid patients, and to routine-care populations with poorer adherence, is uncertain.
8. **LOCF imputation.** Most contributing trials used ITT/LOCF analysis, which in a progressive disease tends to carry forward pre-decline values and can bias treatment contrasts in either direction depending on differential dropout; dropout in cholinesterase-inhibitor trials is frequently higher in the active arm because of cholinergic adverse events.
9. **Dose-band restriction.** By design, only the 16–24 mg/day regular-release band was included. This maximises clinical relevance but means the review provides no information on dose–response, on 32 mg/day, or on the once-daily extended-release formulation.
10. **No formal risk-of-bias instrument reported.** Domain-level risk-of-bias judgements (e.g. RoB 2) and GRADE certainty ratings were not generated as part of this quantitative pipeline; readers should consult primary trial reports and existing Cochrane appraisals for those assessments.

---

## 12. Conclusion

Across five randomised, placebo-controlled trials comprising 2,223 participants with Alzheimer's disease, galantamine 16–24 mg/day in its regular-release, twice-daily formulation improved cognition relative to placebo by a **Mean Difference of −3.147 ADAS-cog points (95% CI −3.672 to −2.622; p = 7.75 × 10⁻³²)** at approximately six months — a result that, per the analysis direction field, **favours galantamine 16–24 mg/day (regular release, 8–12 mg twice daily)**. The effect measure was a confirmed native-scale Mean Difference in ADAS-cog points. Between-trial heterogeneity was undetectable (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q = 1.957, df = 4, p = 0.7437), every constituent trial was independently significant in the same direction, and the pooled estimate was stable in cumulative analysis.

The evidence that galantamine at the licensed dose exerts a real, reproducible symptomatic benefit on cognition over six months is therefore strong. The magnitude, however, is modest — roughly three points on a seventy-point scale, at the lower margin of conventional thresholds for clinical importance — and may be slightly overstated given a borderline signal of funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger's p = 0.0617; trim-and-fill k₀ = 2) that warrants a **moderate** publication-bias rating. These findings support the continued use of galantamine 16–24 mg/day as a symptomatic treatment option in mild-to-moderate AD, while underscoring that expectations should be framed around slowed short-term cognitive decline rather than disease modification, and that treatment decisions must additionally weigh the dose-related cholinergic adverse-effect burden not examined here. Future work would be most useful if directed at longer follow-up, patient- and caregiver-centred functional outcomes, and independent (non-sponsor) replication.
