# Prophylactic Anticoagulant or Antithrombotic Therapy and Adverse Events in Non-Hospitalised Adults With COVID-19: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Prophylactic anticoagulant and antithrombotic therapies have been evaluated in non-hospitalised adults with COVID-19, but their overall safety remains uncertain. This review assessed their effect on the participant-level risk of experiencing at least one adverse event during trial follow-up.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons evaluated prophylactic anticoagulant or antithrombotic treatment—including low-molecular-weight heparin, rivaroxaban, apixaban, aspirin, or similar therapy—against placebo, no anticoagulant, or standard care in adult outpatients or adults receiving post-discharge care. The intended outcome was the broadest reported count of participants with at least one adverse event; narrower serious-adverse-event or bleeding outcomes were eligible only when an overall adverse-event count was unavailable. Five effect sizes from six included studies were quantitatively synthesized using a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model and the Risk Ratio (RR). The synthesis included 2,163 participants.

**Results:** The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 1.8980272227444313 (95% CI 0.950–3.790; p = 0.0693772682631295; k = 5). The supplied favorability classification was “neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).” Between-study heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 73.41%; τ² = 0.408205), and Cochran’s Q was statistically significant (Q = 12.829, p = 0.0121). Egger’s intercept was 1.0698 (p = 0.6449), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0), supporting a low assessed risk of small-study or publication bias, although these analyses had limited power with only five effects.

**Conclusions:** The meta-analysis did not establish a statistically significant difference in adverse-event risk between prophylactic antithrombotic therapy and control because the confidence interval included the null RR of 1. The point estimate was imprecise and heterogeneity was considerable. Interpretation is constrained by the small evidence base, unavailable study-level event counts and adverse-event definitions, absence of study identifiers and risk-of-bias results, and the unexplained non-contribution of one included study to the quantitative synthesis.

## Introduction

COVID-19 is associated with inflammation, endothelial injury, platelet activation, and thrombotic complications. These mechanisms prompted trials of prophylactic anticoagulant and antithrombotic treatment not only during hospital admission but also in adult outpatients and after discharge. Any potential prevention of thromboembolic complications must be balanced against treatment-related harms, particularly bleeding and other adverse events.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among non-hospitalised adults with COVID-19, including outpatients and adults receiving post-discharge care, what is the effect of prophylactic anticoagulant or antithrombotic treatment, compared with placebo, no anticoagulant, or standard care, on the risk of experiencing at least one adverse event during trial follow-up?

The prespecified safety outcome was participant-level: the number of participants with at least one adverse event divided by the number randomised in each arm. The broadest overall adverse-event count was preferred. When that count was unavailable, participants with serious adverse events or bleeding events could be used, with preference for the broadest available category. Death, hospitalisation, venous thromboembolism, symptom resolution, quality of life, and other efficacy outcomes were not considered adverse events for this analysis.

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies enrolled non-hospitalised adults with COVID-19, including adults managed as outpatients and those receiving post-discharge care. Eligible interventions were prophylactic anticoagulant or antithrombotic regimens, such as low-molecular-weight heparin, rivaroxaban, apixaban, aspirin, or a similar thromboprophylactic treatment. Comparators were placebo, no anticoagulant, or standard care.

For multi-arm trials, the planned approach was to combine eligible anticoagulant dose groups by summing participant-event counts and denominators and to compare the resulting group with the eligible control group. Multiple reports of the same trial were to be linked and treated as one study. The number randomised was the preferred intention-to-treat denominator; a smaller safety population was allowable only when the randomised denominator was unavailable.

A study was eligible for this outcome only if it reported the number of participants experiencing an event. Reports presenting only numbers of event episodes were not eligible because recurrent episodes cannot be converted reliably into the number of affected participants. Distinct adverse-event categories were not to be summed unless the report explicitly established that they were non-overlapping.

### Search and selection reporting

The supplied results indicate that six studies met the review-level inclusion criteria and that five study effects entered the quantitative synthesis. No database names, search dates, complete search strategies, deduplication counts, title/abstract screening totals, full-text exclusion counts, or exclusion reasons were supplied. Consequently, a complete PRISMA flow diagram and reproducible electronic search strategy cannot be reported from the available material.

One of the six included studies did not contribute an effect size to this outcome. The reason was not provided; it therefore cannot be determined whether this resulted from missing participant-level safety data, reporting of event episodes only, duplication, or another analytical exclusion. The meta-analysis comprised five effects and 2,163 participants.

## Included Studies

The evidence base comprised six included studies involving non-hospitalised adults with COVID-19, with five studies contributing to the adverse-event meta-analysis. The quantitative dataset did not provide study names, citations, recruitment periods, countries, care settings by study, intervention drugs and doses, follow-up durations, arm-specific sample sizes, participant-event counts, or study-specific adverse-event definitions. It was therefore not possible to determine how many studies used overall adverse events versus serious adverse events or bleeding outcomes as the operational safety endpoint.

Similarly, no trial-level risk-of-bias judgments were supplied. The randomisation process, allocation concealment, blinding, completeness of adverse-event ascertainment, deviations from assigned intervention, selective reporting, and other potential biases could not be evaluated in this report. These omissions limit clinical interpretation even though a numerical synthesis was available.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. Native pooling was reported as having been used, and the effect measure was the **Risk Ratio (RR)**. Five studies with 2,163 participants contributed to the analysis.

The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was **1.8980272227444313** (95% CI **0.950–3.790**; p = **0.0693772682631295**; k = **5**). The confidence interval included the null value of 1, and the two-sided test was not statistically significant at the conventional 0.05 threshold. Thus, this analysis did not establish a difference in the risk of experiencing at least one adverse event during follow-up.

The supplied direction classification for the pooled result was **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)”**. Accordingly, no treatment or control advantage is assigned here beyond that provided classification. Clinically, the wide interval—from 0.950 to 3.790—indicates substantial uncertainty and does not exclude a materially elevated adverse-event risk.

The cumulative meta-analysis plot is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`. It may illustrate how the cumulative estimate changed as studies were added, but no study ordering or numerical cumulative results were supplied; therefore, no further temporal or sequential inference is made.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was considerable:

- **I² = 73.41%**
- **τ² = 0.408205**
- **Cochran’s Q = 12.829**
- **Q-test p = 0.0121**

The statistically significant Q test and I² of 73.41% indicate that the observed differences among study effects were unlikely to reflect sampling error alone. The estimated between-study variance was also substantial on the model’s computation scale. A heterogeneity summary figure is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

Plausible sources of heterogeneity include differences between outpatient and post-discharge populations; baseline thrombotic and bleeding risk; drug class, dose, and duration; placebo-controlled versus open-label standard-care comparisons; follow-up length; and use of overall adverse events, serious adverse events, or bleeding as the selected endpoint. These possibilities are clinically credible but could not be tested with the supplied aggregate dataset.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression yielded an intercept of **1.0698** with p = **0.6449**. This result did not provide evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. The trim-and-fill procedure estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies. The reported trim-and-fill estimate was **0.4238**, although its effect-measure label and scale were not supplied and it should not be interpreted as an adjusted RR. The pipeline classified publication-bias risk as **low**.

These findings are reassuring but should be interpreted cautiously. With only five effects, Egger’s regression has low power, funnel-plot patterns are difficult to distinguish from chance, and trim-and-fill can fail to detect missing studies, especially when genuine heterogeneity is considerable. Thus, the analyses show no detected asymmetry; they do not establish the absence of reporting bias.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across five studies and 2,163 participants, prophylactic anticoagulant or antithrombotic therapy was associated with a pooled RR of 1.8980272227444313 for the selected participant-level adverse-event outcome. The 95% CI was wide (0.950–3.790), crossed the null, and corresponded to p = 0.0693772682631295. The supplied favorability classification was neutral or indeterminate rather than favoring either arm. The result therefore does not establish a statistically significant safety difference, but its imprecision prevents reassurance that clinically important harm is absent.

The evidence was inconsistent across trials, with I² = 73.41%, τ² = 0.408205, and a significant Q test. Such inconsistency is unsurprising if trials differed in clinical setting, antithrombotic mechanism, dose, treatment duration, follow-up, and adverse-event definition. Pooling an “any adverse event” count in one trial with serious adverse events or bleeding in another can be necessary when reporting is sparse, but those outcomes differ in frequency and clinical meaning. The absence of outcome-definition data prevents assessment of this issue.

### Clinical interpretation

Safety decisions should not be based solely on whether the pooled p value crossed 0.05. The upper confidence limit of 3.790 is compatible with a large relative increase in the selected adverse-event outcome, while the lower limit of 0.950 is close to no effect. Absolute event risks, risk differences, and numbers needed to harm could not be calculated because arm-level event counts and control risks were unavailable.

Moreover, antithrombotic agents are not interchangeable. Anticoagulants and aspirin have different mechanisms and safety profiles, and low-dose and therapeutic-intensity regimens may carry different bleeding risks. Combining them addresses a broad class-level question but may obscure regimen-specific effects. Clinical use should therefore consider the individual agent, indication, baseline thrombotic and bleeding risks, and evidence for efficacy as well as safety.

### Implications for research

Future reports should provide, by randomised group, the number of participants experiencing at least one overall adverse event, at least one serious adverse event, major bleeding, clinically relevant non-major bleeding, and any bleeding. Reports should distinguish participants from event episodes, state whether categories overlap, use randomised denominators, and describe the safety population. Individual participant data or consistently defined aggregate outcomes would permit analyses by care setting, regimen, duration, baseline risk, and follow-up period.

A future update should retrieve full trial reports, link duplicate publications, document study-level outcome definitions, and conduct a domain-based risk-of-bias assessment. With sufficient studies, prespecified subgroup analyses or meta-regression could investigate the substantial heterogeneity observed here.

## Limitations

This review has several important limitations:

1. **Incomplete search documentation:** Databases, search dates, search strategies, screening counts, and full-text exclusion reasons were unavailable, preventing complete assessment of search comprehensiveness and full PRISMA flow reporting.
2. **One included study did not enter the synthesis:** Six studies were included, but only five effects were pooled. No reason for the missing effect was provided.
3. **Unavailable trial identities and characteristics:** Study citations, countries, settings, interventions, doses, follow-up periods, and comparator details were not supplied.
4. **Unavailable outcome definitions:** It was not possible to determine which studies contributed overall adverse events, serious adverse events, or bleeding outcomes. Clinical comparability of the pooled endpoint is therefore uncertain.
5. **Unavailable arm-level data:** Participant-event counts and arm-specific denominators were not supplied. Study-specific RRs, model weights, absolute risks, and the pooled calculation cannot be independently reconstructed.
6. **Scale mismatch in displayed study effects:** The supplied study-level estimates were presented on a standardized scale, whereas the pooled analysis was identified as native RR pooling. Although native pooling was explicitly reported, the displayed individual estimates cannot be used to audit the pooled RR.
7. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** Internal validity could not be judged, and sensitivity analyses excluding high-risk studies were not possible.
8. **Considerable heterogeneity:** I² was 73.41%, limiting the interpretability of a single pooled class-level effect.
9. **Limited publication-bias power:** Egger’s test and trim-and-fill were based on only five effects and cannot reliably exclude small-study or reporting bias.
10. **No moderator analyses:** Potentially important differences by setting, drug class, dose, follow-up, and endpoint definition could not be tested.

## Conclusion

In five studies including 2,163 non-hospitalised adults with COVID-19, the REML random-effects pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for experiencing at least one selected adverse event was 1.8980272227444313 (95% CI 0.950–3.790; p = 0.0693772682631295). The confidence interval included the null, and the supplied favorability classification was neither arm. Heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 73.41%), while statistical tests detected no publication-bias signal. Overall, the evidence does not establish a statistically significant safety difference, but it is too heterogeneous and imprecise to exclude clinically important harm. Better reported, outcome-standardized trial data are needed for a definitive safety assessment.
