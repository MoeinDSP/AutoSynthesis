# Cranberry products for the prevention of recurrent symptomatic, culture-verified urinary tract infection in women: a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of 8 randomised controlled trials (1,514 participants)

## Abstract

**Background.** Recurrent urinary tract infection (rUTI) affects a substantial minority of otherwise healthy women and drives repeated antibiotic exposure. Cranberry (*Vaccinium macrocarpon*) products have been promoted for prophylaxis for decades, but individual trials are small, use heterogeneous preparations and doses, and report discordant results.

**Objective.** To determine whether cranberry products (juice, capsules, tablets or powder, at any dose) compared with placebo, no treatment or a non-active control reduce the proportion of women with recurrent UTI who experience at least one symptomatic, culture-verified UTI during study follow-up.

**Methods.** A systematic search and multi-stage screening pipeline identified randomised controlled trials in women with recurrent UTI that reported participant-level occurrence of at least one symptomatic, culture-verified UTI over the whole follow-up period. Events and analysed participants were extracted per arm (intention-to-treat where reported); multi-arm trials with more than one cranberry arm were combined by summing events and denominators, and only placebo/no-treatment arms served as comparators. Asymptomatic bacteriuria, bacteriuria alone, symptom-only definitions, adherence, dropouts, adverse effects, urinary *E. coli* counts and antibiotic use were not pooled. Risk ratios (RR) were pooled on the log scale with a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ²; small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Eight trials contributing eight independent effect estimates and 1,514 analysed participants were pooled. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was **0.773 (95% CI 0.619 to 0.964; p = 0.0226; k = 8; N = 1,514)**, a native-scale result (native pooling confirmed, measure = RR). Under the review's prespecified convention an RR below 1 corresponds to fewer women experiencing at least one symptomatic, culture-verified UTI in the cranberry arm; however, the pipeline's automated direction field classified this result as favouring **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**, and this labelling discrepancy is reported transparently rather than resolved by inference. Heterogeneity was low to moderate and not statistically significant (Q = 9.819, df = 7, p = 0.1991; I² = 27.16%; τ² = 0.027058). Seven of eight study-level standardized estimates pointed in the same direction (Hedges' g from −0.577 to −0.076), with a single trial (g = +0.185, n = 319) favouring the control arm. Publication-bias diagnostics were reassuring: Egger's intercept −0.0141 (p = 0.4935) and trim-and-fill imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized estimate −0.2025, i.e. unchanged).

**Conclusions.** Across eight randomised trials, cranberry prophylaxis was associated with an approximately 23% relative reduction in the proportion of women with recurrent UTI experiencing at least one symptomatic, culture-verified infection (pooled RR 0.773, 95% CI 0.619–0.964), with low-to-moderate, non-significant heterogeneity and no detectable small-study effects. The confidence interval approaches the null (upper bound 0.964) and the approximate prediction interval crosses 1, so the effect is statistically significant but modest and not uniformly expected in every future setting. Product, dose and proanthocyanidin content remain uncharacterised moderators; adequately powered trials using standardised, biologically active preparations and participant-level, culture-verified endpoints are still required.


## 1. Introduction

Urinary tract infection (UTI) is among the most common bacterial infections in women, and a recurrent pattern — conventionally defined as ≥2 culture-verified episodes in six months or ≥3 in twelve months — is reported by a substantial minority of women with an index infection. Recurrence imposes considerable symptom burden, healthcare utilisation and, importantly, cumulative antibiotic exposure. Continuous low-dose antimicrobial prophylaxis reduces recurrence but selects for resistance, disturbs the urogenital and gastrointestinal microbiota, and is poorly tolerated by some women. Non-antibiotic prophylaxis is therefore of substantial clinical and public-health interest.

Cranberry (*Vaccinium macrocarpon*) products have been the most widely used non-antibiotic prophylaxis for decades. The mechanistic rationale rests principally on A-type proanthocyanidins (PACs), which interfere in vitro with P-fimbriae-mediated adhesion of uropathogenic *Escherichia coli* to uroepithelial cells; early acidification/hippuric-acid hypotheses are now regarded as implausible at achievable urinary concentrations. Translating this mechanism into clinical benefit has proved difficult. Trials differ in formulation (juice, concentrate, capsules, tablets, powder), in daily PAC dose (frequently unreported or unverified), in duration, in comparator (placebo juice, placebo capsule, no treatment, or other non-active control), and — most consequentially for evidence synthesis — in the endpoint definition, ranging from asymptomatic bacteriuria through symptom-only self-report to strictly defined symptomatic, culture-verified infection. Pooled estimates from earlier syntheses have oscillated between clear benefit and no benefit, largely as a function of which endpoints and populations were admitted.

This review addresses a deliberately narrow and clinically interpretable question. In **women with recurrent UTI**, do **cranberry products of any form and dose**, compared with **placebo, no treatment or a non-active control**, reduce the **proportion of participants experiencing at least one symptomatic, culture-verified UTI** during the whole study follow-up period? The unit of analysis is the participant, not the episode; the endpoint requires both symptoms and microbiological confirmation; and surrogate or partial endpoints are explicitly excluded from pooling.

## 2. Methods: Search and Screening

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met all of the following:

| Domain | Inclusion | Exclusion |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** | Women with recurrent urinary tract infection (history of repeated culture-verified episodes) | Mixed populations in which women with rUTI could not be isolated; catheterised or neurogenic bladder cohorts where recurrence is catheter-driven; paediatric-only samples |
| **Intervention** | Cranberry product in any form — juice, concentrate, capsules, tablets, powder — at any dose or duration | Multi-ingredient products in which cranberry could not be isolated as the randomised contrast |
| **Comparator** | Placebo, no treatment, or a non-active control | Active comparators (e.g. antibiotic prophylaxis, D-mannose, methenamine) as the *only* comparator; only placebo/no-treatment arms were used as control where both were present |
| **Outcome** | Number of participants with **≥1 symptomatic, culture-verified UTI** over the whole follow-up period, with the number analysed per arm | Asymptomatic bacteriuria; bacteriuria alone; symptom-only definitions without culture; adverse effects; adherence; dropouts; urinary *E. coli* counts; antibiotic consumption |
| **Design** | Randomised controlled trial (parallel or multi-arm) | Non-randomised, single-arm, crossover data without a usable first-period parallel contrast, narrative/systematic reviews, editorials, conference abstracts without extractable 2×2 data |

### 2.2 Information sources and search

A structured literature search of the biomedical databases was executed to retrieve randomised evidence on cranberry prophylaxis in recurrent UTI, supplemented by backward and forward citation chasing of eligible reports and of prior systematic reviews (reviews were used as reference-harvesting sources only and were never pooled as primary data).

### 2.3 Screening and data handling

Records proceeded through de-duplication, title/abstract screening against the PICO above, and full-text assessment. At full-text stage, each candidate was checked for (i) an rUTI population, (ii) a non-active comparator arm, and (iii) a participant-level, symptomatic **and** culture-verified UTI count with an analysable denominator. Where a trial reported the outcome at several follow-up times, the **total over the whole study period (longest on-treatment follow-up)** was used, so that each trial contributes one and only one estimate. Where a trial randomised several cranberry arms (e.g. two doses or two formulations) against a single control, **events and participants were summed across cranberry arms** to preserve independence and avoid unit-of-analysis error from double-counting the shared control group. Intention-to-treat denominators were used where reported; otherwise the analysed sample as reported by the paper was used, and this was recorded.

### 2.4 Yield

The screening pipeline yielded **8 included randomised controlled trials**, contributing **8 independent effect sizes** and **1,514 analysed participants**. The study flow is summarised below.

```
Records identified through database searching and citation chasing
                         |
        Duplicates removed; reviews/editorials set aside for reference harvesting only
                         |
        Title/abstract screening against PICO
                         |
        Full-text assessment for (a) rUTI population, (b) non-active comparator,
        (c) participant-level symptomatic + culture-verified UTI count
                         |
        ===> 8 RCTs included; 8 effect sizes; N = 1,514 analysed participants
```

### 2.5 Statistical analysis

The primary effect measure was the **Risk Ratio (RR)** for "≥1 symptomatic, culture-verified UTI", specified a priori at the user's explicit request (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`). Pooling was performed **on the native binary (log-RR) scale** — `native_pooling_used = true`, `computation_scale = RR` — so the headline estimate is a genuine, confirmed risk-ratio result and not a back-transformed standardized surrogate. Random-effects pooling used **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of the between-study variance τ², with Wald-type 95% confidence intervals on the log scale.

For graphical display, cross-check and bias diagnostics, each trial's 2×2 table was additionally expressed as a standardized mean difference (Hedges' g) with 95% CI; these standardized values are reported in §4 for transparency and are the scale on which Egger's test and trim-and-fill were executed. They are a secondary representation and are explicitly **not** the headline statistic.

Heterogeneity was quantified by Cochran's Q with its p-value, by I² (proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity) and by τ² (absolute between-study variance on the log-RR scale). Small-study effects were examined via Egger's regression of the standardized effect on its precision and via Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill.

Eight randomised controlled trials met all eligibility criteria. All eight trials randomised women with a documented history of recurrent UTI to a cranberry preparation versus placebo, no treatment or another non-active control, and all reported participant-level occurrence of at least one symptomatic, culture-verified UTI over the whole follow-up window. Interventions spanned the full range permitted by the protocol (juice/concentrate and solid-dose capsule, tablet and powder formulations); daily dose and, where stated, proanthocyanidin content varied widely between trials and were frequently not analytically verified in the source reports. Three trials (study_2_R2, study_3_R3, study_7_R7) produced individually statistically significant estimates, all in the direction of fewer women experiencing a UTI on cranberry. One trial (study_4_R4) was borderline (upper CI bound +0.032). Four trials (study_1_R1, study_5_R5, study_6_R6, study_8_R8) were individually inconclusive. No single trial produced a statistically significant result in the direction of harm.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Primary (native) scale

Each trial contributed one risk ratio for the proportion of participants with ≥1 symptomatic, culture-verified UTI, computed from arm-level events and analysed denominators, with multi-arm cranberry arms summed. All eight RRs were pooled on the log scale.

### 4.2 Secondary standardized scale

The study-level Hedges' g values are tabulated in §3 and displayed in the forest plot. Their spread is narrow: seven of eight lie between −0.58 and −0.08, i.e. in the small-effect range by conventional benchmarks, and only one (study_5_R5, g = +0.185) lies on the opposite side of the null. The largest absolute effect (study_7_R7, g = −0.577, 95% CI −0.879 to −0.275) comes from a mid-sized trial (n = 176), and the most precise individual estimate comes from the largest trial (study_3_R3, g = −0.220, 95% CI −0.424 to −0.017, n = 373). There is no monotone relationship between sample size and effect magnitude, which is consistent with the formal bias diagnostics reported in §7.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was defined (one outcome construct — ≥1 symptomatic, culture-verified UTI — at one timepoint convention — the whole-study/longest on-treatment total). No `meta_analysis_groups` with multiple entries were present, so a single pooled estimate is reported and no group was flagged as having insufficient data.

**Pooled result (random-effects, REML):**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Risk Ratio (RR)** (`measure_label`) |
| Native-scale pooling confirmed | **Yes** (`native_pooling_used = true`; computation scale = RR) |
| Pooled Risk Ratio | **0.773** |
| 95% confidence interval | **0.619 to 0.964** |
| p-value | **0.0226** |
| Studies (k) | **8** |
| Participants (N) | **1,514** |
| τ² (log scale) | **0.027058** |
| I² | **27.16%** |
| Q (df = 7) | **9.819**, p = **0.1991** |

The pooled **Risk Ratio of 0.773 (95% CI 0.619 to 0.964, p = 0.0226)** corresponds to a **22.7% relative reduction** in the proportion of women experiencing at least one symptomatic, culture-verified UTI during follow-up, with a confidence interval ranging from a 38.1% relative reduction at the optimistic bound to a 3.6% relative reduction at the pessimistic bound. The interval excludes 1 but only narrowly at its upper end, so the result is statistically significant at the conventional threshold while remaining compatible with an effect small enough to be clinically marginal.

### 5.1 Direction of effect — reported as specified

Per the review protocol's stated convention, *"a positive Hedges' g (equivalently a risk ratio below 1) means fewer women experienced a symptomatic, culture-verified UTI in the cranberry arm — i.e. the result favours cranberry."* The pooled RR of 0.773 is below 1.

However, the direction field attached to this meta-analytic entry records the favoured arm as:

> **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)."**

As instructed, the favoured arm is reported from that field rather than inferred from the sign of the pooled estimate. The honest reading is therefore twofold and should not be collapsed: (a) on the review's own prespecified interpretive convention, an RR of 0.773 with an interval excluding 1 points to fewer women with culture-verified symptomatic UTI under cranberry; but (b) the pipeline's automated direction classifier did **not** confirm a favoured arm, flagging instead that for a ratio measure the favourability of a value below 1 depends entirely on which event was tabulated as the numerator. Readers should rely on the explicit outcome definition in §2.1 — the counted event is an *unwanted* event (≥1 symptomatic, culture-verified UTI) — when interpreting the direction, and should treat the "neither" classification as an unresolved labelling flag rather than as evidence that the two arms performed identically.

### 5.2 Absolute interpretation

A relative risk reduction of roughly 23% translates into different absolute benefits depending on baseline recurrence risk. At a control-arm risk of 30–40% of women experiencing at least one culture-verified symptomatic UTI over the follow-up period — a range typical of rUTI prophylaxis trials — an RR of 0.773 implies an absolute risk reduction of approximately 7 to 9 percentage points, i.e. a number needed to treat of roughly 11 to 15 women for one additional woman to avoid at least one episode. At lower baseline risk the absolute benefit shrinks proportionally. These figures are illustrative extrapolations from the pooled ratio, not directly pooled risk differences, and should be treated as such.

### 5.3 Stability

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows the pooled estimate settling into its final range as the larger trials enter, without any single study driving the summary across the null. Given that the one discordant trial (study_5_R5, g = +0.185) is also one of the two largest, its removal would move the pooled RR further below 1 and away from the null; conversely, removal of the strongest individual trial (study_7_R7, g = −0.577) would move the pooled estimate toward 1 and would be expected to render it borderline. The significance of the pooled result is therefore not robust to the exclusion of the single most favourable trial — a fragility worth stating plainly.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **low to moderate and not statistically significant**:

- **Cochran's Q = 9.819** on 7 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.1991** — the observed dispersion is compatible with sampling error alone.
- **I² = 27.16%** — approximately a quarter of the total variability in observed effects is attributable to real between-study differences rather than within-study error. By the conventional Cochrane tiering this sits at the boundary of "might not be important" (0–40%).
- **τ² = 0.027058** on the log-RR scale, implying a between-study standard deviation of τ ≈ 0.165 in log-RR units. In ratio terms this means that the central 95% of true underlying study effects would be expected to span roughly a 1.4-fold multiplicative range around the pooled value.

Combining τ² with the uncertainty in the pooled mean yields an **approximate 95% prediction interval of about 0.47 to 1.26** for the true RR in a new, exchangeable trial (computed here using the standard t-based prediction-interval formula with k − 2 = 6 degrees of freedom; reported as an approximation and not as a pipeline-returned quantity). The prediction interval **includes 1**. This is the single most important nuance in the heterogeneity assessment: while the *average* effect across these eight trials is a statistically significant reduction, the evidence does not support an expectation that cranberry prophylaxis will reduce culture-verified symptomatic recurrence in every future population, formulation and dose regimen.

A visual summary of the heterogeneity statistics is provided in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`. Qualitatively, the heterogeneity is driven almost entirely by the contrast between study_7_R7 (strongly favouring cranberry) and study_5_R5 (nominally favouring control); the remaining six trials cluster tightly between g = −0.42 and −0.08 and are mutually highly consistent.

## 7. Moderators

No formal moderator analysis (meta-regression or subgroup analysis) was pre-specified or executed in this synthesis, and none is reported here as a quantitative result. With **k = 8** and **I² = 27.16%** this is the appropriate decision: conventional guidance requires roughly ten studies per covariate for meta-regression, and the total between-study variance available to be explained (τ² = 0.027058) is small, so any covariate analysis would be severely underpowered, prone to ecological confounding, and at high risk of producing spurious subgroup effects.

The following moderators are nonetheless of substantive interest and should be regarded as **uninvestigated, not as excluded**:

1. **Formulation** — juice/concentrate versus solid dose (capsule, tablet, powder). These differ in daily fluid volume delivered, in placebo credibility, and in adherence profile; juice trials inherently bundle a hydration co-intervention with the active constituents.
2. **Proanthocyanidin dose and analytical verification** — arguably the most plausible source of true heterogeneity. Several trials in this literature report product dose in milligrams of extract without verified A-type PAC content, and some commercially supplied preparations have been found on later assay to contain negligible active material. Trials delivering a biologically inert product would be expected to generate null estimates regardless of mechanism.
3. **Comparator type** — matched placebo versus no treatment or other non-active control. No-treatment comparators leave the outcome vulnerable to differential symptom reporting and differential culture-seeking behaviour, which generally inflates apparent benefit.
4. **Follow-up duration and adherence** — longer trials accumulate more events but also more dropout, and adherence to a twice-daily juice regimen over 12 months is typically poor.
5. **Baseline recurrence intensity** and **menopausal status**, which govern baseline risk and hence the absolute benefit achievable.

The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) displays effect size against study size/precision; it shows no systematic gradient, which argues against study size acting as a surrogate moderator but says nothing about the clinical moderators listed above.

## 8. Publication Bias

Two complementary diagnostics were applied to the standardized study-level estimates:

| Diagnostic | Result | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | **−0.0141** | Essentially zero asymmetry |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.4935** | Far above 0.05; no detectable funnel asymmetry |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **0** | Funnel already symmetric; nothing imputed |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (standardized) | **−0.2025** | Unchanged relative to the unadjusted standardized estimate |
| Overall risk rating | **Low** | — |

**Interpretation.** Egger's regression test showed no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.4935, well above the conventional 0.05 threshold), indicating no detectable relationship between effect size and study precision. The intercept of −0.0141 is negligible in magnitude. Consistent with this, the trim-and-fill procedure imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), meaning the funnel plot was already symmetric and the pooled estimate requires no adjustment for potentially suppressed results; the adjusted standardized value (−0.2025) is identical to the unadjusted one.

**Caveat.** Together these converge on a **low risk of publication bias**, though both tests have limited statistical power when the number of included studies is small (typically fewer than ten). With k = 8 this analysis is below that threshold, so a null result reflects an *absence of evidence* for bias rather than firm *evidence of absence*. Furthermore, neither test can detect bias arising from selective outcome reporting *within* trials — a particular concern in this literature, where UTI can be defined in at least four ways (culture-verified symptomatic, bacteriuria, symptom-only, antibiotic-treated episode) and where restriction to the strictest definition, as in this review, may itself have excluded arm-level data that exist but were never reported in extractable form. Industry sponsorship by cranberry-product manufacturers is also common in this field and is not captured by funnel-plot methods.

## 9. Discussion

Across eight randomised controlled trials and 1,514 analysed women with recurrent UTI, cranberry prophylaxis was associated with a pooled **Risk Ratio of 0.773 (95% CI 0.619 to 0.964, p = 0.0226)** for experiencing at least one symptomatic, culture-verified UTI during follow-up — an approximate 23% relative reduction in the proportion of women affected. Heterogeneity was low to moderate and non-significant (I² = 27.16%; Q = 9.819, df = 7, p = 0.1991; τ² = 0.027058), seven of eight study-level estimates pointed the same way, and small-study-effect diagnostics were unremarkable (Egger p = 0.4935; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0). Within the narrow outcome definition adopted here, the evidence is internally coherent.

Three features of this synthesis deserve emphasis.

**First, the endpoint is strict and the unit is the woman.** By requiring both symptoms and microbiological verification, and by counting *participants with ≥1 episode* rather than episodes, this review avoids two recurring distortions in the cranberry literature: inflation of apparent benefit by pooling asymptomatic bacteriuria (a condition that generally does not warrant treatment and whose reduction is of uncertain clinical value) and unit-of-analysis error from treating multiple episodes within a woman as independent. The price of this rigour is a smaller evidence base — eight trials rather than the dozen or more admitted by broader syntheses — and consequently wider intervals and weaker moderator resolution.

**Second, the effect is statistically significant but modest and fragile.** The upper confidence bound of 0.964 is only 3.6% below the null, and the approximate 95% prediction interval (≈0.47 to 1.26) crosses 1. Sensitivity considerations in §5.3 indicate that the significance of the pooled estimate depends materially on the single most favourable trial. The defensible summary is that cranberry products *probably* produce a small reduction in the proportion of women with recurrent UTI who experience a further culture-verified symptomatic infection, with an NNT plausibly in the low teens at typical baseline risk — not that they reliably prevent recurrence.

**Third, the direction label is formally unresolved.** The automated direction field returned "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)" rather than naming cranberry as the favoured arm. This is a labelling artefact of applying a generic classifier to a ratio measure of an undesirable event, but per reporting instructions it is stated as returned and not overridden. Substantively, the counted event is unambiguously undesirable (≥1 symptomatic, culture-verified UTI), and the review's own prespecified convention maps RR < 1 onto fewer UTIs in the cranberry arm. Readers requiring a single directional statement should anchor on the explicit outcome definition rather than on the classifier output.

**Relation to prior evidence.** The direction and magnitude found here are compatible with the broad arc of cranberry research: early enthusiasm from small, often unblinded juice trials; a period of pessimism following large, well-conducted negative trials in unselected or institutionalised populations; and more recent re-aggregation suggesting a genuine but small benefit that is confined to women with *recurrent* infection — precisely the population to which this review is restricted. The point estimate here (RR ≈ 0.77) sits comfortably within the range of pooled estimates reported for the rUTI-women subgroup in major prior syntheses. Critically, the absence of a demonstrated dose–response relationship with verified PAC content remains the central unresolved weakness of the entire field; without it, the mechanistic account remains an inference from in-vitro adhesion assays rather than a clinically validated pathway.

**Clinical implications.** For a woman with recurrent UTI who wishes to avoid or defer continuous antibiotic prophylaxis, cranberry products are a reasonable option: the pooled evidence suggests a small reduction in the chance of a further culture-verified symptomatic episode, the intervention is inexpensive, and tolerability concerns are largely confined to gastrointestinal upset and caloric/sugar load from juice preparations (adverse effects were not pooled in this review and cannot be quantified from it). Cranberry should not, on this evidence, be presented as equivalent to antibiotic prophylaxis, nor offered as a substitute for evaluation and treatment of an acute symptomatic infection. Where used, preparations with documented, analytically verified A-type proanthocyanidin content are preferable on mechanistic grounds, acknowledging that this preference is not yet supported by direct randomised comparison.

## 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

No structured `validation_flags` were returned for this synthesis, so no pipeline-generated data-quality exceptions are on record. The following handling decisions, which materially affect what the pooled number represents, are nonetheless documented for transparency:

- **Reviews and editorials** encountered during screening were used only to harvest references and were never pooled as primary data.
- **Multi-arm cranberry trials** were collapsed by summing events and participants across cranberry arms against the single placebo/no-treatment arm, to prevent double-counting of the shared control group; only placebo or no-treatment arms were admitted as controls.
- **Multiple follow-up timepoints** within a trial were reduced to the whole-study total at the longest on-treatment follow-up, so each trial contributes exactly one estimate (k = 8 effect sizes from 8 trials).
- **Non-pooled outcomes.** Asymptomatic bacteriuria, bacteriuria alone, symptom-only UTI definitions, adverse effects, adherence, dropouts, urinary *E. coli* counts and antibiotic consumption were extracted by some source trials but were, by protocol, excluded from pooling. No statement in this report should be read as a synthesis of those outcomes.
- **Denominators.** Intention-to-treat samples were used where reported; otherwise the analysed sample as reported by the paper. The mix of ITT and as-analysed denominators across trials is a known, unquantified source of inconsistency.
- **Effect measure.** The RR was set by explicit user specification (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`), with no measure conflict recorded (`effect_measure_conflict = null`), and pooling was performed natively on the RR scale (`native_pooling_used = true`). The Hedges' g values in §3/§4 are a secondary standardized representation used for plotting and bias diagnostics only.
- **Direction classification.** The automated `favors` field did not resolve to a named arm (see §5.1). This is reported as returned.
- **Study identification.** Included trials are carried under coded identifiers (`study_1_R1`–`study_8_R8`); bibliographic attribution of each coded identifier is held in the pipeline's study register and is not reproduced here, so the reference list below is confined to methodological sources.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Small number of trials.** With k = 8, the between-study variance τ² is estimated imprecisely, the prediction interval is wide, moderator analysis is not feasible, and both publication-bias tests are underpowered.
2. **Fragile significance.** The upper confidence bound (0.964) sits close to the null and the pooled significance is sensitive to removal of the single most favourable trial.
3. **Prediction interval crosses the null.** The average effect is significant; the expected effect in a *new* trial is not confidently a reduction (≈0.47 to 1.26).
4. **Unmeasured intervention heterogeneity.** Formulation, daily dose and — most importantly — verified A-type proanthocyanidin content vary across trials and were not analysable. Trials using biologically inert product cannot be distinguished from trials of adequately dosed product.
5. **Comparator heterogeneity.** Protocol permitted placebo, no treatment, or other non-active control. No-treatment comparators are susceptible to differential symptom reporting and differential culture-seeking, which tends to exaggerate apparent benefit; the proportion of trials using each comparator type was not analysed.
6. **Outcome ascertainment.** Although all included trials required symptoms plus culture, the symptom thresholds, colony-forming-unit cut-offs and surveillance intensity (scheduled versus symptom-triggered culture) are not harmonised.
7. **Mixed denominators.** ITT and as-analysed samples were combined; differential dropout — frequently substantial in long cranberry-juice trials — may bias individual estimates in either direction.
8. **Risk-of-bias and certainty grading not formally executed.** No Cochrane RoB 2 domain assessment or GRADE certainty rating accompanied this synthesis. The absence of a formal appraisal means that allocation concealment, blinding integrity (notoriously difficult for juice preparations), and selective reporting cannot be factored into the certainty of the pooled estimate.
9. **Unblinded direction label.** The pipeline's direction classifier returned "neither", leaving the formal favoured-arm designation unresolved (§5.1).
10. **Generalisability.** The pooled estimate applies to ambulatory adult women with recurrent UTI. It should not be extrapolated to children, men, pregnant women, catheterised patients, or people with neurogenic bladder or structural urinary tract abnormality.
11. **Non-pooled safety data.** Adverse effects, tolerability and adherence were explicitly outside the pooling protocol, so this review cannot support any benefit–harm balance statement.

---

## 12. Conclusion

In women with recurrent urinary tract infection, cranberry products — juice, capsules, tablets or powder, at any dose — were associated with a pooled **Risk Ratio of 0.773 (95% CI 0.619 to 0.964; p = 0.0226; k = 8 trials; N = 1,514 participants; REML random-effects, native RR-scale pooling)** for experiencing at least one symptomatic, culture-verified UTI during follow-up, corresponding to an approximate 23% relative reduction in the proportion of women affected. Heterogeneity was low to moderate and not statistically significant (I² = 27.16%; τ² = 0.027058; Q = 9.819, df = 7, p = 0.1991), and publication-bias risk was low on the available diagnostics (Egger p = 0.4935; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0), subject to the power limitations inherent at k = 8. The pipeline's automated direction field classified the result as favouring **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**; under the review's own prespecified convention, an RR below 1 for this undesirable, culture-verified endpoint corresponds to fewer affected women in the cranberry arm.

The clinically honest summary is a **small, statistically significant, but fragile and not universally expectable benefit**: the confidence interval approaches the null, the approximate prediction interval crosses it, and significance depends on the single most favourable trial. Cranberry products may reasonably be offered to women with recurrent UTI who prefer a non-antibiotic option, with explicit counselling that the expected absolute benefit is modest (illustratively, NNT ≈ 11–15 at typical baseline risk) and that the product is not a treatment for acute infection. Resolution of this question now requires adequately powered randomised trials that (i) use analytically verified, standardised A-type proanthocyanidin doses, (ii) employ matched placebo rather than no-treatment comparators, (iii) adopt participant-level, symptom-plus-culture endpoints with symptom-triggered ascertainment, and (iv) pre-register a dose–response contrast — the single most informative missing piece of evidence in this literature.
