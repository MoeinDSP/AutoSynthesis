# Cranberry Products for Preventing Recurrent Symptomatic, Culture-Verified Urinary Tract Infection in Women: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Cranberry products are widely used to prevent recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI), but their effect on participant-level recurrence defined jointly by urinary symptoms and positive urine culture remains uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of cranberry products versus placebo, no treatment, or another non-active control on the risk of at least one symptomatic, culture-verified UTI among women with a history of recurrent UTI during the longest on-treatment follow-up.

**Methods:** Eligible interventions included cranberry juice, capsules, tablets, or powder at any dose. The prespecified outcome was the number of women experiencing at least one symptomatic, culture-verified UTI divided by the number analysed in each arm. The supplied pipeline identified 8 studies, of which 4 contributed effect sizes to the quantitative synthesis (745 participants). Risk ratios were pooled with a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.6564506598170393 (95% CI 0.366 to 1.178; p=0.15798569326778497; k=4; n=745). The confidence interval included the null value. The supplied favourability field classified the result as favouring neither arm. Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=72.14%; τ²=0.253638), and Cochran’s Q was statistically significant (Q=11.694; p=0.0085). Egger’s intercept was 1.0181 (p=0.0246), and trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study; publication-bias risk was classified as high.

**Conclusions:** The available quantitative synthesis did not establish a statistically significant reduction in symptomatic, culture-verified UTI with cranberry products. Substantial heterogeneity, evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry, the small number of pooled studies, and inconsistencies in the supplied study-level effect representation materially limit confidence in the estimate.

# Introduction

Recurrent urinary tract infection (UTI) is common among women and can cause substantial morbidity, repeated antimicrobial exposure, and healthcare use. Cranberry products—including juice, capsules, tablets, and powder—have been proposed as non-antibiotic preventive options. Their putative benefit is commonly attributed to inhibition of bacterial adherence to the urothelium, although formulations, doses, adherence, and bioactive-constituent content vary considerably.

A clinically meaningful prevention outcome is the participant-level risk of experiencing at least one recurrence that is supported by both symptoms and a positive urine culture. This avoids conflating recurrent disease with asymptomatic bacteriuria, symptoms without microbiological confirmation, or episode-based incidence rates that may count the same participant multiple times.

This review addressed the following question: among women with a history of recurrent UTIs, what is the effect of cranberry products, at any dose or formulation, versus placebo, no treatment, or another non-active control on the risk of at least one symptomatic, culture-verified UTI during the longest on-treatment follow-up?

The supplied trim-and-fill output included a value of **-0.2443** in a standardized-effect field. Because this is not labelled as a Risk Ratio and is not on the confirmed native RR scale, it must not be interpreted as an adjusted pooled RR or compared numerically with the pooled RR of 0.6564506598170393.

These diagnostics require caution. With only 4 pooled studies, Egger’s test is generally underpowered and potentially unstable, while funnel asymmetry may reflect heterogeneity, methodological differences, chance, selective reporting, or publication bias. Trim-and-fill also depends on assumptions that may not hold when heterogeneity is substantial. Even so, the significant Egger test and the supplied high-risk classification mean that selective dissemination or small-study effects cannot be dismissed.

# Discussion

## Principal findings

Across 4 studies comprising 745 participants, random-effects REML pooling yielded a Risk Ratio of 0.6564506598170393 (95% CI 0.366 to 1.178; p=0.15798569326778497). The estimate was statistically inconclusive because its confidence interval crossed 1. The supplied direction field classified the result as favouring neither arm.

Interpretation is further constrained by substantial heterogeneity (I²=72.14%; τ²=0.253638; Q=11.694, p=0.0085) and evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger’s intercept 1.0181, p=0.0246). Trim-and-fill suggested one potentially missing study, and the overall risk of publication bias was designated high.

## Clinical interpretation

The synthesis does not provide firm evidence that cranberry products reduce the risk of at least one symptomatic, culture-verified recurrence during treatment. It also does not establish equivalence or absence of benefit: the 95% confidence interval is broad and encompasses materially different effects. Because no control-group event rate was supplied, the clinical magnitude cannot be expressed on an absolute scale.

The intervention category encompasses products that may differ markedly in formulation, dose, standardization, palatability, and adherence. Pooling these interventions may be reasonable for a broad class-effect question, but the substantial heterogeneity suggests that a single pooled estimate may not describe every product or population adequately.

## Certainty and applicability

A formal certainty-of-evidence assessment was not supplied and cannot be completed reliably without study-level risk-of-bias judgments, directness assessments, event counts, and precision information. Nonetheless, confidence is limited by inconsistency, possible publication bias, the small number of contributing studies, incomplete reporting of study characteristics, and discrepancies in effect-measure representation.

Applicability is specifically intended for women with recurrent UTI receiving cranberry products during active treatment follow-up. The evidence should not automatically be extrapolated to children, men, pregnant women, patients with neurogenic bladder or urinary catheters, asymptomatic bacteriuria, post-treatment follow-up, or outcomes based only on symptoms or bacteriuria.

## Implications for research

Future trials should prespecify a participant-level outcome requiring both compatible urinary symptoms and a positive culture; report arm-specific numbers with at least one event and full intention-to-treat denominators; distinguish first recurrence from total episode counts; and provide recurrence timing without substituting hazard ratios for risks when cumulative risks can be reported. Cranberry formulations should be chemically characterized, with dose, proanthocyanidin content, adherence, co-interventions, attrition, and adverse events reported consistently. Prospective registration and complete publication of null findings would reduce selective-reporting concerns.

# Conclusion

In 4 studies with 745 participants, cranberry products versus non-active controls produced a pooled **Risk Ratio (RR) of 0.6564506598170393** (95% CI 0.366 to 1.178; p=0.15798569326778497) under a random-effects REML model. The result was not statistically significant, and the supplied metadata classified it as favouring neither arm. Substantial heterogeneity (I²=72.14%) and evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger’s p=0.0246), together with incomplete study reporting and effect-measure inconsistencies, mean that the pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously. The available results do not establish that cranberry products prevent symptomatic, culture-verified recurrent UTI in women, although clinically important benefit or harm cannot be excluded.
