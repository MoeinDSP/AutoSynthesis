# Testosterone Pretreatment Before Ovarian Stimulation for Women Undergoing IVF or ICSI: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis of Live Birth or Ongoing Pregnancy

## Abstract

### Background
Testosterone pretreatment has been proposed to improve reproductive outcomes before ovarian stimulation in women undergoing in vitro fertilisation (IVF) or intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI).

### Objective
To estimate the effect of testosterone pretreatment, compared with placebo or no pretreatment, on live birth per woman randomised, substituting ongoing pregnancy only when live birth was not reported.

### Methods
Eight studies were represented in the review dataset. Six study estimates, comprising 450 women, contributed to a random-effects meta-analysis using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The prespecified effect measure was the odds ratio (OR). No other reproductive, laboratory, or safety outcomes were pooled.

### Results
The pooled Odds Ratio (OR) was 2.3187708780772733 (95% CI 1.337 to 4.021; p = 0.0027540427615511585; k = 6; n = 450). Heterogeneity was not detected (Q = 3.717, p = 0.5908; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). The analysis output classified favourability as “neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)”; therefore, notwithstanding the prespecified outcome definition, this report does not override that classification or make an arm-specific benefit claim without confirmed event coding. Egger’s intercept was 1.1077 (p = 0.6457), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0); publication-bias risk was classified as low.

### Conclusions
The synthesis produced a statistically significant pooled OR with no observed between-study heterogeneity. However, interpretation is constrained by unresolved event-direction metadata, unavailable study-level outcome labels and bibliographic details, only six contributing estimates, and incomplete screening information. Confirmation of event coding and direct verification against trial reports are required before drawing a definitive clinical conclusion about benefit.

## Introduction

Testosterone pretreatment before controlled ovarian stimulation has been investigated as a strategy for improving outcomes in women undergoing assisted reproduction, particularly IVF or ICSI. Proposed biological rationales include enhanced follicular responsiveness to gonadotrophins, but clinical decisions should be based on patient-important outcomes rather than intermediate measures such as oocyte yield.

This review addressed the following question: among women undergoing IVF or ICSI, what is the effect of testosterone pretreatment before ovarian stimulation—such as transdermal testosterone gel or patch—compared with placebo or no pretreatment on live birth per woman randomised? Ongoing pregnancy per woman randomised was the prespecified substitute only when live birth was unavailable. The direction convention specified that an OR greater than 1 would represent higher odds of the counted event, but the analysis output’s separate arm-favourability classification is reported without reinterpretation below.

## Meta-Analytic Result

Across six contributing studies and 450 women, the REML random-effects pooled Odds Ratio (OR) was **2.3187708780772733** (95% CI **1.337 to 4.021**; p = **0.0027540427615511585**). The confidence interval excluded the null OR of 1, and the model therefore indicated a statistically detectable difference in the odds of the counted outcome between groups.

The analysis output assigned the following favourability classification: **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)”**. In accordance with that supplied classification, the result is not described here as favouring either testosterone or control. Although the review question prespecified live birth—or ongoing pregnancy when live birth was unavailable—as the event of interest, the underlying event coding was not supplied for verification. Confirming that the model counted the desired positive event, rather than its complement, is necessary before making an arm-specific clinical interpretation.

The cumulative meta-analysis plot is located at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`. No cumulative numerical sequence was supplied, so temporal or sequential changes in the pooled estimate cannot be reported exactly from the context data alone.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was not detected:

- Cochran’s Q = **3.717**;
- Q-test p = **0.5908**;
- I² = **0.0%**; and
- τ² = **0.0**.

Thus, the estimated proportion of observed variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity was 0.0%, and REML estimated zero between-study variance. This should not be interpreted as proof that the studies were clinically identical. With only six estimates, heterogeneity statistics have limited power, and the absence of study-level information prevents assessment of differences in populations, treatment regimens, and outcome definitions.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **1.1077** with p = **0.6457**. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies and reported an adjusted value of **0.5054** in the source output. Because the scale and label of that adjusted value were not identified as an OR, it is not interpreted as an adjusted native-scale pooled effect.

The pipeline classified publication-bias risk as **low**. The non-significant Egger test and absence of imputed studies provide no detected evidence of small-study asymmetry, but they do not exclude publication bias. Both Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill are unreliable with very few studies; here, only six estimates were available. No funnel-plot path was supplied.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis of six estimates from 450 women yielded a pooled OR of 2.3187708780772733, with a 95% CI of 1.337 to 4.021 and p = 0.0027540427615511585. Statistical heterogeneity was absent by the supplied metrics, and the available asymmetry analyses did not identify evidence of publication bias.

The analysis focused appropriately on a patient-important reproductive endpoint and excluded surrogate or secondary outcomes from pooling. It also used ongoing pregnancy only as the planned substitute when live birth was unavailable. This hierarchy avoids mixing the primary result with clinical pregnancy, miscarriage, multiple pregnancy, oocyte counts, or adverse effects. Nevertheless, the dataset did not identify which outcome each study actually contributed, preventing assessment of possible differences between live birth and ongoing pregnancy ascertainment.

The statistical result is potentially clinically important because its confidence interval excludes the null. However, the pipeline’s explicit favourability output did not assign the result to either treatment arm, noting dependence on which event was counted. That unresolved coding issue is central: a ratio measure cannot be translated into a claim of benefit without verifying the numerator event. The result should therefore be regarded as evidence of a between-group difference on the supplied OR scale, pending verification of event coding against trial-level data.

The finding of I² = 0.0% and τ² = 0.0 does not establish universal consistency. Six studies provide limited information for estimating heterogeneity, and no details were available regarding participant prognosis, testosterone exposure, stimulation protocols, or study methods. Apparent statistical consistency may coexist with clinically important differences that could not be examined.

Publication-bias diagnostics were reassuring in direction but underpowered. Egger’s p = 0.6457 and trim-and-fill k₀ = 0 support the pipeline’s low-risk designation, while the small number of studies warrants substantial caution. Selective outcome reporting also cannot be assessed using funnel-plot methods and may be especially relevant when live birth is replaced by ongoing pregnancy in some reports.

## Limitations

1. **Incomplete search and screening documentation:** Databases, search dates, strategies, screening counts, and exclusion reasons were unavailable, preventing full assessment of search completeness and PRISMA flow.
2. **Missing trial characteristics:** Authors, settings, participant profiles, intervention regimens, comparators, and outcome definitions were not supplied.
3. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** Randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding, missing outcomes, selective reporting, and other bias domains could not be evaluated.
4. **Unverified endpoint coding:** The counted binary event was not directly documented at study level, and the supplied favourability field did not identify an arm.
5. **Mixed endpoint hierarchy not traceable:** The data did not indicate which studies reported live birth and which used ongoing pregnancy as the substitute.
6. **Study-level scale mismatch:** Listed individual values appeared in a field named `hedges_g`, while the synthesis used a native OR; event counts and study-specific ORs were unavailable for verification.
7. **Two represented studies did not contribute:** The reasons for their omission from quantitative pooling were not supplied, creating uncertainty about missing outcome data or eligibility.
8. **Small evidence base:** Only six estimates and 450 women contributed, limiting precision in heterogeneity and publication-bias assessments.
9. **No moderator analyses:** Potential effect modification by patient prognosis, testosterone regimen, or assisted-reproduction protocol could not be examined.
10. **Safety not assessed:** In accordance with the prespecified scope, adverse effects were not pooled; consequently, the report cannot establish the balance of benefits and harms.

## Conclusion

The REML random-effects synthesis of six estimates from 450 women produced a pooled Odds Ratio (OR) of **2.3187708780772733** (95% CI **1.337 to 4.021**; p = **0.0027540427615511585**) for live birth per woman randomised, using ongoing pregnancy only where live birth was unavailable. No statistical heterogeneity was detected (I² = **0.0%**, τ² = **0.0**), and publication-bias risk was classified as low.

However, the supplied favourability classification was **neither arm**, because interpretation depended on the event counted, and the underlying event coding could not be verified. Combined with incomplete study documentation, lack of risk-of-bias information, and a mismatch between the study-level effect field and the pooled native measure, this prevents a definitive claim that testosterone pretreatment improves live birth or ongoing pregnancy. Verification against primary trial reports and correctly coded event counts is required before the result is used to guide clinical practice.
