# Short-term tolerability of the live attenuated Oka/Merck herpes zoster vaccine (Zostavax) versus placebo in adults aged 50 years and older: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials

## Abstract

**Background.** The live attenuated Oka/Merck herpes zoster vaccine (Zostavax) was for more than a decade the principal immunisation strategy for preventing herpes zoster and post-herpetic neuralgia in older adults. Its efficacy is well characterised, but the aggregate short-term reactogenicity signal — the probability that a vaccinee reports at least one adverse event (AE) of any type in the days or weeks after injection — is less frequently synthesised quantitatively.

**Objective.** To estimate the effect of a single dose of live attenuated Oka/Merck zoster vaccine, compared with placebo injection, on the risk of reporting one or more adverse events of any type during short-term post-vaccination safety follow-up in adults aged 50 years or older.

**Methods.** We conducted a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of randomised, placebo-controlled trials. The outcome was the number of participants reporting ≥1 AE of any type (injection-site or systemic, vaccine-related or not) during the trial-defined short-term safety window (e.g. days 0–28 or 0–42), out of the number randomised or vaccinated per arm. Where AEs were ascertained only in a designated safety substudy of a larger trial, substudy counts and denominators were used; multiple vaccine batches or dose levels within a trial were combined into a single vaccine arm by summing events and participants; percentages were back-converted to counts using the relevant arm size. Effects were pooled on the risk ratio (RR) scale using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) random-effects estimation. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Four randomised placebo-controlled trials contributing four effect estimates and 7,080 participants were included. The pooled risk ratio for reporting one or more adverse events of any type was **RR = 1.790 (95% CI 1.342 to 2.389; p = 0.0000756; k = 4; n = 7,080)**, computed natively on the risk ratio scale. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 72.75%, τ² = 0.051079, Q = 7.618, df = 3, p = 0.0546). The direction field recorded by the analysis pipeline for this estimate is "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"; by the pre-specified effect-direction convention for this outcome, an RR above 1 denotes a larger proportion of participants reporting ≥1 AE in the vaccine arm — i.e. poorer short-term tolerability with vaccine. Egger's regression showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept 0.8221, p = 0.3552) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), indicating a low assessed risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** Live attenuated Oka/Merck zoster vaccine approximately doubles the probability that an older adult will report at least one adverse event of any type during short-term post-vaccination follow-up relative to placebo. This composite endpoint is dominated by expected, self-limited injection-site reactogenicity and does not by itself indicate serious harm; between-trial heterogeneity was substantial and only four trials were available, so the pooled magnitude should be read as an approximate, imprecise summary of reactogenicity rather than a precise risk estimate.


## 1. Introduction

Herpes zoster (shingles) results from reactivation of latent varicella-zoster virus in sensory ganglia and becomes markedly more frequent and more severe with advancing age, largely because of age-associated decline in varicella-zoster-virus-specific cell-mediated immunity. Its most burdensome complication, post-herpetic neuralgia, can persist for months to years and is poorly responsive to analgesia. For this reason, immunisation of older adults became a central public-health strategy, and the live attenuated Oka/Merck zoster vaccine (Zostavax) — a higher-potency formulation of the paediatric varicella vaccine strain — was licensed for adults aged 50 years and older on the basis of large randomised efficacy trials.

The efficacy of this vaccine against incident herpes zoster and post-herpetic neuralgia has been synthesised repeatedly. Its *tolerability*, by contrast, is more often reported descriptively, trial by trial, than pooled. Clinicians and vaccinees are nevertheless routinely interested in a single, intuitive quantity: how much more likely is a person to notice *something* — soreness, erythema, swelling, pruritus, headache, fatigue, or any other event — after the vaccine than after a placebo injection? This composite "one or more adverse events of any type" endpoint is a blunt instrument: it aggregates trivial, expected, self-limited injection-site reactogenicity with unrelated intercurrent illness, and it therefore says little about serious harm. It is, however, the endpoint that most directly maps onto the subjective experience of vaccination and onto counselling conversations about what to expect.

This review therefore addresses a single, deliberately narrow question: **in adults aged 50 years or older, what is the effect of a single administration of the live attenuated Oka/Merck herpes zoster vaccine, compared with a placebo injection, on the risk of reporting one or more adverse events of any type during the short-term post-vaccination safety follow-up period defined by each trial?**

By pre-specification, a risk ratio greater than 1 indicates that a larger proportion of participants experienced one or more adverse events in the live zoster vaccine group than in the placebo group — that is, more adverse events and poorer short-term tolerability with the vaccine.

## 2. Methods: Search and Screening

### 2.1 Design

The review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the PRISMA framework for systematic reviews and meta-analyses of intervention studies. The protocol elements (population, intervention, comparator, outcome, analysis scale) were fixed before synthesis, including the decision to pool on the risk ratio scale.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults aged 50 years or older. Trials recruiting mixed-age populations were eligible where the randomised population was predominantly in this age band.

**Intervention.** Live attenuated Oka/Merck herpes zoster vaccine (Zostavax), any batch or dose level, administered as a single injection. Trials evaluating more than one manufacturing batch or more than one potency level contributed a single combined vaccine arm (see §2.5).

**Comparator.** Placebo injection.

**Outcome.** The number of participants reporting one or more adverse events of any type — injection-site or systemic, judged vaccine-related or not — during the short-term post-vaccination safety follow-up window as defined by the individual trial (typically days 0–28 or days 0–42 after injection), expressed out of the number randomised or vaccinated in each arm.

**Design.** Randomised controlled trials with a concurrent placebo arm.

**Exclusions.** Non-randomised and single-arm studies; narrative and systematic reviews; trials of the adjuvanted recombinant subunit zoster vaccine (a different product and therefore outside the stated comparison); trials reporting only efficacy endpoints with no extractable count of participants with ≥1 AE; and duplicate or secondary reports of a trial already represented in the dataset.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

Electronic bibliographic databases of biomedical literature and clinical-trial registries were searched for randomised placebo-controlled trials of the live attenuated Oka/Merck zoster vaccine, combining vaccine terms (Zostavax, Oka/Merck, zoster vaccine live, varicella-zoster vaccine) with design terms (randomised, placebo, double-blind) and safety terms (adverse event, reactogenicity, safety, tolerability). Reference lists of included trials and of prior reviews of zoster vaccination were screened for additional eligible reports. Prior reviews were used only as sources of candidate references and were not themselves eligible for inclusion.

### 2.4 Study selection

Records were screened on title and abstract, and potentially eligible reports were assessed in full text against the criteria above. The selection process is summarised in the PRISMA flow below, reporting the stages as realised in this synthesis.

**PRISMA flow (summary).**

| Stage | n |
|---|---|
| Records identified and screened | multiple records across databases and registries |
| Reports assessed for eligibility against the PICO | full-text reports of candidate randomised placebo-controlled trials |
| Reports excluded | reviews and non-randomised designs; trials without an extractable count of participants with ≥1 AE of any type; secondary/duplicate reports of an already-included trial |
| **Studies included in quantitative synthesis** | **4** |
| **Effect estimates contributed** | **4** |
| **Participants analysed** | **7,080** |

### 2.5 Data extraction and handling rules

For each trial we extracted the number of participants with ≥1 AE of any type and the corresponding denominator (number randomised or vaccinated) in each arm, the definition and duration of the safety window, the vaccine batches/dose levels evaluated, and whether AEs were ascertained in the full randomised population or in a designated safety substudy. Three pre-specified handling rules were applied consistently:

1. **Safety substudies.** Where adverse events were collected only in a designated safety substudy nested within a larger efficacy trial, the substudy event counts and substudy denominators were used, not the full trial denominators. This is the correct denominator for a per-protocol-ascertained reactogenicity endpoint, but it means that the participant total contributing to this meta-analysis is much smaller than the total randomised across the underlying efficacy programme.
2. **Multiple batches or dose levels.** Trials evaluating more than one vaccine batch or potency contributed a single combined vaccine group, obtained by summing events and summing participants across the vaccine arms. This avoids the unit-of-analysis error that would arise from entering the shared placebo arm more than once.
3. **Percentages.** Where a trial reported only the percentage of participants with ≥1 AE, counts were reconstructed by applying the reported percentage to the arm size to which it referred.

### 2.6 Statistical analysis

Effects were pooled on the **risk ratio (RR)** scale. The effect measure was set by explicit specification of the review question (`effect_measure_source`: explicit user request), and **native pooling on the RR scale was used** — that is, the reported pooled value is a genuine risk ratio and not a standardised surrogate back-transformed onto a ratio scale.

A random-effects model was fitted by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**, which is appropriate given anticipated clinical and methodological diversity in safety-window definitions, solicitation methods and populations. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, the I² statistic and the between-study variance τ². Small-study effects and potential publication bias were examined using Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. Study-level estimates were additionally expressed on a standardised scale (Hedges' g) for display purposes and for the bias diagnostics; these standardised values are reported transparently below and are *not* interchangeable with the pooled risk ratio.

All figures referenced in this report were generated from the analytic dataset and are archived at the paths given in §9.

## 3. Included Studies

Four randomised, placebo-controlled trials met all eligibility criteria and contributed four independent effect estimates to the synthesis, with a combined analysed sample of **7,080 participants**. All four compared a single administration of live attenuated Oka/Merck zoster vaccine (pooled across batches or dose levels where applicable) against a placebo injection, and all reported the number of participants experiencing one or more adverse events of any type within a short post-vaccination safety window.

The included trials span a wide range of size — from small immunogenicity/lot-consistency and dose-ranging studies with a few dozen analysed participants to a very large safety substudy embedded in a major efficacy trial. This size gradient is central to interpreting the synthesis: one study contributes 6,616 of the 7,080 analysed participants (93.4%), while the three remaining trials together contribute 464 participants (6.6%).

### 3.1 Risk of bias considerations

All included studies were randomised and placebo-controlled, which is the strongest available design for an adverse-event contrast because it supplies a concurrent estimate of the background rate of symptom reporting (nocebo effects, unrelated intercurrent illness, and the reactogenicity of the injection procedure itself). Several features nevertheless warrant caution for this particular endpoint:

- **Ascertainment method.** Trials that actively solicited injection-site symptoms using diary cards will record far higher "≥1 AE" rates in both arms, and a different vaccine-versus-placebo contrast, than trials relying on spontaneous report. Because the endpoint aggregates everything, differences in solicitation intensity propagate directly into the risk ratio.
- **Blinding integrity.** Live zoster vaccine produces more visible and palpable injection-site reactions than saline or diluent placebo, so participant blinding can be partially undermined in the vaccine arm. This can inflate systemic symptom reporting in vaccinees independently of any biological effect.
- **Safety-window length.** Windows ranging from 28 to 42 days differ in how much unrelated intercurrent morbidity they capture; longer windows dilute the vaccine-specific signal towards the null.
- **Substudy selection.** Where AEs were ascertained only in a nested safety substudy, substudy participants may not be fully representative of the parent trial population.

These are properties of the endpoint and its ascertainment rather than defects of trial conduct, but they should be read alongside the heterogeneity results in §6.

## 4. Effect Sizes

Four effect estimates were available, one per trial. On the standardised display scale, three of the four point estimates lay above zero (indicating a higher proportion of participants reporting ≥1 AE in the vaccine arm), and one — the smallest study, Study 1 (n = 54) — lay marginally below zero (g = −0.124) with a confidence interval spanning the null (−0.658 to 0.410), i.e. entirely compatible with no difference and with a moderate difference in either direction.

The three remaining studies showed consistent, statistically unambiguous increases in AE reporting with vaccine on the standardised scale: Study 2, g = 0.899 (0.609 to 1.190); Study 3, g = 0.633 (0.355 to 0.911); Study 4, g = 0.532 (0.483 to 0.581). The precision gradient is extreme — the width of Study 4's interval is 0.098 units against 1.068 units for Study 1, an order-of-magnitude difference — and this drives the weighting structure of the pooled analysis.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was specified; the review does not span multiple distinct outcomes or timepoints, so one pooled estimate is reported.

> **Pooled risk ratio (RR) for reporting one or more adverse events of any type, live attenuated Oka/Merck zoster vaccine versus placebo:**
> **RR = 1.790 (95% CI 1.342 to 2.389), p = 7.556 × 10⁻⁵, k = 4 studies, n = 7,080 participants, REML random-effects model.**

The effect measure is the **Risk Ratio (RR)**, and **native pooling on the risk ratio scale was used** (`native_pooling_used`: true), so this is a confirmed native-scale risk ratio rather than a standardised fallback.

**Direction.** The direction field recorded for this estimate is **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"** — that is, the pipeline does not assign favourability to either arm for this estimate, because for a ratio measure the desirability of a value above 1 depends entirely on which event was being counted. Applying the pre-specified effect-direction convention for *this* outcome, in which the counted event is "experiencing one or more adverse events", an RR above 1 means a **larger proportion of participants experienced one or more adverse events in the live zoster vaccine group than in the placebo group**, i.e. more adverse events and worse short-term tolerability with the vaccine.

**Magnitude.** Taken at face value, the point estimate implies that participants given live zoster vaccine were approximately **1.8 times as likely** as placebo recipients to report at least one adverse event of any type during the short-term safety window. The 95% confidence interval (1.342 to 2.389) excludes 1.0 and is consistent with a relative increase of between roughly 34% and 139%. The p-value of 7.56 × 10⁻⁵ indicates that an increase of this kind is very unlikely to be a chance finding under the null hypothesis of equal risk, even allowing for the small number of trials.

**What this does and does not mean.** Because the endpoint counts *any* adverse event of *any* type, including mild and expected injection-site soreness, erythema, swelling and pruritus, an RR of 1.790 should be read as a reactogenicity signal rather than as evidence of harm. It is entirely consistent with — indeed, expected from — an immunologically active injectable product compared with inert placebo, and it carries no information about serious adverse events, hospitalisations or deaths, which were not the outcome of this review.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **substantial**:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| I² | **72.75%** |
| τ² (between-study variance) | **0.051079** |
| Cochran's Q | **7.618** (df = 3) |
| p-value for Q | **0.0546** |
| Estimation method | REML |

An I² of 72.75% indicates that roughly three-quarters of the observed variability in effect estimates exceeds what would be expected from within-study sampling error alone, placing this body of evidence in the conventionally "substantial" heterogeneity band. Notably, Cochran's Q test is only borderline (p = 0.0546, i.e. not significant at the conventional 5% threshold) — a familiar pattern when k is small, because Q has low power with four studies. The two statistics are not in conflict: Q asks whether heterogeneity can be *detected*, while I² describes how much of the observed dispersion is attributable to it. With k = 4 the honest reading is that heterogeneity is probably material but cannot be characterised precisely, and τ² itself (0.051079, on the log-RR scale) is estimated with very wide uncertainty.

The practical consequence is that the 95% confidence interval around the pooled RR (1.342 to 2.389) is appreciably wider than a fixed-effect interval would be, and that a *prediction* interval for the true RR in a new, comparable trial would be wider still — plausibly spanning values close to 1 as well as values above 2.5. Readers should therefore treat 1.790 as the centre of a fairly broad plausible range rather than as a tightly determined parameter.

Inspection of the study-level estimates suggests the heterogeneity arises mainly from a contrast between the single discordant small study (Study 1, g = −0.124, n = 54) and the three concordant studies (g = 0.532 to 0.899). Among those three, the dispersion is modest and the confidence intervals overlap substantially — Study 4's interval (0.483 to 0.581) sits inside Study 3's (0.355 to 0.911), and Study 2's lower limit (0.609) only narrowly exceeds Study 3's upper limit. Heterogeneity is thus better described as *one outlying small trial* than as pervasive inconsistency.

## 7. Moderators

No formal moderator analysis (meta-regression or subgroup analysis) was performed, and none could be performed credibly. With k = 4 studies and one degree of freedom to spare after estimating an intercept and a between-study variance, any meta-regression would be severely overfitted; conventional guidance requires approximately ten studies per covariate. Subgroup analyses by safety-window length (28 versus 42 days), by ascertainment method (solicited diary card versus spontaneous report), by vaccine potency/batch configuration, or by full-trial versus nested-substudy ascertainment were therefore not attempted.

The plausible moderators are nevertheless worth naming explicitly, because they are the most likely explanations for the 72.75% I²:

1. **Intensity of adverse-event solicitation** — the single most powerful determinant of how many participants in *either* arm report "≥1 AE of any type".
2. **Length of the safety window** — longer windows capture more unrelated intercurrent events in both arms, pulling the RR towards 1.
3. **Study size and setting** — the smallest trial (n = 54) is the lone discordant estimate, which is the signature of sampling noise rather than of a distinct biological effect.
4. **Vaccine potency** — dose-ranging trials that pooled lower- and higher-potency lots into a single vaccine arm will have attenuated reactogenicity relative to trials using only the licensed potency.
5. **Blinding integrity for visible injection-site reactions**, as discussed in §3.1.

The exploratory precision-versus-effect display (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) is presented for descriptive purposes only; no inferential claim about moderators is made.

## 8. Publication Bias

Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the trim-and-fill procedure:

| Diagnostic | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.8221 |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.3552** |
| Trim-and-fill: studies imputed (k₀) | **0** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (standardised scale) | 0.5347 |
| Overall assessed risk | **Low** |

Egger's regression showed no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept 0.8221, p = 0.3552), indicating no detectable small-study effect in this body of evidence. Consistent with this, the trim-and-fill procedure imputed **no** missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the pooled estimate is unchanged after adjustment; the adjusted standardised value of 0.5347 corresponds to the unadjusted funnel-symmetry-based estimate rather than to a revised risk ratio. Together these results support a **low** assessed risk of publication bias.

**Caveat.** These tests have limited statistical power when the number of included studies is small — conventionally fewer than ten, and here only four. "No evidence of bias" must therefore not be read as "evidence of no bias". Funnel asymmetry, where present, can also reflect genuine between-study heterogeneity or differences in methodological quality rather than selective publication alone; and with k = 4 the Egger intercept is estimated from very few points and is correspondingly unstable. A further consideration specific to safety endpoints is that reactogenicity results are rarely the headline finding of a vaccine trial and are typically published regardless of their direction, which weakens the usual selective-publication mechanism but introduces a different problem: *selective reporting within* published papers, where composite "≥1 AE" counts may be omitted even though the trial was published in full. Such within-report selection is not detectable by funnel-based methods.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 Principal findings

Pooling four randomised placebo-controlled trials and 7,080 analysed participants, a single administration of the live attenuated Oka/Merck herpes zoster vaccine was associated with a **pooled risk ratio of 1.790 (95% CI 1.342 to 2.389; p = 7.556 × 10⁻⁵)** for reporting one or more adverse events of any type during short-term post-vaccination follow-up. In plain terms, older adults who received the vaccine were roughly 1.8 times as likely as placebo recipients to report at least one adverse event of some kind in the weeks after injection. The finding was statistically robust and not materially attributable to small-study effects or publication bias, but it was accompanied by substantial statistical heterogeneity (I² = 72.75%).

### 10.2 Interpretation

This result is best understood as a **reactogenicity** finding. The outcome was defined deliberately broadly — any adverse event, of any type, injection-site or systemic, judged vaccine-related or not — precisely to capture the aggregate experience of being vaccinated. Such a composite is dominated by the most common events, which for an intramuscular or subcutaneous live viral vaccine in older adults are mild and transient injection-site symptoms: pain or soreness, erythema, swelling and pruritus. An approximate doubling of the probability of reporting *something* is the expected consequence of administering an immunologically active biological product rather than an inert injection, and it is consistent with the reactogenicity profile described in the individual trial reports and in the product labelling.

Three corollaries follow. First, the estimate speaks to **tolerability, not safety**: it contains no information about serious adverse events, vaccine-related hospitalisation, disseminated varicella-like rash, or death, none of which were the outcome of this review. A review restricted to serious adverse events would be a different analysis with a different — and, on the published evidence, substantially more reassuring — answer. Second, the estimate is **endpoint-definition-sensitive**: because the denominator of "any event" includes background intercurrent morbidity that occurs at similar rates in both arms, the RR is pulled towards 1 in trials with long safety windows and pushed away from 1 in trials with intensive short-window solicitation. Third, the estimate is **not a clinical decision quantity on its own**: it must be weighed against the vaccine's demonstrated efficacy against herpes zoster and post-herpetic neuralgia, which is the benefit side of a ledger this review does not attempt to balance.

### 10.3 Relation to other evidence

The direction and approximate magnitude of this pooled estimate are concordant with the narrative safety descriptions in the pivotal zoster-vaccine programme, in which injection-site adverse experiences were consistently more frequent among vaccinees than among placebo recipients while systemic adverse experiences and serious adverse events were comparable between arms. The present synthesis adds a quantitative, random-effects summary of the composite endpoint with an explicit confidence interval and an explicit heterogeneity assessment, which trial-by-trial descriptive reporting does not provide. It also makes transparent that the pooled estimate rests overwhelmingly on one very large safety substudy.

### 10.4 Weight concentration

A dominant structural feature of this synthesis deserves emphasis. Study 4 contributes 6,616 of 7,080 analysed participants (93.4%) and, on the standardised scale, is by far the most precise estimate (95% CI width 0.098 versus 0.556 to 1.068 for the other three). Even under a random-effects model — which deliberately down-weights large studies relative to a fixed-effect analysis — this trial substantially determines the pooled result. The pooled RR of 1.790 should therefore be read partly as a precise description of one large trial's reactogenicity experience, moderated upward and widened by the smaller trials, rather than as a consensus of four independent and equally informative replications. The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) makes this dependence visible.

## 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were raised for this synthesis. For transparency, the following data-handling decisions were nevertheless applied and should be borne in mind when reading the pooled estimate:

- **Reviews and non-randomised reports were excluded** from the evidence base and were used only as sources of candidate references.
- **Secondary and duplicate reports of the same trial were not entered separately**, so that no trial contributes more than one effect estimate and no placebo arm is counted twice.
- **Multiple vaccine batches or dose levels within a trial were summed into a single vaccine arm**, which prevents unit-of-analysis error from re-using a shared placebo arm but blurs any dose–reactogenicity gradient.
- **Where adverse events were collected only in a nested safety substudy, substudy counts and substudy denominators were used.** Consequently the analysed n of 7,080 is much smaller than the total randomised across the underlying trial programme, and the AE population may not be fully representative of the parent trials.
- **Percentages were back-converted to counts** using the arm size to which they referred, which introduces minor rounding error in the reconstructed numerators.
- **A single effect measure (risk ratio) was used throughout and pooled natively**; no effect estimates required a standardised fallback, and no outcomes had to be pooled on separate scales. The study-level Hedges' g values reported in Table 1 are display/diagnostic quantities and are not the basis of the pooled risk ratio.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small number of trials (k = 4).** This limits every downstream inference: τ² and I² are imprecisely estimated, Cochran's Q is underpowered (hence its borderline p = 0.0546 despite I² = 72.75%), meta-regression is impossible, and the funnel-based bias diagnostics are weak.
2. **Substantial unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 72.75% with τ² = 0.051079 indicates meaningful between-trial inconsistency that we could not attribute to specific study features. The prediction interval for a future trial is correspondingly wide and may well include values close to no difference.
3. **Extreme weight concentration.** One trial supplies 93.4% of analysed participants, so the pooled estimate is not a balanced average of independent replications (§10.4).
4. **Heterogeneous outcome ascertainment.** Safety windows varied (e.g. days 0–28 versus 0–42) and solicitation intensity almost certainly varied; a composite "≥1 AE of any type" endpoint is exquisitely sensitive to both, and these were not harmonised.
5. **Composite, non-specific endpoint.** The outcome mixes trivial expected reactogenicity with unrelated intercurrent illness and provides no information on serious adverse events. It should not be used to characterise vaccine safety in the regulatory sense.
6. **Possible partial unblinding.** Visible injection-site reactions in the vaccine arm may compromise blinding and inflate reporting of systemic symptoms independently of biology.
7. **Direction field ambiguity.** The pipeline records the favoured arm as "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"; the clinical reading of RR > 1 as "worse tolerability with vaccine" rests on the pre-specified convention for this outcome rather than on an automatically assigned favourability label.
8. **Nested-substudy denominators.** Use of substudy populations for AE ascertainment is methodologically correct but may limit generalisability to the full randomised populations of the parent trials.
9. **Formal risk-of-bias scoring not tabulated.** Risk of bias was considered qualitatively (§3.1) but domain-level judgements per study are not reported, and the certainty of evidence was not formally graded.
10. **Absolute risks not estimable.** Because arm-level event rates vary enormously with ascertainment method, this review reports only the relative effect; a pooled risk difference or number-needed-to-harm would be misleading across such heterogeneous ascertainment and was not computed.

## 13. Conclusion

Across four randomised placebo-controlled trials and 7,080 analysed participants, a single dose of the live attenuated Oka/Merck herpes zoster vaccine increased the probability that an adult aged 50 years or older would report **one or more adverse events of any type** during short-term post-vaccination follow-up, with a **pooled risk ratio of 1.790 (95% CI 1.342 to 2.389; p = 7.556 × 10⁻⁵; k = 4; REML random-effects; native risk-ratio pooling)**. By the pre-specified convention for this outcome, this corresponds to a larger proportion of vaccinees than placebo recipients reporting at least one adverse event — approximately a doubling of that probability — although the pipeline's own favourability field assigns the result to neither arm because favourability of a ratio measure depends on which event was counted. There was no detectable funnel asymmetry (Egger p = 0.3552) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), supporting a low assessed risk of publication bias, with the usual caveat that such tests are underpowered at k = 4.

Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 72.75%, τ² = 0.051079; Q = 7.618, df = 3, p = 0.0546) and could not be explained, and 93.4% of the analysed sample came from a single trial. The pooled figure should therefore be taken as an approximate, imprecise summary of **short-term reactogenicity** — driven predominantly by expected, mild, self-limited injection-site symptoms — and not as evidence of serious harm. For clinical counselling, the practical message is that vaccinees should be told that noticeable but typically minor post-injection symptoms are roughly twice as likely after live zoster vaccine as after placebo. For research, the priority is standardised, pre-specified reactogenicity reporting (fixed solicitation windows, separate reporting of injection-site and systemic events, and separate reporting of serious adverse events) so that future syntheses of vaccine tolerability are less dependent on a single large substudy and less vulnerable to ascertainment-driven heterogeneity.
