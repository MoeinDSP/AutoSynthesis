# Fluoropyrimidine-Based Combination Chemotherapy versus Gemcitabine plus Nab-Paclitaxel for Overall Survival in Advanced Pancreatic Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel (GnP) is a reference first-line regimen for locally advanced or metastatic pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma. Whether fluoropyrimidine-based combination regimens — nab-paclitaxel or gemcitabine partnered with S-1, capecitabine, or 5-fluorouracil/leucovorin — confer a survival advantage over GnP remains uncertain.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of fluoropyrimidine-based combination chemotherapy versus gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel on overall survival (OS) in adults with advanced pancreatic cancer.

**Methods.** A systematic search of the clinical oncology literature was screened against prespecified eligibility criteria restricted to adults with locally advanced or metastatic pancreatic cancer, a fluoropyrimidine-based combination experimental arm, a gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel comparator, and a reported time-to-event estimate for overall survival. Six records entered the evidence synthesis stage and five contributed extractable, non-overlapping effect estimates. The target outcome was overall survival, defined as time from randomisation or treatment initiation to death from any cause, extracted as each paper's own Cox proportional-hazards / time-to-event estimate. Progression-free survival, objective response rate, disease control rate, and grade 3/4 toxicity were explicitly not target outcomes. Effects were pooled on the ratio scale with a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML). The effect measure was Risk Ratio (RR) at explicit user request; pooling was performed natively on that scale. Heterogeneity was characterised with Q, I², and τ²; small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Five effect estimates comprising 1,245 patients were pooled. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.873 (95% CI 0.760 to 1.003; p = 0.0556; k = 5; N = 1,245). Per the prespecified direction field, this result favours **neither arm** — it is essentially a tie, or a ratio measure whose favourability depends on which event was counted — and the 95% confidence interval crosses the line of no effect. Heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 59.18%, τ² = 0.011731, Q = 7.829, df = 4, p = 0.0981). Egger's regression showed no statistically significant funnel asymmetry (intercept −0.0395; p = 0.4013), although trim-and-fill imputed two potentially missing studies (k₀ = 2) with an adjusted estimate of −0.1127 on the log scale (≈0.893 on the ratio scale), i.e. unchanged in direction and magnitude. Overall publication-bias risk was rated moderate.

**Conclusions.** In this small body of evidence, fluoropyrimidine-based combination chemotherapy did not demonstrate a statistically significant overall survival advantage over gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel in advanced pancreatic cancer. The pooled point estimate lies on the side of the experimental regimens but the interval includes no difference, and moderate between-study heterogeneity together with a small number of contributing studies limits confidence. The findings are best read as compatible with therapeutic equipoise rather than as evidence of superiority of either strategy.

**Registration.** Not registered.

## 1. Introduction

Pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma (PDAC) remains among the most lethal solid tumours, with the majority of patients presenting with locally advanced or metastatic disease that is not amenable to curative resection. Median overall survival in the advanced setting has improved only incrementally over two decades, and systemic cytotoxic chemotherapy continues to define the standard of care for most patients.

Two combination strategies dominate first-line practice. The first, gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel (GnP), pairs a nucleoside analogue with an albumin-bound taxane and was established on the basis of a survival gain over gemcitabine monotherapy. The second family of strategies is fluoropyrimidine-based: regimens in which a fluoropyrimidine backbone — S-1, capecitabine, or infusional 5-fluorouracil with leucovorin — is combined with gemcitabine, with nab-paclitaxel, or with both. These regimens are attractive for several reasons. Fluoropyrimidines have well-characterised single-agent activity in PDAC; oral agents such as S-1 and capecitabine reduce infusional burden and are widely used in East Asian practice; and fluoropyrimidine-containing triplets and doublets offer toxicity profiles that differ from, rather than simply exceed, those of GnP, potentially widening the population of patients who can be treated.

The clinical question is therefore not whether fluoropyrimidine-based combinations are active — they are — but whether, when benchmarked directly against gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel, they deliver a measurable overall survival advantage. Individual trials and comparative cohorts addressing this question have been modest in size, geographically concentrated, and heterogeneous in the specific fluoropyrimidine partner employed, which has left the field without a quantitative synthesis.

### 1.1 Objective

To systematically identify and quantitatively synthesise studies comparing fluoropyrimidine-based combination chemotherapy regimens (nab-paclitaxel or gemcitabine plus S-1, capecitabine, or 5-FU/leucovorin) with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel in adults with locally advanced or metastatic pancreatic cancer, with overall survival as the single prespecified outcome.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

This review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. The review protocol was not prospectively registered; this is acknowledged as a limitation (Section 10).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they satisfied all of the following, specified in PICO form:

- **Population (P).** Adults (≥18 years) with histologically or cytologically confirmed locally advanced or metastatic pancreatic cancer. Studies confined to resectable disease, adjuvant-only populations, or mixed gastrointestinal cancers without pancreatic-specific survival data were ineligible.
- **Intervention (I).** A fluoropyrimidine-based combination chemotherapy regimen, i.e. nab-paclitaxel or gemcitabine combined with S-1, capecitabine, or 5-FU/leucovorin.
- **Comparator (C).** Gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel.
- **Outcome (O).** Overall survival, defined as time from randomisation or treatment start to death from any cause, reported as the paper's own Cox proportional-hazards or other time-to-event estimate — specifically log[HR] with its standard error, or a reported ratio measure with 95% confidence interval. Progression-free survival, objective response rate, disease control rate, and grade 3/4 toxicity were **not** the target outcome and did not qualify a study for inclusion.
- **Design.** Randomised controlled trials and comparative (two-arm) observational or single-institution cohort studies reporting an adjusted or unadjusted time-to-event contrast between the two arms of interest.

Narrative reviews, editorials, single-arm studies, duplicate publications of the same patient series, and reports lacking an extractable time-to-event estimate for overall survival were excluded.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

A structured search of the biomedical and oncology literature was conducted to identify comparative studies of fluoropyrimidine-based combinations versus gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel in advanced pancreatic cancer. Search concepts combined (i) disease terms (pancreatic cancer, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, locally advanced, metastatic), (ii) intervention terms (S-1, TS-1, tegafur, capecitabine, 5-fluorouracil, leucovorin, fluoropyrimidine, gemcitabine, nab-paclitaxel, albumin-bound paclitaxel), and (iii) outcome terms (overall survival, hazard ratio, mortality). Reference lists of retrieved reports and relevant reviews were hand-searched. No language restriction was applied at the screening stage.

### 2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Records were screened against the eligibility criteria at title/abstract level and then at full text. For each included study, the following were extracted: author/study identifier, design, country/region, number of patients per arm, disease stage distribution, the precise composition of the fluoropyrimidine-based regimen, the comparator regimen as administered, and the reported overall survival effect estimate with its uncertainty. Where a paper reported a hazard ratio with a 95% confidence interval, log[HR] and its standard error were back-calculated from the interval bounds.

### 2.5 Effect measure

The effect measure for this synthesis was specified as a **Risk Ratio (RR)** by explicit user request, and pooling was carried out natively on that scale (`native_pooling_used = true`). It must be stated transparently that a measure conflict was recorded and resolved in favour of the user specification: **the source paper(s) explicitly report hazard ratios, whereas the user requested a risk ratio; the user request was retained as authoritative.** Readers should therefore interpret the pooled statistic as a ratio-scale mortality contrast, recognising that its underlying inputs are predominantly time-to-event (hazard) estimates. Ratio values below 1.00 correspond, on the raw scale, to a lower risk of death in the fluoropyrimidine-based arm.

Standardised effect sizes (Hedges' g) were additionally computed for each study for display and diagnostic purposes only; these are re-signed so that a positive g corresponds to better overall survival for the fluoropyrimidine-based regimen. The primary pooled inference reported in Section 5 is the Risk Ratio, not Hedges' g.

### 2.6 Synthesis methods

Effects were pooled using a random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), which does not assume a single common underlying effect and is appropriate given the anticipated clinical diversity of fluoropyrimidine partners and study designs. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q (with its p-value), the I² statistic (proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity), and τ² (the absolute between-study variance on the analysis scale). Cumulative meta-analysis was performed to visualise the stability of the pooled estimate as evidence accrued.

### 2.7 Small-study effects and publication bias

Funnel-plot asymmetry was assessed using Egger's regression test of the intercept, with the conventional p < 0.10 threshold applied given the low power of the test with few studies. The Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure was applied as a sensitivity analysis to estimate the number of potentially missing studies (k₀) and the correspondingly adjusted pooled effect.

## 3. Search and Screening Results

The screening and synthesis pipeline identified **six studies** that reached the evidence-synthesis stage. Of these, **five** yielded an extractable, non-overlapping overall survival effect estimate suitable for quantitative pooling; one study progressed through screening but did not contribute a usable effect size for the target outcome (it did not supply an extractable time-to-event estimate for overall survival meeting the extraction rule described in Section 2.2/2.4) and was therefore carried forward in the narrative synthesis only.

The analytic dataset thus comprises **k = 5 effect sizes** representing **N = 1,245 patients**. The PRISMA flow of records — identification, screening, eligibility assessment, and inclusion — is summarised numerically above; no separate flow diagram file was generated by the analysis pipeline.

Several structural features of this evidence base warrant emphasis before the pooled result is interpreted:

1. **One study dominates.** study_4_R4 contributes 770 of 1,245 patients (61.8%) and has by far the tightest confidence interval (half-width ≈0.14 on the g scale, versus ≈0.31–0.43 for the other four). Under inverse-variance weighting it will drive the pooled estimate, and any sensitivity analysis omitting it would materially widen the interval.
2. **Four studies are small.** The remaining studies enrol 83–157 patients each. All four have confidence intervals that comfortably include zero on the standardised scale, i.e. none is individually conclusive.
3. **Directional consistency.** All five study-level estimates sit on the same side of the null on the raw scale (all g values negative, corresponding to ratio estimates below 1), ranging from −0.0457 to −0.2790. This consistency of sign, in the absence of any individually significant study, is characteristic of a small, directionally coherent but underpowered literature.
4. **Clinical heterogeneity of the experimental arm.** The "fluoropyrimidine-based combination" category is deliberately broad, encompassing S-1-, capecitabine-, and 5-FU/leucovorin-containing doublets and triplets built on either a gemcitabine or a nab-paclitaxel scaffold. These are not pharmacologically interchangeable, and the pooled estimate should be read as an average across a family of regimens rather than as an estimate for any single protocol.

The pooled Risk Ratio of 0.873 lies below 1.00, and the upper bound of the 95% confidence interval (1.003) only marginally exceeds the line of no effect, with a two-sided p-value of 0.0556 that falls just short of the conventional 0.05 threshold. **Per the prespecified direction field, this result favours neither arm**: the synthesis is reported as essentially a tie, with the caveat that for a ratio measure the favourable direction depends on which event was counted. We therefore do **not** characterise this as a demonstrated survival advantage for fluoropyrimidine-based combinations. The honest summary is that the data are statistically compatible both with a modest ~13% relative reduction in the mortality contrast and with no difference at all, and that the confidence interval includes the null.

Because the effect measure was set by explicit user request while the underlying papers report hazard ratios (Section 2.5), the ratio reported here should be interpreted with that provenance in mind. Pooling was nonetheless performed natively on the requested RR scale, so the value is a native-scale pooled result rather than a standardized fallback.

## 6. Heterogeneity

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| I² | **59.18%** | Moderate heterogeneity; roughly three-fifths of observed variability exceeds that expected from sampling error alone |
| τ² | **0.011731** | Between-study variance on the analysis scale; modest in absolute terms |
| Cochran's Q | **7.829** (df = 4) | — |
| Q p-value | **0.0981** | Not significant at α = 0.05; marginal at α = 0.10 |

The two heterogeneity indicators point in slightly different directions, and this tension is worth stating plainly rather than resolving by fiat. I² of 59.18% falls in the conventionally "moderate" band and would ordinarily prompt caution about pooling. Cochran's Q, however, does not reach significance at α = 0.05 (p = 0.0981), and with only five studies Q is notoriously underpowered — a non-significant Q cannot be read as evidence of homogeneity. Conversely, I² is estimated with very wide uncertainty when k = 5 and can be inflated by a single highly precise study sitting apart from a cluster of imprecise ones, which is structurally what this dataset contains.

The small absolute value of τ² (0.011731) is reassuring in context: the dispersion, while a substantial *fraction* of total variability, is small in *magnitude* on the ratio scale. All five study-level estimates lie on the same side of the null, and the spread between the most and least favourable (g = −0.2790 to −0.0457) is clinically narrow. This is heterogeneity of degree, not of direction — none of the studies points toward superiority of gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel. The random-effects model was the appropriate specification and already incorporates this dispersion into the width of the reported confidence interval.

Plausible sources of the observed heterogeneity include: the differing fluoropyrimidine partner (S-1 vs capecitabine vs 5-FU/leucovorin) and backbone (gemcitabine vs nab-paclitaxel); differences in the proportion of locally advanced versus metastatic patients; regional differences in pharmacogenomics and supportive care, particularly relevant for S-1, whose tolerability profile differs between East Asian and Western populations; variation in performance status thresholds; and design heterogeneity between randomised and comparative cohort evidence, with attendant differences in confounding control.

## 7. Moderator Analyses

No formal moderator (meta-regression or subgroup) analyses were conducted, and none should be. With k = 5 studies the degrees of freedom available for covariate modelling are negligible; any meta-regression would be overfitted, and subgroup contrasts — for example S-1-containing versus capecitabine-containing regimens, or randomised versus observational designs — would rest on one or two studies per cell and would be uninterpretable.

The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) permits informal visual inspection of the relationship between study size/precision and effect magnitude. Descriptively, the four smaller studies (N = 83–157) report numerically larger standardised effects (g = −0.2148, −0.0457, −0.2707, −0.2790; three of four at or beyond −0.21) than the single large study (N = 770, g = −0.1026). Such a pattern is consistent with a small-study effect, but with four small studies and one large one it is equally consistent with chance, and it is formally indistinguishable from genuine clinical differences between the regimens evaluated in smaller versus larger programmes. It is noted here as a hypothesis-generating observation only and is taken up again in Section 8.

Prespecified moderators that would merit investigation in a larger future synthesis include: fluoropyrimidine agent, chemotherapy backbone, disease extent (locally advanced vs metastatic), study region, study design, line of therapy, and baseline performance status.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

| Assessment | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | **−0.0395** |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.4013** |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **2** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted effect | **−0.1127** (log scale; ≈0.893 on the ratio scale) |
| Overall risk rating | **Moderate** |

Egger's regression test shows no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.4013, well above the conventional 0.10 threshold applied to this test), so there is no strong statistical evidence of small-study effects. However, the trim-and-fill procedure imputed two potentially missing studies (k₀ = 2), indicating some mild asymmetry on the funnel plot that the regression test was not powered to detect — a common situation when the number of included studies is small.

Critically, the trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (−0.1127 on the log scale, corresponding to approximately 0.893 on the ratio scale) remains in the same direction and of essentially the same magnitude as the unadjusted pooled estimate of 0.873. The adjustment attenuates the effect only slightly and does not change the qualitative conclusion: the interval continues to be compatible with no difference.

**Bottom line.** The "moderate" risk rating is appropriate and reflects residual uncertainty rather than demonstrated bias. The pooled effect should be reported alongside the trim-and-fill adjusted estimate as a sensitivity analysis, and because the adjusted estimate remains in the same direction and magnitude, the conclusions can be considered reasonably robust to publication bias. That said, with k = 5 all funnel-based diagnostics are of limited diagnostic value, and the absence of detected asymmetry should not be mistaken for the absence of selective reporting — particularly in an oncology literature where negative comparative cohorts are less likely to be written up and where regional publication practices may differ across the S-1, capecitabine, and 5-FU literatures.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Principal findings

Pooling five comparative studies comprising 1,245 adults with locally advanced or metastatic pancreatic cancer, fluoropyrimidine-based combination chemotherapy was associated with a pooled Risk Ratio for overall survival of 0.873 (95% CI 0.760–1.003; p = 0.0556). The direction field for this synthesis records the result as favouring **neither arm** — essentially a tie — and the confidence interval crosses the null. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 59.18%; τ² = 0.011731), and the result was robust to trim-and-fill adjustment for potential small-study effects.

The most defensible reading is one of **statistical equipoise with a non-significant directional signal**. Every contributing study points the same way on the raw scale, and the pooled point estimate corresponds to a ~13% relative reduction in the mortality contrast — a magnitude that, if real, would be clinically meaningful in a disease where absolute survival gains are measured in weeks. But the evidence does not cross the threshold required to claim superiority: p = 0.0556, and the upper confidence bound of 1.003 explicitly admits no difference. A result of this shape is exactly what one expects from either (a) a true modest benefit studied with insufficient aggregate power, or (b) no true difference combined with mild small-study optimism. The present data cannot adjudicate between these.

### 9.2 Relation to the clinical context

The finding is consistent with the broader clinical impression that the leading first-line combination strategies in advanced PDAC deliver broadly comparable survival, with differences lying more in toxicity profile, route of administration, and patient-level tolerability than in overall survival. Gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel remains a well-validated standard, and these data do not displace it. Equally, they provide no basis for regarding fluoropyrimidine-based combinations as inferior: the lower confidence bound of 0.760 means a substantial survival benefit for the fluoropyrimidine strategy remains statistically compatible with the data, while the upper bound of 1.003 all but excludes meaningful harm.

For practice, this supports regimen selection on grounds other than projected overall survival — notably toxicity, comorbidity, performance status, oral versus intravenous administration preference, and regional availability and pharmacogenomic suitability (particularly relevant for S-1). It should be stressed that toxicity, progression-free survival, objective response, and disease control were deliberately outside the scope of this review; a regimen-selection decision requires that evidence alongside the survival evidence synthesised here.

### 9.3 Interpretation of the measure

A methodological caveat conditions all of the above. The effect measure was fixed as a Risk Ratio at explicit user request, while the underlying report(s) present hazard ratios; the conflict was resolved in favour of the user specification, which was treated as authoritative. Pooling was executed natively on the RR scale, so the reported figure is a genuine native-scale pooled ratio rather than a standardized approximation. Nevertheless, risk ratios and hazard ratios are not numerically identical quantities — the former summarises cumulative risk over a defined interval, the latter an instantaneous rate ratio over follow-up — and in a disease with high event rates such as advanced PDAC the two can diverge appreciably. Readers transporting this estimate into other analyses should be explicit about which quantity they intend.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No formal validation flags were returned by the analysis pipeline for this synthesis. For completeness and transparency, the following data-handling facts arising from the pipeline record are stated plainly:

- **Six studies reached the synthesis stage, but only five contributed effect sizes.** One study did not yield an extractable time-to-event estimate for overall survival meeting the prespecified extraction rule and so contributes to the narrative synthesis only, not to the pooled estimate. The analytic k is therefore 5, not 6.
- **An effect-measure conflict was recorded and resolved.** The user explicitly requested a Risk Ratio; the source paper(s) explicitly report hazard ratios. The user request was retained as authoritative. Pooling was performed natively on the RR scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the headline figure is not a standardized fallback — but the measure is not the one the primary reports used, and this discrepancy is material to interpretation.
- **Only one outcome group was analysed.** No multiple outcome/timepoint groups were present, so a single pooled estimate is reported; no groups were marked as having insufficient data.
- **Standardised effect sizes (Hedges' g) are reported for description only.** The study-level g values in Table 1 retain the raw treatment-minus-control sign convention and must not be confused with the primary pooled Risk Ratio.
- **One study dominates the weighting.** study_4_R4 contributes 61.8% of pooled patients; no leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was executed by the pipeline, so the dependence of the pooled estimate on that single study has not been quantified.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Few studies.** With k = 5, every downstream statistic — I², τ², Egger's intercept, trim-and-fill — is estimated with substantial imprecision. The synthesis is underpowered for heterogeneity assessment and for bias detection alike.
2. **Borderline inference.** The pooled result (p = 0.0556; upper CI bound 1.003) sits immediately adjacent to the conventional significance threshold. Small changes in the included set, the weighting model, or the extraction of any single estimate could move it across that boundary in either direction, and no leave-one-out analysis was performed to test this.
3. **Dominance of a single study.** Over 60% of pooled patients derive from one study; the pooled estimate is, in practical terms, that study's estimate modestly adjusted by four imprecise neighbours.
4. **Clinical heterogeneity of the intervention.** "Fluoropyrimidine-based combination" aggregates S-1-, capecitabine-, and 5-FU/leucovorin-containing regimens on gemcitabine or nab-paclitaxel backbones. These differ in pharmacology, toxicity, and regional applicability; the pooled estimate is an average over a non-exchangeable family of treatments.
5. **Measure mismatch.** The requested Risk Ratio differs conceptually from the hazard ratios reported in the source literature (Sections 2.5, 9.3, 10).
6. **Moderate heterogeneity unexplained.** I² = 59.18% was not decomposed; with k = 5, moderator and subgroup analyses were not feasible, so the drivers of dispersion remain unidentified.
7. **Mixed designs and residual confounding.** Where comparative cohort evidence contributes, allocation was not randomised and residual confounding by performance status, disease burden, and treatment era cannot be excluded.
8. **Possible small-study effects.** Trim-and-fill imputed two studies (k₀ = 2); although the adjusted estimate was materially unchanged, the overall publication-bias risk is rated moderate and selective reporting cannot be excluded.
9. **No protocol registration.** The absence of a prospectively registered protocol raises the possibility of post hoc flexibility in eligibility and analysis decisions.
10. **Single outcome, no certainty grading.** Progression-free survival, response, disease control, and toxicity were out of scope by design, so this review cannot support a net benefit–harm judgement. No formal GRADE assessment of certainty was undertaken; informally, the certainty of evidence for overall survival would be rated low, downgraded for imprecision and inconsistency.

---

## 12. Conclusion

In adults with locally advanced or metastatic pancreatic cancer, this meta-analysis of five comparative studies (N = 1,245) found a pooled Risk Ratio for overall survival of **0.873 (95% CI 0.760–1.003; p = 0.0556)** for fluoropyrimidine-based combination chemotherapy versus gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel. Consistent with the prespecified direction field, this result **favours neither arm** and is best described as essentially a tie, with a confidence interval that includes no difference. Heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 59.18%, τ² = 0.011731, Q = 7.829, p = 0.0981), and the conclusion was robust to trim-and-fill adjustment (adjusted estimate −0.1127 on the log scale, ≈0.893 on the ratio scale), with publication-bias risk rated moderate.

Fluoropyrimidine-based combinations are therefore neither established as superior nor shown to be inferior to gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel with respect to overall survival. All five contributing estimates lie on the same side of the null, which keeps open the possibility of a modest real benefit, but the aggregate evidence is too sparse and too imprecise to support a change in practice on survival grounds. Regimen choice in this setting should continue to be individualised on the basis of toxicity profile, comorbidity, performance status, administration route, and regional considerations. Adequately powered randomised trials that compare specific fluoropyrimidine-based regimens head-to-head with gemcitabine plus nab-paclitaxel — reporting hazard ratios with full survival curves, stratified by disease extent and region, and accompanied by complete toxicity and quality-of-life data — are required to resolve the question.
