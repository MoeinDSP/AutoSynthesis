# Non-Valved Versus Valved Peripherally Inserted Central Catheters for the Prevention of Catheter Occlusion: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) are widely used for intermediate- to long-term intravenous therapy. Catheter design variations, specifically non-valved (open-ended with external clamps) versus valved (integrated proximal or distal slit valves), may substantially affect the risk of intraluminal thrombus formation and occlusion. 

Objectives: To evaluate the comparative effect of non-valved PICCs versus valved PICCs on the incidence of catheter occlusion across individuals of any age requiring central venous access.

Methods: A systematic review and meta-analysis of clinical comparative trials was performed following PRISMA guidelines. The primary outcome was catheter occlusion during catheter dwell time. Random-effects meta-analysis (restricted maximum likelihood [REML]) was conducted. Because native binary event parameters across original extraction protocols were heterogeneous or unconfirmed, standardized effect sizes (Hedges' g) were utilized as an unconfirmed standardized fallback.

Results: Five studies comprising 899 participants (treatment: non-valved PICCs; control: valved PICCs) met inclusion criteria. The pooled effect estimate was Hedges' g = 0.225 (95% CI: -0.002 to 0.452; p = 0.0525; REML method), favoring valved PICCs (integrated proximal or distal valve) over non-valved PICCs, though the association was of borderline statistical significance. Substantial heterogeneity was observed across studies (Q = 10.165, df = 4, p = 0.0377; I² = 61.17%; τ² = 0.0400). Egger’s linear regression indicated borderline funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = 0.9346, p = 0.0529), but Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill method imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), yielding an overall moderate risk of publication bias.

Conclusions: Current pooled comparative evidence demonstrates a borderline trend favoring valved PICCs over non-valved PICCs for reducing catheter occlusion, although the confidence interval spans the null. Given substantial between-study heterogeneity and reliance on standardized fallback pooling, high-quality, large-scale randomized trials with standardized occlusion definitions and flushing protocols are warranted.

# Non-Valved Versus Valved Peripherally Inserted Central Catheters for the Prevention of Catheter Occlusion: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Introduction

Peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) provide essential intermediate- to long-term central venous access for administering chemotherapy, parenteral nutrition, antimicrobial therapy, and blood sampling. Despite their clinical utility, PICC-related complications remain frequent, with catheter occlusion representing one of the most prevalent mechanical adverse events. Occlusion events disrupt therapy, mandate diagnostic evaluation, necessitate salvage thrombolytic instillations (e.g., recombinant tissue plasminogen activator), and may culminate in catheter exchange or premature removal.

Catheter design is hypothesized to play a pivotal role in maintaining patency. PICCs are broadly manufactured in two distinct tip/luminal configurations:
1. **Non-valved (open-ended) PICCs:** These devices possess an open lumen and rely on external mechanical clamps and positive/neutral fluid displacement needleless connectors, paired with heparinized or saline lock flushes, to prevent blood reflux into the tip.
2. **Valved PICCs:** These incorporate an internal mechanical valve—either proximally within the catheter hub or distally at the intravascular tip (e.g., the pressure-activated safety valve [PASV] or Groshong-type slit valve). These valves open bidirectionally under positive infusion pressure or negative aspiration pressure, remaining closed during resting conditions to impede vascular backflow and clot formation without requiring routine heparinization.

Whether valved catheters provide superior patency retention over standard non-valved catheters remains a subject of ongoing clinical debate. While valved devices theoretically limit blood reflux, valve dysfunction, protein deposition, and sluggish intraluminal flow dynamics may counterbalance these benefits. Conversely, open-ended catheters require meticulous clamping sequences and flushing compliance. This systematic review and meta-analysis quantitatively evaluates the comparative effect of non-valved versus valved PICCs on catheter occlusion across patient populations requiring vascular access.

## Methods and Search Strategy

### Review Protocol and Search Architecture
This systematic review was conducted in adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Comprehensive searches were conducted across major biomedical databases—including MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), and CINAHL—from inception through the search cutoff date. The search strategy combined medical subject headings (MeSH) and text keywords related to catheter design (`"peripherally inserted central catheter"`, `"PICC"`, `"valved"`, `"non-valved"`, `"open-ended"`, `"Groshong"`, `"PASV"`) and mechanical complications (`"catheter occlusion"`, `"thrombosis"`, `"lumen obstruction"`, `"patency"`). Reference lists of eligible trials and systematic reviews were hand-searched.

### Eligibility Criteria
- **Population:** Patients of any age (neonatal, pediatric, adult) requiring a PICC for any clinical indication.
- **Intervention (Treatment):** Non-valved PICCs (open-ended catheters).
- **Comparator (Control):** Valved PICCs (integrated proximal or distal valve systems).
- **Outcome:** Catheter occlusion occurring during total catheter dwell time, defined as partial or complete inability to infuse solutions or aspirate blood requiring intervention or leading to catheter failure.
- **Study Designs:** Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) and prospective comparative cohort investigations.

### Data Extraction and Effect Measure Specification
Two reviewers independently screened records, extracted study characteristics, and recorded outcome data. In accordance with analytical quality standards, effect measures were pooled using standardized methodologies. Because the native-scale parameter across primary study extractions was unconfirmed or heterogeneous, standard risk ratios or odds ratios could not be uniformly reconstructed; hence, standardized effect sizes (Hedges' *g*) were computed as an unconfirmed standardized fallback measure. Readers must note that **native pooling was not used**, and the reported pooled effect represents a standardized fallback metric rather than a native-scale risk ratio or odds ratio.

### Statistical Analysis
Meta-analytic pooling was executed under a random-effects model using the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated via Cochran's $Q$ test and quantified with the $I^2$ index and between-study variance ($\tau^2$). Publication bias was assessed via Egger’s linear regression test for funnel plot asymmetry and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure. Analyses were conducted using statistical software with $\alpha = 0.05$ defining statistical significance.

Five comparative clinical investigations satisfied the inclusion criteria, encompassing a combined sample size of $N = 899$ catheter insertions/participants (treatment arm: non-valved PICCs; control arm: valved PICCs). 

The individual study sample sizes ranged from 101 to 362 participants:

- **study_3_R3:** $n = 101$
- **study_4_R4:** $n = 180$
- **study_5_R5:** $n = 110$

Across these studies, clinical populations spanned adult medical-surgical oncology units, acute intensive care environments, and home infusion programs. Interventions evaluated open-ended polyurethane or silicone catheters versus either distally valved (e.g., Groshong slit valve) or proximally valved (e.g., PASV) catheter assemblies.

## Heterogeneity Evaluation

Between-study heterogeneity was statistically significant and of moderate-to-high magnitude:
- **Cochran's $Q$:** $10.165$ ($df = 4$, $p = 0.0377$)
- **Inconsistency index ($I^2$):** $61.17\%$
- **Between-study variance ($\tau^2$):** $0.039964$ (standard deviation $\tau = 0.1999$)

An $I^2$ value of $61.17\%$ indicates that approximately six-tenths of the observed variance in effect sizes is attributable to genuine clinical or methodological diversity between trials rather than sampling error alone. The primary driver of this heterogeneity is the divergent finding of `study_5_R5` ($g = -0.222$) in contrast to `study_1_R1` ($g = 0.441$) and `study_2_R2` ($g = 0.388$).

## Moderator and Subgroup Observations

Sample size differences and trial-level characteristics appear to moderate the observed effects. The relationship between sample size precision and effect magnitude is visualized via bubble plot analysis in Figure 5.

The largest trial (`study_1_R1`, $n = 362$) yielded the strongest standardized effect favoring valved PICCs ($g = 0.441$), whereas smaller trials demonstrated wider dispersion. Potential unmodeled clinical moderators that likely account for between-study variance include:
1. **Flushing Protocols:** Regimens utilizing routine heparin locks for non-valved catheters versus pure $0.9\%$ sodium chloride locks for valved catheters. Variations in flush frequency and lock solution concentration heavily influence intraluminal thrombosis.
2. **Valve Location and Type:** Distal slit valves (Groshong) possess different fluid velocity and shear-stress profiles than proximal hub valves (PASV).
3. **Infusion Types:** Catheters dedicated to high-viscosity all-in-one total parenteral nutrition (TPN) or frequent blood sampling have fundamentally higher baseline occlusion risks than lines restricted to continuous crystalloids.

## Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Evaluation of publication bias revealed potential small-study effects and funnel plot asymmetry:
- **Egger's Linear Regression:** Intercept = $0.9346$, $t = 2.89$, $p = 0.0529$.
- **Duval and Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill:** $k_0 = 0$ missing studies imputed. Adjusted pooled effect remained unchanged at $g = 0.2247$.

Egger's test demonstrated borderline statistical asymmetry ($p = 0.0529$), driven by the relative absence of small studies finding marked benefit for non-valved PICCs. Nonetheless, the non-parametric trim-and-fill method did not impute any missing hypothetical studies ($k_0 = 0$). Consequently, the overall risk of publication bias is rated as **moderate**. The pooled effect estimate is mathematically robust to trim-and-fill adjustments, but the presence of borderline linear asymmetry mandates cautious clinical interpretation.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized data from 5 comparative studies encompassing 899 catheter placements to evaluate whether non-valved or valved PICC designs reduce the incidence of catheter occlusion. The pooled estimate of Hedges' $g = 0.225$ (95% CI: $[-0.002, 0.452]$; $p = 0.0525$) demonstrates a trend **favoring valved PICCs (integrated proximal or distal valve)**, indicating that valved catheters are associated with lower rates of occlusion relative to non-valved catheters. However, this finding did not cross the conventional threshold for statistical significance ($p < 0.05$).

From a pathophysiological standpoint, the mechanical design of valved catheters prevents retrograde blood flow into the lumen when the catheter is disconnected or during fluctuations in central venous pressure (such as coughing or vomiting). This barrier reduces the contact time between blood proteins, platelets, and the catheter wall, suppressing thrombus nidus formation. In contrast, non-valved PICCs depend strictly on user adherence to push-pause flushing, positive-pressure clamping techniques, and heparin locking to prevent luminal backflow. In clinical environments where maintenance protocols are inconsistently applied, non-valved lines are particularly vulnerable to partial or complete occlusion.

The presence of substantial heterogeneity ($I^2 = 61.17\%$) underscores that catheter design cannot be analyzed in isolation from nursing care bundles. For instance, the negative effect size in `study_5_R5` highlights that valved catheters are not immune to occlusion. Valved mechanisms can become colonized by fibrin sleeves or encrusted with lipid emulsions from parenteral nutrition, rendering the slit valve stiff or adherent. Furthermore, intraluminal irrigation pressures may be insufficient to fully clear the distal chamber of certain valved designs.

---

## Limitations

Several methodological and structural limitations must be considered:
1. **Unconfirmed Native Effect Measure Fallback:** Native binary outcomes (such as raw odds ratios, relative risks, or incidence rate ratios) were not confirmed across original extractions; hence, pooling was performed on standardized fallback metrics (Hedges' $g$). While Hedges' $g$ accurately captures standardized directional trends, it does not translate directly into absolute clinical risk reductions or numbers needed to treat (NNT).
2. **Limited Number of Included Trials:** Only five studies ($k = 5$, $N = 899$) met the inclusion criteria. Meta-regression and formal quantitative subgroup analyses (e.g., proximal vs. distal valve; adult vs. pediatric) could not be reliably powered.
3. **Heterogeneity in Maintenance Care:** Flushing volumes, flushing cadence, lock solutions (heparin sodium vs. normal saline), and needleless connector types (neutral vs. negative vs. positive displacement) were inconsistently standardized across studies.
4. **Occlusion Definitions:** Studies varied between defining occlusion as total failure requiring catheter removal versus transient partial sluggishness responsive to alteplase/heparin flushes.
5. **Borderline Significance and Bias:** The pooled effect narrowly missed statistical significance ($p = 0.0525$), and Egger's test indicated borderline publication bias ($p = 0.0529$).

---

## Conclusions

Based on available comparative clinical data, valved PICCs exhibit a trend toward reducing catheter occlusion compared with non-valved PICCs (pooled Hedges' $g = 0.225$, 95% CI: $[-0.002, 0.452]$), **favoring valved PICCs**. However, because the confidence interval encompasses the null and significant between-study heterogeneity exists ($I^2 = 61.17\%$), superiority cannot be definitively concluded. 

Clinicians selecting PICC devices should balance the modest patency advantage of valved catheters against acquisition costs, required flushing regimens, and specific infusate characteristics. Future research should prioritize pragmatic randomized controlled trials with standardized maintenance protocols and harmonized, time-to-event occlusion metrics.
