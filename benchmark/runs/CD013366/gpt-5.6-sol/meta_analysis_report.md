# Catheter Occlusion With Non-Valved Versus Integrated-Valve Peripherally Inserted Central Catheters: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

## Background
Integrated proximal or distal valve technology may influence the risk of occlusion during peripherally inserted central catheter (PICC) dwell. This review evaluated catheter occlusion with PICCs without a valve versus PICCs incorporating integrated valve technology.

## Methods
Eligible studies enrolled people of any age requiring a PICC and compared non-valved PICCs with proximal- or distal-valved PICCs. The outcome was the occurrence of at least one catheter occlusion during dwell, using each study’s stated unit of analysis. Five studies were included, but only four effect estimates (total *n* = 753) were available for quantitative synthesis. A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. The supplied analysis could not confirm a native dichotomous effect measure; consequently, standardized fallback estimates labeled Hedges’ *g* were pooled. Publication bias or small-study effects were examined with Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

## Results
The four individual Hedges’ *g* estimates were 0.441435 (95% CI 0.233 to 0.650), 0.129151 (95% CI −0.284 to 0.542), 0.119360 (95% CI −0.192 to 0.431), and −0.221989 (95% CI −0.597 to 0.153). The pooled Hedges’ *g* was 0.14460296192064892 (95% CI −0.137 to 0.426; *p* = 0.3143631377579243; *k* = 4; *n* = 753), and the supplied analysis classified the result as favoring PICCs with integrated valve technology. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 68.46%; τ² = 0.055176), with significant Cochran’s Q = 10.246 (*p* = 0.0166). Egger’s intercept was 0.959 (*p* = 0.0079). Trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study and produced an adjusted Hedges’ *g* of 0.2529. Publication-bias risk was classified as high.

## Conclusions
The analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in catheter occlusion between non-valved and integrated-valve PICCs. Interpretation is materially constrained by substantial heterogeneity, funnel-plot asymmetry, the small number of studies, and use of an unconfirmed standardized fallback rather than a clinically interpretable native dichotomous effect measure. Event-count reanalysis using a risk ratio or odds ratio is required before firm clinical conclusions can be drawn.

# Introduction

Peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) provide medium- to long-term venous access across diverse populations and clinical settings. Catheter occlusion is a clinically important complication because it may interrupt treatment, require thrombolytic or mechanical intervention, and prompt catheter replacement. PICCs may have no integrated valve or may incorporate proximal or distal valve technology intended to control blood reflux and catheter flow. Whether these designs differ in their risk of occlusion during catheter dwell remains uncertain.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among people of any age requiring a PICC, what is the effect of a PICC without a valve, compared with a PICC incorporating proximal or distal integrated valve technology, on the occurrence of catheter occlusion during catheter dwell time?

The prespecified treatment was a PICC without a valve, and the comparator was a PICC with proximal or distal integrated valve technology. The intended outcome was dichotomous: the number of participants or catheters experiencing at least one occlusion during dwell and the corresponding number assessed, retaining each study’s stated analysis unit. Catheter failure, venous thromboembolism, infection, breakage, and other catheter outcomes were outside the scope of this synthesis.

A random-effects model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The confidence interval crossed the null, and the test of the pooled effect was not statistically significant.

The supplied analysis classified the pooled result as favoring **PICCs with integrated valve technology (proximal or distal valve)**. Nevertheless, the estimate does not establish a difference between catheter designs. # Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- Cochran’s Q = **10.246**;
- Q-test *p* = **0.0166**;
- I² = **68.46%**; and
- τ² = **0.055176**.

The significant Q test indicates that the dispersion of estimates exceeded that expected from sampling error alone under a common-effect assumption. I² suggests that a substantial proportion of observed variability was attributable to between-study differences rather than sampling error. The τ² estimate describes the between-study variance on the fallback Hedges’ *g* scale and therefore lacks direct interpretation as a difference in occlusion risk.

Potential sources of heterogeneity could include age group, clinical setting, catheter design and brand, proximal versus distal valve location, insertion and maintenance protocols, flushing practices, dwell time, and definitions of occlusion. These explanations remain hypotheses because the corresponding study-level data were not supplied. # Publication Bias

Egger’s intercept was **0.959**, with *p* = **0.0079**, indicating statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry. Trim-and-fill estimated **one** potentially missing study and yielded an adjusted Hedges’ *g* of **0.2529**. The pipeline classified publication-bias risk as **high**.

These results are consistent with possible publication bias or small-study effects, but they are not specific to publication bias. Heterogeneity, selective outcome reporting, methodological differences, and chance can also produce asymmetry. In addition, formal asymmetry tests are generally unreliable with very few studies; only four estimates were available here. Trim-and-fill relies on assumptions that may be violated in the presence of substantial heterogeneity and should be treated as a sensitivity analysis rather than a corrected primary estimate. The adjusted value remains on the same unconfirmed fallback scale, and no confidence interval or *p* value for the adjusted estimate was supplied.

# Discussion

## Principal findings

This synthesis of four study estimates found no statistically significant difference in catheter occlusion between non-valved PICCs and PICCs with integrated proximal or distal valves. The pooled Hedges’ *g* was 0.14460296192064892 (95% CI −0.137 to 0.426; *p* = 0.3143631377579243), and the supplied direction field classified the estimate as favoring integrated-valve PICCs. The confidence interval included the null, heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 68.46%), and publication-bias risk was classified as high.

The evidence therefore does not show a reliable benefit of non-valved PICCs for preventing occlusion during dwell. It also does not establish a benefit of integrated-valve PICCs: statistical non-significance is not evidence of equivalence, and the interval estimate remains compatible with effects in either direction on the reported standardized scale.

## Clinical interpretation

Clinical interpretation is limited principally by the absence of native dichotomous effect estimates. Catheter occlusion is naturally represented by the proportion of participants or catheters experiencing at least one event. Without the numbers of occlusions and denominators by arm, the pooled standardized effect cannot be translated into relative or absolute risk. Therefore, the magnitude of any potential clinical benefit or harm remains unknown.

The substantial heterogeneity further suggests that a single average estimate may not apply uniformly across populations and catheter systems. Proximal and distal valves may differ mechanistically, and maintenance practices may interact with catheter design. Combining both valve types into one comparator is consistent with the stated question, but adequately powered subgroup analyses would be useful if enough studies and complete event data became available.

## Implications for practice and research

On the current evidence, catheter selection should not be based on a presumed occlusion advantage derived from this pooled estimate alone. Decisions should also consider device availability, clinician experience, maintenance requirements, patient characteristics, cost, and evidence for other outcomes—while recognizing that other outcomes were not part of this analysis.

Future evidence syntheses should retrieve arm-level occlusion events and denominators, record whether the unit is the participant or catheter, and distinguish first occlusion from recurrent-event analyses. Investigators should use explicit, standardized definitions of partial and complete occlusion; report dwell time and censoring; distinguish proximal from distal valve technology; and provide results suitable for pooling as risk ratios or another prespecified native dichotomous measure. Prospective protocol registration and complete outcome reporting would reduce risks of selective analysis.

# Limitations

This report has several important limitations.

First, the meta-analysis did not use a confirmed native dichotomous effect measure. Hedges’ *g* is not the preferred measure for an event outcome and does not directly communicate the probability of occlusion. Second, event counts, arm-specific denominators, and analysis units were unavailable, so the calculations and outcome classification could not be independently verified. Third, the evidence base was small: only four studies and 753 observations contributed to the pooled estimate. Fourth, heterogeneity was substantial and its causes could not be examined because study characteristics and valid moderator analyses were unavailable.

Fifth, significant Egger’s-test asymmetry and the trim-and-fill result raised concern about small-study effects, but both methods are unstable with four studies. Sixth, no study-level risk-of-bias information was provided, precluding a formal assessment of bias arising from randomization, allocation concealment, missing data, outcome measurement, or selective reporting. Seventh, the absence of a reproducible search strategy and PRISMA screening counts prevents assessment of search completeness. Finally, no bibliographic information was available, limiting appraisal of overlapping cohorts, multiple reports, and study context.

Because of these limitations, certainty in the pooled result is low and no formal GRADE rating can be completed from the supplied information.

# Conclusion

Across four studies totaling 753 analyzed observations, the REML random-effects pooled Hedges’ *g* was 0.14460296192064892 (95% CI −0.137 to 0.426; *p* = 0.3143631377579243), classified by the supplied analysis as favoring PICCs with integrated valve technology. The result was not statistically significant. Substantial heterogeneity (I² = 68.46%; τ² = 0.055176; Q = 10.246, *p* = 0.0166) and evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger’s intercept 0.959, *p* = 0.0079) further weaken confidence.

No firm conclusion can be drawn that either non-valved or integrated-valve PICCs reduce catheter occlusion during dwell. Reanalysis using verified arm-level occlusion events and a native dichotomous measure—preferably a prespecified risk ratio—is necessary for clinically interpretable inference.
