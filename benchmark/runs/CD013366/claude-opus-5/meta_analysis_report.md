# Non-valved versus valved peripherally inserted central catheters and catheter occlusion during dwell time: a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) are widely used for medium- to long-term intravenous access. Integrated valve technology (proximal or distal valves) was introduced partly to reduce blood reflux into the catheter lumen and thereby lower the risk of occlusion, but whether valved devices actually outperform conventional open-ended (non-valved) PICCs on this outcome remains contested.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of non-valved (open-ended) PICCs compared with PICCs incorporating integrated proximal or distal valve technology on catheter occlusion occurring during catheter dwell time, in people of any age.

**Methods.** A systematic search of comparative studies (randomised and non-randomised) of non-valved versus valved PICCs was conducted; records were screened in duplicate against pre-specified eligibility criteria, and data were extracted as dichotomous events (participants/catheters with partial or complete occlusion, withdrawal occlusion, lumen blockage, occlusion requiring thrombolytic or declotting therapy, or occlusion leading to catheter removal) per arm. Where more than one valved arm was reported, valved arms were combined into a single comparator. Effect sizes were pooled using a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The native effect measure for the pooled analysis could not be confirmed from the extracted records, so pooling was performed on a standardized (Hedges' g) scale as a fallback. Heterogeneity was summarised with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression test and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Five studies contributing five effect estimates (N = 899 participants/catheters) were included. Individual standardized effects ranged from −0.222 (95% CI −0.597 to 0.153) to 0.714 (95% CI 0.380 to 1.049). The pooled Hedges' g was 0.250 (95% CI −0.056 to 0.556; p = 0.1092), which did not reach conventional statistical significance; per the pre-specified direction metadata this pooled estimate favours the valved PICC comparator. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (Q = 17.047, df = 4, p = 0.0019; I² = 78.56%; τ² = 0.093687). Egger's test showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = 0.9048, p = 0.3731) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted g = 0.2502), giving an overall low rated risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** Across five comparative studies, there was no statistically significant difference between non-valved and valved PICCs in the risk of catheter occlusion during dwell time, and the pooled point estimate carried wide confidence limits spanning the null. Heterogeneity was high and the effect measure could not be confirmed on its native dichotomous scale, so the magnitude reported here should be regarded as an approximate standardized summary rather than a precise risk-based estimate. Current evidence does not support a clinically decisive occlusion advantage for either valved or non-valved PICC designs; adequately powered randomised trials reporting occlusion with explicit definitions and denominators (catheters and catheter-days) are needed.

**Registration.** Not registered.


## 1. Introduction

Peripherally inserted central catheters (PICCs) provide reliable central venous access for the delivery of chemotherapy, parenteral nutrition, prolonged antimicrobial courses and other vesicant or long-duration infusions. Because PICCs typically remain in situ for weeks to months, the cumulative burden of device-related complications over the dwell period is a central determinant of their clinical value. Among these complications, catheter occlusion is the most frequent non-infectious failure mode. Occlusion interrupts therapy, triggers unscheduled clinical contacts, consumes nursing time, frequently requires thrombolytic or declotting therapy, and in a proportion of cases mandates premature catheter removal and reinsertion, with all the attendant risks of a further vascular access procedure.

The mechanistic rationale for integrated valve technology addresses this problem directly. In a conventional open-ended (non-valved) PICC, the lumen is continuous with the bloodstream whenever the external clamp is open, so transient pressure changes — coughing, straining, arm movement, changes in intrathoracic pressure, or syringe recoil at the end of a flush — can permit blood reflux into the distal lumen. Refluxed blood provides the substrate for intraluminal fibrin and thrombus accumulation, which is the dominant pathway to partial (withdrawal) and subsequently complete occlusion. Valved designs interpose a pressure-sensitive, bidirectional valve that remains closed at neutral pressure, opening only under positive pressure for infusion or negative pressure for aspiration. The valve may be sited at the catheter tip (distal valve) or at the hub (proximal valve). By excluding blood from the lumen at rest, valved catheters are intended to reduce intraluminal thrombus formation, reduce or eliminate the need for heparin locking, and thereby lower occlusion rates.

Against this theoretical advantage, several counterarguments have been advanced. Valve mechanisms add a mechanical component that can itself malfunction, become encrusted with fibrin or precipitate, or impose higher resistance to flow and to blood sampling; valved catheters may be less tolerant of viscous infusates or of the high flush pressures used to clear sluggish lumens; and clinical teams may apply less rigorous flush-and-lock protocols to valved devices on the assumption that the valve is protective. Valved PICCs are also typically more expensive per unit. Whether the net balance of these considerations translates into a measurable reduction in occlusion in routine care is therefore an empirical question rather than a design-theoretic one.

Clinical guidance in this area has consequently remained equivocal, and individual comparative studies have reported effects in both directions, often in small samples with heterogeneous occlusion definitions. The present systematic review and meta-analysis was undertaken to address a single, narrowly specified question: in people of any age requiring a PICC, what is the effect of non-valved (open-ended) PICCs compared with PICCs incorporating integrated valve technology (proximal or distal valve) on catheter occlusion occurring during catheter dwell time? Other outcomes of the PICC literature — overall catheter failure, catheter-related venous thromboembolism, infection and central line-associated bloodstream infection (CLABSI), catheter breakage, dislodgement, phlebitis and other complications — were deliberately excluded from pooling in order to preserve a single, mechanistically coherent outcome construct and to avoid the interpretive ambiguity that arises when composite failure endpoints of differing composition are combined.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

The review followed the PRISMA 2020 reporting framework. The review was not prospectively registered; this is acknowledged as a limitation in Section 10.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met all of the following criteria.

**Population (P).** Human participants of any age (neonatal, paediatric, adult, or mixed) requiring insertion of a PICC for any clinical indication, in any care setting (inpatient, outpatient, home care, oncology, intensive care, neonatal intensive care).

**Intervention (I).** Non-valved, open-ended PICC — the treatment arm for the purposes of this analysis.

**Comparator (C).** PICC with integrated valve technology, including distal (tip-sited) valves and proximal (hub-sited) valves. Where a study reported more than one distinct valved device arm, the valved arms were combined into a single comparator arm (events and denominators summed) so that each study contributed one independent treatment-versus-control contrast.

**Outcome (O).** Catheter occlusion arising during catheter dwell time, operationalised as the number of participants (or catheters) experiencing partial or complete occlusion. Accepted operational definitions included withdrawal occlusion (infusion possible, aspiration not), lumen blockage, occlusion requiring thrombolytic or declotting therapy, and occlusion leading to catheter removal. The outcome was treated as dichotomous (events/total per arm).

**Study design (S).** Comparative designs with a concurrent valved comparator: randomised controlled trials, quasi-randomised trials, and non-randomised comparative cohort studies.

Exclusion criteria were: single-arm or non-comparative studies; comparisons of PICCs against other device types (tunnelled catheters, ports, midlines) without a valved-versus-non-valved contrast; narrative or systematic reviews, editorials, commentaries and conference abstracts without extractable arm-level data; duplicate publications and secondary reports of an already-included trial; and studies in which occlusion events could not be separated from a composite complication total.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

Electronic bibliographic databases covering the biomedical and nursing literature were searched from inception to the review date, using a strategy combining controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for the device (`peripherally inserted central catheter`, `PICC`, `central venous catheter`), the exposure contrast (`valve*`, `valved`, `open-ended`, `non-valved`, `Groshong`, `PASV`, `Solo`), and the outcome domain (`occlusion`, `blockage`, `patency`, `thrombolytic`, `urokinase`, `alteplase`, `catheter failure`, `complication*`). Reference lists of all included reports and of relevant prior reviews were screened for additional eligible studies. No language or date restrictions were applied at the search stage.

### 2.4 Study selection

Records retrieved by the search were de-duplicated and screened on title and abstract against the eligibility criteria. Potentially eligible records proceeded to full-text assessment, at which stage eligibility was confirmed and reasons for exclusion were recorded. Review articles, duplicate records of the same study population, and reports lacking extractable arm-level occlusion data were removed at this stage.

### 2.5 Data extraction

For each included study, the following were extracted: author and year; country and care setting; population (age group, clinical indication); device characteristics and valve location for each arm; number of participants and/or catheters per arm; the definition of occlusion used; the number of occlusion events per arm; dwell-time information where reported; and study design. For dichotomous data, events and totals were extracted per arm; where multiple valved arms existed, these were summed prior to analysis.

### 2.6 Effect measures and synthesis

Each study contributed a single effect estimate for the occlusion outcome. The direction convention adopted for this review was that a **positive effect size indicates fewer catheter occlusions in the non-valved PICC arm** than in the valved arm.

The native effect measure appropriate to a dichotomous outcome (risk ratio or odds ratio) could **not** be confirmed from the extracted records: the effect-measure field for the synthesis resolved to `UNKNOWN` and was populated by an unresolved fallback, with native pooling flagged as not used (`native_pooling_used = false`). Pooling was therefore carried out on a **standardized scale, with the pooled statistic reported as Hedges' g** (`measure_label = "Hedges' g"`; `computation_scale = HEDGES_G`). This is an important interpretive constraint and is stated explicitly wherever results are reported: the pooled value below is a **standardized fallback estimate, not a confirmed native-scale (risk ratio or odds ratio) result**, and it should not be back-translated into a risk difference or number-needed-to-treat without reanalysis of the primary event data.

Effects were pooled using a random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), which was pre-specified on the expectation that the included studies would differ in population, device, flushing protocol and occlusion definition and would therefore estimate related but non-identical underlying effects. Two-sided p-values and 95% confidence intervals are reported throughout.

### 2.7 Assessment of heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q with its associated p-value, the I² statistic (the proportion of total observed variability attributable to between-study differences rather than sampling error), and τ² (the REML estimate of the between-study variance on the Hedges' g scale). Interpretation of I² followed conventional thresholds (approximately 25% low, 50% moderate, 75% substantial/considerable), with the caveat that I² is imprecisely estimated when k is small.

### 2.8 Moderator analysis

Formal moderator analyses (subgroup analysis and meta-regression) were planned for valve location (proximal vs distal), population age group (paediatric/neonatal vs adult), study design (randomised vs non-randomised), and occlusion definition (any occlusion vs occlusion requiring intervention/removal). Given k = 5, these analyses were specified as exploratory only, and a cumulative analysis and a bubble plot were produced to visualise the stability and the precision-related patterning of the estimates.

### 2.9 Publication bias and small-study effects

Small-study effects were assessed by Egger's regression test of funnel plot asymmetry and by the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure, which estimates the number of studies (k₀) hypothetically missing from the funnel plot and recomputes the pooled estimate with imputed counterparts.

## 3. Search and Screening Results

The search and screening process yielded **five studies** meeting all eligibility criteria, contributing **five independent effect estimates** and a combined sample of **899 participants/catheters** to the occlusion synthesis. Records excluded during screening comprised non-comparative and single-arm device evaluations, studies comparing PICCs with non-PICC access devices, review and commentary articles, duplicate reports of the same study population, and comparative reports in which occlusion events could not be disaggregated from composite complication counts.

The one-to-one correspondence between included studies and effect estimates (5 studies → 5 effect sizes) confirms that no study contributed more than one contrast to the pooled analysis, so no statistical dependency arising from multiple arms or multiple timepoints within a single study required adjustment. Where studies had reported more than one valved device arm, those arms were collapsed into a single valved comparator as specified in the protocol.

All five included studies reported the occlusion outcome at a single analytic timepoint — cumulative occurrence during catheter dwell time. Accordingly, the synthesis comprised **one meta-analytic group only**; no separate outcome/timepoint subgroups required independent pooling, and no eligible group was set aside for insufficient data.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Study-level standardized effects are shown in Table 1 and displayed in the forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`). Their distribution is additionally visualised in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`.

The five estimates spanned a range of 0.936 standardized units, from **−0.221989** (Study 5; 95% CI −0.597 to 0.153) to **0.714473** (Study 2; 95% CI 0.380 to 1.049). Under the review's direction convention — positive values indicating fewer occlusions in the non-valved arm — four of the five point estimates were positive and one was negative, but only two reached conventional statistical significance individually:

- **Study 1** (g = 0.441, 95% CI 0.233 to 0.650) and **Study 2** (g = 0.714, 95% CI 0.380 to 1.049) had confidence intervals excluding zero, indicating a statistically detectable between-arm difference in each.
- **Study 3** (g = 0.129, 95% CI −0.284 to 0.542) and **Study 4** (g = 0.119, 95% CI −0.192 to 0.431) were close to the null, with confidence intervals comfortably spanning zero; both are consistent with no appreciable difference between device types.
- **Study 5** (g = −0.222, 95% CI −0.597 to 0.153) was the sole negative estimate and likewise non-significant, although its confidence interval does extend to a moderate effect in the opposite direction to the majority of the set.

A notable structural feature of these data is that the two statistically significant estimates (Studies 1 and 2) are also the two most extreme positive values, while the three remaining studies cluster near or below zero. The confidence intervals of Study 2 (lower limit 0.380) and Study 5 (upper limit 0.153) do not overlap, which is itself direct evidence of genuine between-study disagreement rather than simple imprecision — a point borne out quantitatively by the heterogeneity statistics in Section 7.

The pooled standardized effect was **Hedges' g = 0.250 (95% CI −0.056 to 0.556), p = 0.1092**. The confidence interval includes zero and the p-value exceeds the conventional 0.05 threshold; the analysis therefore **did not detect a statistically significant difference** between non-valved and valved PICCs in the risk of catheter occlusion during dwell time. Per the direction field recorded for this synthesis, the pooled estimate **favours the valved PICC comparator** (valved PICC with integrated proximal or distal valve; multiple valved arms combined). Because the result is not statistically significant, this stated direction of favour should be read as the orientation of a non-significant point estimate rather than as a demonstrated advantage for either device type.

Two interpretive qualifications must accompany this number. First, and most importantly, the **effect measure was not confirmed**: the native dichotomous measure for this outcome resolved to `UNKNOWN` and the reported statistic is a **standardized fallback on the Hedges' g scale**, not a confirmed risk ratio, odds ratio or risk difference. The magnitude 0.250 should therefore be treated as an approximate standardized summary of the direction and relative size of the between-arm difference, and not as a quantity from which absolute occlusion risks, relative risks or numbers-needed-to-treat can be derived. Second, the confidence interval is wide relative to the point estimate, spanning from a small effect in one direction (−0.056) to a moderate effect in the other (0.556); with k = 5 and N = 899 the analysis is simply underpowered to exclude clinically relevant differences in either direction.

The width of the interval also reflects the substantial between-study variance. Incorporating τ² = 0.093687, an approximate 95% prediction interval for the effect in a new, exchangeable study spans roughly **−0.84 to 1.34** — that is, a future comparable study could plausibly observe a moderate advantage for either device. This prediction interval, not the confidence interval, is the quantity most relevant to a clinician deciding which device to stock, and its breadth is the single most important result of this review.

The cumulative analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) illustrates how the running pooled estimate evolved as studies were added. The estimate was pulled upward by the early inclusion of the two positive, significant studies and was then drawn back toward the null by the three subsequent near-null and negative estimates, with the confidence interval crossing zero and remaining there once the full set was incorporated. No single study's removal would be expected to move the pooled estimate across the significance threshold in a way that materially changes the clinical conclusion, but the pattern does show that the headline result is sensitive to the relative weighting of Studies 1 and 2 against Studies 3–5.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **substantial and statistically significant**:

- **Cochran's Q = 17.047** on 4 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.0019** — the null hypothesis of a common underlying effect is firmly rejected.
- **I² = 78.56%** — approximately four-fifths of the total observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to real between-study differences rather than to within-study sampling error. This falls in the "substantial to considerable" band by conventional interpretation.
- **τ² = 0.093687** — the estimated between-study variance on the Hedges' g scale, equivalent to a between-study standard deviation of approximately 0.306 standardized units. This is of the same order as the pooled effect itself (0.250), which is a compact way of saying that the typical difference between studies is as large as the average effect being estimated.

The heterogeneity summary figure (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`) displays these statistics together. The practical consequence is that the pooled estimate should **not** be read as a single transportable effect applicable to all PICC populations and devices. It is the mean of a genuinely dispersed distribution of effects, and the dispersion is wide enough that studies on opposite sides of the null are both compatible with the model.

Plausible sources of this heterogeneity, all of which vary across the included literature, include: (i) the operational definition of occlusion, which ranged in this field from any withdrawal difficulty through to occlusion requiring thrombolytic therapy or forcing catheter removal — definitions that can differ several-fold in event rate for the same underlying catheter behaviour; (ii) the specific valve technology compared, since proximal (hub) and distal (tip) valve mechanisms differ mechanically and may not share a common effect; (iii) flushing and locking protocols, including whether heparin or saline-only locks were used and whether these differed systematically between arms, which directly modifies the mechanism through which valves are supposed to act; (iv) dwell duration and the denominator used (participants, catheters, or catheter-days), since cumulative occlusion risk rises with time in situ; (v) case mix, including paediatric versus adult populations, oncology versus parenteral nutrition versus antimicrobial indications, and the viscosity and thrombogenicity of the infusate; and (vi) study design and risk of bias, with non-randomised comparisons vulnerable to confounding by indication in device selection.

With only five studies, none of these candidate explanations could be tested with any confidence.

## 8. Moderator Analyses

Pre-specified moderator analyses were **not formally estimated**. With k = 5, any subgroup analysis would leave one to three studies per stratum and any meta-regression would have fewer than the commonly recommended minimum of approximately ten studies per covariate; such analyses would be severely underpowered, prone to spurious findings, and liable to be over-interpreted. We therefore report them as not performed rather than presenting uninterpretable coefficients.

Exploratory visual inspection was undertaken instead. The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) plots effect size against study size/precision, with marker area proportional to weight. It shows no monotonic relationship between study size and effect magnitude: the largest study (N = 362) returned an intermediate-to-large positive effect (g = 0.441), while the smallest (N = 101) returned a near-null effect (g = 0.129) and a mid-sized study (N = 110) returned the single negative effect (g = −0.222). The absence of a clear size–effect gradient is consistent with the formal publication-bias diagnostics in Section 9 and suggests that the observed heterogeneity is more likely to reflect genuine clinical and methodological diversity — most plausibly differences in occlusion definition, valve type and flushing protocol — than a small-study artefact.

The effect-distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`) shows a dispersed, right-shifted but non-unimodal spread of estimates, again consistent with the possibility that the five studies are estimating more than one underlying effect. Identifying which study-level characteristics separate them is a task for a larger evidence base.

## 9. Publication Bias

Small-study effects were assessed with two complementary methods.

**Egger's regression test** produced an intercept of **0.9048** with **p = 0.3731**, providing no statistically significant evidence of funnel plot asymmetry.

**Trim-and-fill** imputed **k₀ = 0** missing studies, and the adjusted pooled estimate was therefore identical to the unadjusted estimate at **g = 0.2502**. No adjustment for hypothetically unpublished results was required.

On this basis the overall **risk of publication bias was rated low**.

As noted in the analysis interpretation: Egger's regression test showed no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.3731), indicating no detectable small-study effects in this body of evidence; this is corroborated by the trim-and-fill analysis, which imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), meaning the pooled estimate required no adjustment for potentially unpublished results and is unlikely to be inflated by selective reporting.

An important caveat applies. While the overall risk of publication bias is rated low, these tests have limited statistical power — particularly when fewer than approximately ten studies are pooled, or when between-study heterogeneity is high, both of which are the case here (k = 5; I² = 78.56%). High heterogeneity in particular degrades the performance of regression-based asymmetry tests, because between-study variance inflates the scatter in the funnel plot irrespective of any bias mechanism. A null result should accordingly be read as **"no evidence of bias" rather than definitive "evidence of no bias."** Forms of reporting bias that these tests cannot detect — notably selective outcome reporting, where occlusion is measured but reported only when the result is notable, and selective definition of the occlusion endpoint after data inspection — remain plausible in a device literature of this kind, part of which is likely to be manufacturer-supported.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis of five comparative studies (N = 899 participants/catheters) found **no statistically significant difference between non-valved (open-ended) and valved PICCs in the risk of catheter occlusion during dwell time** (pooled Hedges' g = 0.250, 95% CI −0.056 to 0.556, p = 0.1092). The pooled point estimate is recorded as favouring the valved PICC comparator; however, because the confidence interval comfortably includes the null and because the effect measure is a standardized fallback rather than a confirmed native dichotomous measure, this should be read as the orientation of a non-significant summary rather than as evidence that valved devices reduce occlusion.

Three features of the evidence base dominate the interpretation.

First, **the evidence is sparse and imprecise**. Five studies, none large, cannot resolve a difference of the magnitude that would plausibly exist between two device designs that share the same insertion technique, the same material class and, in most settings, broadly similar flushing practice. The confidence interval is compatible with everything from a small advantage for valved catheters to a moderate advantage for non-valved catheters. The analysis is, in short, an honest null — a failure to detect a difference, not a demonstration of equivalence.

Second, **the evidence is genuinely inconsistent**. With I² = 78.56% and Q p = 0.0019, the five studies do not share a common effect. The two significant positive studies and the one negative study have non-overlapping or barely overlapping intervals. This inconsistency is clinically informative in itself: it suggests that whether a valve helps, hinders or makes no difference may depend on context — on the valve mechanism, on the population, on the infusate, and above all on how occlusion is defined and ascertained. A valve that reliably prevents blood reflux may still be associated with more "sluggish withdrawal" events if the valve itself raises aspiration resistance, which could invert the measured direction of effect depending on whether withdrawal occlusion is counted as an event.

Third, **the mechanistic rationale for valves does not appear to translate into a robust, reproducible occlusion benefit in practice**. If valve technology substantially and consistently reduced intraluminal thrombus, one would expect a tighter, more uniformly directed set of estimates than the one observed. The observed pattern is more consistent with valve effects being small relative to other determinants of patency — flushing technique, lock solution, catheter-to-vein ratio, tip position, lumen number and gauge, and the thrombogenicity of the infused therapy — which are rarely standardised between arms in observational comparisons and are often not reported at all.

The clinical implication is pragmatic rather than directive. On current evidence, occlusion risk alone does not provide a sound basis for preferring valved over non-valved PICCs or vice versa. Device selection should therefore be driven by other considerations for which evidence or local data are stronger: unit cost and procurement, compatibility with the institution's flushing and locking protocol (including whether a heparin-free regimen is desired), the need for blood sampling through the device, staff familiarity, and the full complication profile. Institutions should not expect to resolve a long-standing occlusion problem by switching valve technology, and should instead prioritise the interventions with better-established effects on patency — standardised pulsatile flushing with adequate volume, correct positive-pressure disconnection technique, appropriate catheter gauge selection, and verified central tip position.

For research, the priority is clear. What is needed is not another small single-centre comparison, but adequately powered randomised trials that (i) pre-specify and publish an explicit, reproducible occlusion definition distinguishing partial/withdrawal occlusion from complete occlusion; (ii) report events with both a per-catheter denominator and a catheter-days denominator, so that incidence rates can be compared across studies of differing dwell duration; (iii) standardise and report flushing and locking protocols identically in both arms; (iv) report the specific valve mechanism and its location, so that proximal and distal valve designs can be analysed separately; and (v) report raw event counts per arm so that future syntheses can be conducted on the native dichotomous scale. The last of these is directly relevant here, given that the present synthesis could not be performed on a confirmed native measure.

## 11. Data Quality & Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were raised for this synthesis. Two data-quality matters nonetheless warrant plain statement.

1. **The effect measure could not be confirmed.** For the single meta-analytic group reported here, the effect-measure field resolved to `UNKNOWN`, with the source recorded as an unresolved fallback and native pooling flagged as not used (`native_pooling_used = false`). The pooled statistic is therefore reported on a **standardized Hedges' g scale as a fallback**, and is **not** a confirmed risk ratio, odds ratio or risk difference, despite the outcome being dichotomous by definition. Readers should not convert the reported value into absolute or relative risks. A deterministic Effect Measure Summary table is appended after this text and should be read as the authoritative record of the measure used.

2. **Screening-stage exclusions.** Consistent with the eligibility criteria, review articles, duplicate records of the same study population, non-comparative reports, and comparative reports lacking separable arm-level occlusion counts were excluded. Where a study reported multiple valved device arms, these were combined into a single valved comparator so that each study contributed exactly one independent contrast; the 1:1 ratio of included studies (5) to effect estimates (5) confirms that no study was double-counted in the pooled analysis.

## 12. Limitations

The following limitations should be weighed when interpreting these results.

1. **Very small number of studies (k = 5).** This limits precision, prevents formal moderator analysis, and renders the heterogeneity and publication-bias diagnostics underpowered. I² and τ² are themselves estimated with considerable uncertainty at this k.

2. **Unconfirmed effect measure.** The pooled result is a standardized fallback (Hedges' g), not a confirmed native dichotomous effect measure. This is the most consequential analytic limitation, because it prevents translation of the finding into the risk-based quantities clinicians use.

3. **Substantial unexplained heterogeneity.** With I² = 78.56% and Q p = 0.0019, the pooled mean is a summary of a dispersed distribution. The approximate 95% prediction interval (roughly −0.84 to 1.34) indicates that a future comparable study could plausibly favour either device.

4. **Heterogeneous outcome definitions.** The included studies accepted a range of operationalisations of occlusion (partial/withdrawal occlusion, lumen blockage, occlusion requiring thrombolysis, occlusion prompting removal). Pooling across these definitions introduces construct-level heterogeneity that cannot be separated from true effect heterogeneity at this k.

5. **Combination of valved arms.** Proximal and distal valve technologies were combined into a single comparator where studies reported both. If these mechanisms have different effects on occlusion — which is mechanistically plausible — the combined comparator will mask them and contribute to the observed heterogeneity.

6. **Mixed study designs and unassessed risk of bias.** The evidence base includes non-randomised comparative studies susceptible to confounding by indication in device allocation, and no formal risk-of-bias assessment (e.g. RoB 2 or ROBINS-I) or GRADE certainty rating was incorporated into this synthesis. Certainty in the pooled estimate would, on the face of the available information, be downgraded for imprecision and inconsistency at minimum.

7. **Denominator ambiguity.** Events were extracted as participants or catheters affected, without consistent adjustment for differing dwell durations. Because cumulative occlusion risk increases with time in situ, studies with longer dwell periods will report higher event proportions irrespective of device, and unadjusted pooling of proportions across differing dwell times is an additional source of heterogeneity.

8. **No prospective protocol registration**, which limits protection against post hoc changes to the analysis plan.

9. **Restriction to a single outcome.** By design, catheter failure, venous thromboembolism, infection/CLABSI, breakage, dislodgement and other complications were not pooled. The present result therefore speaks only to occlusion and must not be read as an overall comparative assessment of valved versus non-valved PICCs.

## 13. Conclusion

In people of any age requiring a PICC, this meta-analysis of five comparative studies (N = 899 participants/catheters) found **no statistically significant difference between non-valved (open-ended) PICCs and PICCs with integrated proximal or distal valve technology in the risk of catheter occlusion during catheter dwell time** (pooled Hedges' g = 0.250, 95% CI −0.056 to 0.556; p = 0.1092; k = 5). Per the recorded direction field, the non-significant pooled point estimate favours the valved PICC comparator. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant (I² = 78.56%; τ² = 0.093687; Q = 17.047, df = 4, p = 0.0019), and the corresponding prediction interval is wide enough to encompass a moderate advantage for either device. There was no detectable publication bias (Egger's p = 0.3731; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted g = 0.2502), though these tests are underpowered at this k.

Two caveats are integral to the conclusion. The effect measure for this synthesis was **not confirmed on its native dichotomous scale**, and the reported magnitude is a standardized fallback rather than a risk ratio or odds ratio. And the evidence base is small, inconsistent and methodologically mixed.

Taken together, current evidence does not demonstrate a clinically decisive occlusion advantage for either valved or non-valved PICC designs. Decisions between these devices should rest on cost, compatibility with local flushing and locking protocols, sampling requirements and the wider complication profile, rather than on an expected difference in occlusion risk. Adequately powered randomised trials, using explicit and reproducible occlusion definitions, reporting both per-catheter and per-catheter-day denominators, standardising flush and lock protocols across arms, distinguishing proximal from distal valve mechanisms, and publishing raw event counts per arm, are required before a firm recommendation can be made.
