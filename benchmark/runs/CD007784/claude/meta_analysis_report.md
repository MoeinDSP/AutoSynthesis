# Statin Therapy and Urinary Protein Excretion in Adults with Chronic Kidney Disease Not Requiring Dialysis: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of End-of-Treatment Proteinuria (g/24 h)

## Abstract

**Background.** Dyslipidaemia is near-universal in chronic kidney disease (CKD), and experimental work suggests that HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors (statins) exert pleiotropic, lipid-independent effects on glomerular permselectivity and tubulointerstitial inflammation. Whether these effects translate into a measurable reduction in urinary protein excretion in pre-dialysis CKD remains uncertain.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of statin therapy versus placebo or no statin (co-interventions balanced across arms) on end-of-treatment 24-hour urinary protein excretion (g/24 h) in adults with CKD not requiring dialysis.

**Methods.** A systematic search of randomised controlled trials was screened against pre-specified eligibility criteria. The pre-specified outcome was the final (end-of-treatment) mean, SD and n per arm for 24-hour urinary protein excretion — or 24-hour urinary albumin excretion where this was the trial's only measure of urinary protein loss — expressed in g/24 h, taken at the last on-treatment timepoint before any washout, crossover or withdrawal phase. Change scores and baseline values were not used. Variability reported as SE/SEM was converted to SD (SD = SE × √n); units reported in mg/24 h were divided by 1000. Effect sizes were pooled on the native mean-difference scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** Eight trials met the eligibility criteria and six contributed extractable end-of-treatment proteinuria data suitable for pooling (k = 6; N = 355 randomised participants). The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was **−0.378 g/24 h (95% CI −0.687 to −0.069; p = 0.0164)**, a result that **favours statin therapy** (i.e., lower urinary protein excretion at end of treatment than placebo/no statin). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (Q = 23.636, df = 5, p = 0.0003; I² = 79.85%; τ² = 0.0914), with study-level estimates ranging from −1.626 to +0.078 g/24 h and two of six trials crossing no boundary of benefit. The approximate 95% prediction interval (−1.32 to +0.57 g/24 h) includes the null, indicating that a future trial could plausibly show no effect or a small adverse effect. Egger's test showed no funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept −0.8586; p = 0.9205) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0); small-study-effect risk was rated **low**.

**Conclusions.** In adults with pre-dialysis CKD, statin therapy was associated with a statistically significant but heterogeneous reduction in end-of-treatment 24-hour urinary protein excretion of approximately 0.38 g/24 h compared with placebo or no statin. The magnitude is clinically non-trivial but the confidence interval approaches the null, heterogeneity is high and unexplained, the evidence base is small (six trials, 355 participants), and two eligible trials could not be pooled. The finding should be regarded as hypothesis-supporting rather than definitive, and does not by itself establish that statins alter hard renal endpoints.

## 1. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects an estimated 8–16% of the global adult population and is accompanied by a characteristic atherogenic dyslipidaemia. HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors (statins) are widely prescribed in this population, principally for cardiovascular risk reduction. Beyond lipid lowering, however, statins have been reported to exert so-called pleiotropic effects — inhibition of mevalonate-dependent isoprenylation of small GTPases, downregulation of monocyte chemoattractant protein-1, reduced mesangial cell proliferation, attenuation of transforming growth factor-β signalling and improved endothelial nitric oxide availability — any of which could plausibly reduce glomerular protein leak and tubular protein handling.

Proteinuria is both a marker and a mediator of CKD progression: urinary protein excretion is one of the strongest independent predictors of progression to kidney failure, and reduction in proteinuria is widely used as a surrogate endpoint in nephroprotection trials. If statins reduce urinary protein excretion over and above the effects of renin–angiotensin system blockade and blood pressure control, this would constitute an important ancillary benefit of a drug class already indicated in most patients with CKD.

The randomised evidence is, however, fragmented. Trials have been small, have used heterogeneous statins and doses, have enrolled populations with widely differing baseline proteinuria (from microalbuminuria to overt nephrotic-range proteinuria), and have reported urinary protein loss in inconsistent units and metrics (percentage change, change from baseline, albumin-to-creatinine ratio, 24-hour collections). This meta-analysis therefore restricts itself to a single, tightly specified quantity — the **end-of-treatment mean 24-hour urinary protein excretion in g/24 h** — in order to permit pooling on the native, clinically interpretable scale rather than as a dimensionless standardised effect.

### Research question

In adults with chronic kidney disease not requiring dialysis, what is the effect of statin therapy (HMG-CoA reductase inhibitors), compared with placebo or no statin treatment (with other co-interventions given equally to both arms), on urinary protein excretion (proteinuria, g/24 h) at the end of the randomised treatment period?

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Design and reporting standard

The review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement. The structure below follows the required sequence: search and screening, included studies, effect sizes, meta-analytic results, heterogeneity, moderators, publication bias, discussion, limitations and conclusion.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults (≥18 years) with chronic kidney disease not requiring dialysis. Trials enrolling mixed populations (CKD and non-CKD participants) were eligible only if data for the CKD subgroup randomised to statin versus placebo/no statin could be isolated.

**Intervention.** Any HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor — pravastatin, atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, fluvastatin, lovastatin, cerivastatin or simvastatin — at any dose, by any route, for any duration.

**Comparator.** Placebo or no statin treatment, with all other treatments (antihypertensives, RAS blockade, glycaemic therapy, dietary advice) administered equally in both arms.

**Outcome.** End-of-treatment 24-hour urinary protein excretion expressed in g/24 h. Where 24-hour urinary *albumin* excretion was the trial's only measure of urinary protein loss, this was accepted as the outcome. The final (end-of-treatment) mean, standard deviation and number of analysed participants per arm were extracted at the last on-treatment timepoint preceding any washout, crossover or withdrawal phase. Change-from-baseline scores and baseline values were explicitly *not* extracted.

**Design.** Randomised controlled trials (parallel-group or, for the pre-crossover phase only, randomised crossover trials).

### 2.3 Data extraction and transformation rules

A pre-specified extraction protocol was applied:

1. **Unit conversion.** Values reported in mg/24 h were divided by 1000 to yield g/24 h; mg/day was treated as equivalent to mg/24 h. The original unit and the conversion applied were recorded for every extraction.
2. **Dispersion conversion.** Where variability was reported as a standard error or SEM, the standard deviation was reconstructed as SD = SE × √n. Footnotes and statistical-methods sections were checked in every case to determine whether a "±" value denoted SD or SEM; ambiguous reports were resolved conservatively.
3. **Graphical extraction.** Where end-of-treatment values appeared only in a figure, means and error bars were read from the figure and SEM converted to SD as above.
4. **Duplicate reports.** Where the same trial was described in more than one publication, data were extracted once, from the report containing the full randomised sample.
5. **Multi-arm and mixed-population trials.** Only the CKD participants randomised to statin versus placebo/no statin were used; where a trial had multiple active statin arms, the arm matching the primary comparison was selected to avoid unit-of-analysis error through double-counting of the shared control group.

### 2.4 Effect measure and synthesis

The effect measure was specified *a priori* by the review question as the **Mean Difference (MD)** on the native g/24 h scale (effect-measure source: explicit user request). Native-scale pooling was successfully executed (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the pooled statistic reported below is a confirmed mean difference in grams of protein excreted per 24 hours — not a standardised fallback.

**Direction convention.** Effect estimates retain the raw statistical sign convention (statin minus control). Because lower urinary protein excretion is the clinically desirable outcome, a *negative* mean difference corresponds to *less* proteinuria on statin therapy. In all statements below, the arm favoured is reported from the analysis's own `favors` field rather than inferred from the sign.

Pooling used an inverse-variance random-effects model with between-study variance estimated by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, the I² statistic and τ². A 95% prediction interval was derived as the pooled estimate ± t₍k−2₎,₀.₉₇₅ × √(τ² + SE²).

### 2.5 Assessment of small-study effects

Funnel-plot asymmetry was tested with Egger's regression of the standard normal deviate on precision, and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure was applied to estimate the number of potentially missing studies and the correspondingly adjusted pooled effect.

## 3. Search and Screening

The screening pipeline identified and assessed candidate randomised trials of statin therapy in pre-dialysis CKD reporting urinary protein excretion. After title/abstract and full-text screening against the eligibility criteria above, **eight trials** were judged to meet the population, intervention, comparator and design criteria for this review.

Of these eight eligible trials, **six provided end-of-treatment 24-hour urinary protein (or albumin) excretion data in a form that could be converted to g/24 h with an estimable per-arm mean, SD and n**, and were carried forward to quantitative synthesis. The remaining **two trials (25% of the eligible set) did not yield extractable end-of-treatment data on the pre-specified scale** — for example because only change scores, percentage changes, or ratio-based measures (albumin-to-creatinine ratio) were reported, or because no per-arm dispersion statistic could be recovered or reconstructed. These trials are retained in the narrative description of the evidence base but contribute no weight to the pooled estimate. Their exclusion from the synthesis is a potential source of outcome-reporting bias and is discussed in Sections 9 and 11.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Study-level effects are expressed as mean differences in end-of-treatment 24-hour urinary protein excretion (g/24 h), statin minus control, so that negative values denote less protein in the urine on statin therapy.

The six estimates span **−1.626 to +0.078 g/24 h**, a range of 1.70 g/24 h. Five of six point estimates lie below zero (favouring statins) and one (study_7_R7, +0.078 g/24 h) lies marginally above zero. **Two of six trials have confidence intervals excluding the null**, and both are the trials with the largest effects: study_6_R6 (−1.626 g/24 h, 95% CI −2.125 to −1.126) and study_1_R1 (−1.402 g/24 h, 95% CI −1.953 to −0.851). The remaining four trials — study_2_R2, study_3_R3, study_5_R5 and study_7_R7 — have intervals that comfortably include no difference, with point estimates clustered tightly between −0.514 and +0.078 g/24 h.

This bimodal pattern (two trials showing large reductions of ~1.4–1.6 g/24 h; four trials showing effects of ≤0.5 g/24 h and statistically indistinguishable from nil) is the dominant structural feature of the dataset and drives both the pooled estimate and its heterogeneity. Notably, the two large-effect trials are **not** the smallest trials in the set — study_6_R6 (n = 82) is the largest trial pooled and study_1_R1 (n = 63) is above the median — so the divergence cannot be attributed to a simple precision gradient. ## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

The random-effects (REML) synthesis of six trials and 355 participants yielded:

> **Pooled Mean Difference (MD) = −0.378 g/24 h (95% CI −0.687 to −0.069), p = 0.0164, k = 6, N = 355.**

The effect measure is the **Mean Difference (MD)**, pooled on its native scale (`native_pooling_used = true`); this is therefore a confirmed native-scale result in grams of urinary protein per 24 hours, not a standardised proxy. Per the analysis's direction field, the result **favours statin therapy** — that is, participants allocated to a statin excreted, on average, about 0.38 g less protein per 24 hours at the end of the randomised treatment period than participants allocated to placebo or no statin. The 95% confidence interval is consistent with a reduction as large as 0.69 g/24 h and as small as 0.07 g/24 h; it excludes zero, but only narrowly at its upper limit.

The forest plot of individual and pooled estimates is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. A cumulative meta-analysis, which shows how the pooled estimate evolves as studies are added, is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; inspection of this plot is informative because the pooled estimate is sensitive to the inclusion of the two large-effect trials.

**Clinical framing.** A 0.38 g/24 h absolute reduction is a meaningful quantity in nephrology — in trials of RAS blockade, reductions of this order have been associated with slower eGFR decline — but its interpretation depends critically on baseline proteinuria. In a cohort with nephrotic-range proteinuria (>3.5 g/24 h) a 0.38 g/24 h difference is a ~10% relative reduction of modest clinical importance; in a cohort with baseline excretion of ~1 g/24 h it would represent a substantial ~38% relative reduction. Because baseline proteinuria was not harmonised across the pooled trials, the absolute pooled MD should not be read as a universally applicable treatment effect.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **substantial to considerable** and statistically unambiguous:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 23.636 (df = 5) |
| p-value for Q | 0.0003 |
| I² | 79.85% |
| τ² (REML) | 0.0914 (g/24 h)² |
| τ (between-study SD) | ≈ 0.302 g/24 h |
| 95% prediction interval (approx.) | −1.32 to +0.57 g/24 h |

Approximately 80% of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to genuine between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error, and the Q-test decisively rejects the hypothesis of a common underlying effect (p = 0.0003). The between-study standard deviation of true effects (τ ≈ 0.30 g/24 h) is of the same order as the pooled effect itself (0.38 g/24 h), which means the true treatment effect plausibly differs in magnitude by a factor of two or more across clinical settings.

Critically, **the approximate 95% prediction interval (−1.32 to +0.57 g/24 h) includes zero**. Whereas the confidence interval tells us the average effect across these six trials is probably non-null, the prediction interval tells us that in a new, exchangeable trial of statins in pre-dialysis CKD the effect on end-of-treatment proteinuria could plausibly range from a large reduction (1.3 g/24 h) to a small increase (0.6 g/24 h). This distinction is central to the correct interpretation of the review and is emphasised again in the Discussion.

A graphical summary of the heterogeneity diagnostics is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

**Sources of heterogeneity (narrative).** Because the two divergent trials (R1, R6) are not distinguished by sample size, the most plausible drivers are clinical and methodological: (i) differences in baseline proteinuria, which mechanically scale the absolute mean difference attainable; (ii) differences in statin agent, dose and lipophilicity; (iii) differences in treatment duration, since anti-proteinuric effects may require months to manifest; (iv) differences in background RAS blockade, which may leave little residual proteinuria available for a statin to reduce in optimally treated cohorts; and (v) methodological differences in outcome ascertainment, including trials in which 24-hour albumin excretion substituted for total protein excretion, and trials in which SDs were reconstructed from SEM or read from figures. None of these can be tested formally in a dataset of six trials.

## 8. Moderators

No formal moderator analysis (subgroup analysis or meta-regression) was undertaken, and none could be defended statistically. With **k = 6 trials**, the available degrees of freedom are far below the conventional minimum of approximately ten studies per covariate for meta-regression; any single-covariate model would be severely overfitted and its coefficients uninterpretable, while subgroup analyses would compare groups of two or three trials each with near-complete confidence-interval overlap.

The only moderator-like dimension that could be examined descriptively is **study precision/sample size**, explored visually in the bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, in which marker area is proportional to study weight. Descriptively, there is **no monotonic relationship between trial size and effect magnitude**: the largest trial (study_6_R6, n = 82) reports the largest reduction (−1.626 g/24 h), while the second- and third-largest trials (study_7_R7, n = 80; study_1_R1, n = 63) report, respectively, the only positive estimate (+0.078 g/24 h) and the second-largest reduction (−1.402 g/24 h). The smallest trials (n = 36, 38) report small, null-compatible effects. This pattern is inconsistent with a classical small-study-effect gradient and is consistent with the formal bias tests reported in Section 9.

Clinically important moderators that *should* be examined when the evidence base matures — baseline proteinuria stratum, statin agent and intensity, treatment duration, diabetic versus non-diabetic CKD aetiology, baseline eGFR category, and concomitant RAS blockade — could not be evaluated here because trial-level covariate data were not available in the extraction dataset in a form permitting quantitative modelling. **The 79.85% heterogeneity observed in this review therefore remains entirely unexplained**, and that fact should be weighed heavily when applying the pooled estimate to any individual patient or population.

## 9. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Two complementary assessments were performed:

| Assessment | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | −0.8586 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.9205 |
| Trim-and-fill: studies imputed (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill: adjusted estimate | −0.6273 |
| Overall small-study-effect risk rating | **Low** |

Egger's regression test shows no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.9205, far from conventional significance), indicating no detectable small-study effects in which smaller or less precise trials report systematically different effects from larger ones. This is corroborated by the trim-and-fill procedure, which imputed **zero** missing studies (k₀ = 0) — meaning the funnel configuration is already symmetric and the pooled estimate requires no imputation-based adjustment. Hence the "low risk" rating. (The trim-and-fill routine additionally reports an adjusted point estimate of −0.6273 g/24 h; because no studies were imputed, this value reflects the differing variance-weighting internal to the trim-and-fill estimator rather than any correction for missing data, and the unadjusted REML estimate of −0.378 g/24 h remains the result of record.)

**Caveat.** These results indicate an *absence of evidence* for bias rather than evidence of its absence. Both Egger's test and trim-and-fill have low statistical power when fewer than ten studies are pooled — as here, with k = 6 — and neither can detect bias that affects all studies uniformly, such as outcome-reporting bias within published trials, exclusion of grey literature, or a field-wide tendency toward positive findings. Moreover, in the presence of I² ≈ 80%, funnel asymmetry tests are known to be unreliable because true effect heterogeneity itself induces scatter that is difficult to distinguish from selection. The conclusion is therefore best framed as "**no statistical indication of small-study effects**," ideally triangulated with visual inspection of the funnel plot and with a search strategy that reaches unpublished and trial-registry sources.

A specific, non-trivial threat remains: **two of the eight eligible trials could not be pooled because end-of-treatment proteinuria was not reported in an extractable form**. If the decision to report proteinuria as a change score, percentage change or ratio rather than as an end-of-treatment mean was related to the direction or significance of the result, selective-outcome-reporting bias would persist undetected by Egger's test, which can only interrogate the trials that were pooled.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 Principal findings

Across six randomised trials and 355 adults with CKD not requiring dialysis, statin therapy reduced end-of-treatment 24-hour urinary protein excretion by a pooled mean difference of 0.378 g/24 h relative to placebo or no statin (95% CI 0.069 to 0.687 g/24 h lower; p = 0.0164). The direction of the pooled result favours statin therapy. This is consistent with a genuine, lipid-independent anti-proteinuric action of HMG-CoA reductase inhibition, and is directionally concordant with the mechanistic literature on statin effects on podocyte function, mesangial proliferation and tubulointerstitial inflammation.

Three qualifications, however, substantially temper the strength of this inference.

**First, the statistical significance is fragile.** The upper confidence limit (−0.069 g/24 h) lies close to the null, and p = 0.0164 provides only moderate evidence against the no-effect hypothesis. In a six-study random-effects model, the width and position of the interval are sensitive to the τ² estimate; alternative between-study variance estimators (e.g., DerSimonian–Laird, Paule–Mandel) or a Hartung–Knapp small-sample adjustment would widen the interval and could render it null-inclusive. Readers should not treat p = 0.0164 as robust confirmation.

**Second, the heterogeneity is both large and unexplained.** With I² = 79.85% and τ ≈ 0.30 g/24 h, the six trials are plainly not estimating a single common effect. The dataset is effectively bimodal: two trials report reductions of 1.4–1.6 g/24 h and four report effects between −0.51 and +0.08 g/24 h that are individually indistinguishable from no effect. The pooled value of −0.378 g/24 h is an average over these two regimes and does not correspond to the effect observed in any individual trial. Because the prediction interval (−1.32 to +0.57 g/24 h) spans the null, the honest summary is that statins reduce proteinuria *on average across these trials*, but that whether they will do so in any particular clinical setting remains uncertain.

**Third, the scale of the outcome is itself heterogeneous.** The pre-specified outcome permitted 24-hour urinary albumin excretion to substitute for total protein excretion where it was a trial's only measure of urinary protein loss. Albumin excretion is numerically lower than total protein excretion in the same patient, so pooling the two on an absolute g/24 h scale introduces a systematic, direction-unpredictable scale mismatch that contributes to τ². Likewise, trials enrolling patients with nephrotic-range proteinuria can generate much larger absolute mean differences than trials enrolling patients with microalbuminuria, purely as an arithmetic consequence of baseline level. A ratio-of-means or standardised analysis would be less vulnerable to this specific artefact, at the cost of clinical interpretability.

### 10.2 Relationship to the wider evidence

The direction and approximate magnitude of this result are broadly consistent with earlier syntheses of statin effects on proteinuria in CKD, which have generally reported modest reductions with high heterogeneity. It is important, however, to distinguish surrogate from hard endpoints: large cardiovascular outcome trials in CKD populations have not demonstrated that statins slow the loss of glomerular filtration rate or reduce progression to kidney failure, notwithstanding their established cardiovascular benefit. The present finding should therefore be interpreted as evidence about a *surrogate* marker. A reduction in measured proteinuria does not automatically imply preserved kidney function, particularly where the mechanism of the reduction (e.g., haemodynamic change, altered tubular reabsorption of filtered protein) is not established.

### 10.3 Implications for practice

The results do not alter the primary indication for statins in CKD, which remains cardiovascular risk reduction. They provide weak, heterogeneous support for a secondary, anti-proteinuric benefit of approximately 0.4 g/24 h on average, which may be worth monitoring in patients with residual proteinuria despite maximal RAS blockade. They do not justify initiating or intensifying statin therapy *for the purpose of* reducing proteinuria.

### 10.4 Implications for research

Priority needs are: (i) adequately powered trials (several hundred participants per arm, versus the current maximum of 82 in total) with proteinuria as a pre-specified primary endpoint; (ii) stratification or pre-specified subgroup analysis by baseline proteinuria stratum and statin intensity; (iii) uniform reporting of end-of-treatment 24-hour total protein excretion with explicit standard deviations, so that future syntheses do not lose a quarter of the eligible evidence to non-extractable reporting; and (iv) parallel reporting of eGFR slope, so that any anti-proteinuric effect can be linked to, or dissociated from, preservation of filtration function.

## 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No formal validation flags were raised by the analytic pipeline for this synthesis. The following data-quality observations nonetheless warrant plain statement:

- **Two of eight eligible trials were not pooled.** Eight trials met the eligibility criteria but only six (75%) yielded an extractable end-of-treatment mean, SD and n for urinary protein excretion convertible to g/24 h. The two non-contributing trials add no weight to the pooled estimate and their results are therefore absent from every statistic in this report. This is a potential source of selective-outcome-reporting bias that the formal bias tests in Section 9 cannot detect.
- **Effect measure.** The effect measure was the **Mean Difference (MD)**, set by explicit specification of the review question, and native-scale pooling was achieved (`native_pooling_used = true`). The pooled value is therefore a confirmed native-scale mean difference in g/24 h and is **not** a standardised fallback. No effect-measure conflict was recorded. The pooled statistic must not be described as Hedges' *g*.
- **Single outcome group.** All six contributing trials were pooled into a single outcome/timepoint group (end-of-treatment 24-hour urinary protein excretion). No separate outcome or timepoint groups were defined, so no group-specific sub-syntheses are reported, and no group was marked as having insufficient data.
- **Derived dispersion statistics.** By protocol, standard deviations were reconstructed from standard errors (SD = SE × √n) where necessary, and means and error bars were read from figures where end-of-treatment values appeared only graphically. Both procedures introduce measurement error into study weights that is not propagated into the reported confidence intervals.
- **Outcome-definition substitution.** Where 24-hour urinary albumin excretion was a trial's only measure of urinary protein loss, it was accepted in place of total protein excretion, per protocol. This is a known scale mismatch within the pooled set.
- **Unit-of-analysis integrity.** De-duplication (one effect size per trial, extracted from the report containing the full randomised sample) and single-arm selection for multi-arm trials were applied, so no control group is double-counted.
- **Discrepant trim-and-fill point estimate.** Trim-and-fill imputed zero studies yet reports an adjusted estimate (−0.6273 g/24 h) that differs from the REML estimate (−0.378 g/24 h). With k₀ = 0 no imputation-based correction is implied; the discrepancy reflects the internal estimator of the trim-and-fill routine and should not be read as a bias-corrected effect.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base.** Six trials and 355 participants in total; the largest single trial contributed 82 participants. The synthesis is underpowered to detect modest effects reliably and highly sensitive to the influence of any single trial.
2. **High, unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 79.85%, τ² = 0.0914, Q p = 0.0003. The pooled mean does not represent any individual trial's effect, and the 95% prediction interval (−1.32 to +0.57 g/24 h) includes the null.
3. **No moderator analysis possible.** With k = 6, meta-regression and subgroup analysis were statistically indefensible; the drivers of heterogeneity — baseline proteinuria, statin agent and dose, duration, CKD aetiology, background RAS blockade — remain untested.
4. **Fragile significance.** p = 0.0164 with an upper confidence limit of −0.069 g/24 h; alternative τ² estimators or small-sample (Hartung–Knapp) adjustment could plausibly move the interval across the null.
5. **Incomplete capture of eligible evidence.** Two of eight eligible trials were unpoolable for reporting reasons (Section 11), raising the possibility of selective outcome reporting.
6. **Outcome-scale inconsistency.** Mixing 24-hour albumin and 24-hour total protein excretion on an absolute g/24 h scale, together with wide variation in baseline proteinuria, introduces arithmetic heterogeneity independent of any true biological difference.
7. **Derived data.** Reconstruction of SDs from SEMs and extraction of values from figures add unquantified measurement error.
8. **Low-powered bias diagnostics.** Egger's test and trim-and-fill are unreliable with k < 10 and in the presence of high heterogeneity; the "low risk" rating reflects absence of detectable asymmetry, not demonstrated absence of bias.
9. **Risk of bias within trials not quantified here.** The supplied dataset did not include per-domain risk-of-bias judgements (randomisation concealment, blinding of outcome assessment, completeness of 24-hour collections, attrition), all of which are directly relevant to a self-collected outcome such as a 24-hour urine specimen. The certainty of evidence should accordingly be regarded as no better than low, on grounds of imprecision and inconsistency alone.
10. **Surrogate endpoint.** Proteinuria is a surrogate; this review provides no information on eGFR decline, kidney failure, or mortality.

## 13. Conclusion

In adults with chronic kidney disease not requiring dialysis, random-effects meta-analysis of six randomised trials (N = 355) found that statin therapy was associated with a **pooled Mean Difference in end-of-treatment 24-hour urinary protein excretion of −0.378 g/24 h (95% CI −0.687 to −0.069; p = 0.0164), a result that favours statin therapy** — that is, approximately 0.38 g/24 h less urinary protein than with placebo or no statin. Formal assessment showed no indication of small-study effects (Egger's p = 0.9205; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0; risk rated low).

This apparent benefit must, however, be reported alongside three findings of equal importance: heterogeneity was substantial and wholly unexplained (I² = 79.85%; τ² = 0.0914; Q p = 0.0003), the 95% prediction interval (−1.32 to +0.57 g/24 h) includes no effect, and two of the eight eligible trials could not be included because end-of-treatment proteinuria was not reported extractably. Four of the six pooled trials individually showed effects indistinguishable from nil; the pooled significance rests largely on two trials reporting reductions of 1.4–1.6 g/24 h.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore **qualified and provisional**: statins probably produce a modest average reduction in proteinuria in pre-dialysis CKD, but the size of that reduction is highly variable, its determinants are unknown, and the evidence is too sparse and too heterogeneous to support a confident clinical recommendation. Statins should continue to be prescribed in CKD for cardiovascular indications; an anti-proteinuric benefit should be regarded as plausible but unproven, and requires confirmation in adequately powered trials that pre-specify 24-hour urinary protein excretion as a primary endpoint and report it in extractable, end-of-treatment form.
