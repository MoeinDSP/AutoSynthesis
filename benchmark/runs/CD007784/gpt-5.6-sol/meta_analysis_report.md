# Statin Therapy and End-of-Treatment Proteinuria in Adults With Non-Dialysis Chronic Kidney Disease: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Statins may influence urinary protein loss in chronic kidney disease (CKD) independently of their lipid-lowering effects. This review evaluated statin therapy versus placebo or no statin treatment for end-of-treatment proteinuria in adults with CKD not requiring dialysis.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons involved adults with non-dialysis CKD randomized to a statin or placebo/no statin, with co-interventions applied equally. The prespecified outcome was final, end-of-treatment 24-hour urinary protein excretion, or 24-hour urinary albumin excretion only when it was the study’s sole measure of urinary protein loss, standardized to g/24 h. The supplied evidence set contained eight studies, of which five contributed effect estimates. A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled the native-scale Mean Difference (MD). Negative MD values represent less proteinuria and favor statin therapy.

**Results:** Five comparisons including 299 analyzed participants contributed to the meta-analysis. The pooled Mean Difference was −0.18661092317055 g/24 h (95% CI −0.458 to 0.084; p=0.17703393312130147), favoring statin therapy by the prespecified direction but not excluding no effect. Heterogeneity was considerable (Q=34.811; reported p=0.0; I²=87.03%; τ²=0.066329). Egger’s intercept was −1.1968 (p=0.8538), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k0=0); the supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as low. No moderator analyses were available.

**Conclusions:** The pooled estimate was compatible with a reduction in end-of-treatment proteinuria of approximately 0.187 g/24 h with statin therapy, but its confidence interval included no effect and the between-study heterogeneity was substantial. The result is therefore inconclusive rather than evidence of a definitive antiproteinuric effect. Interpretation is further limited by the small evidence base and absence of trial-level arm means, standard deviations, treatment details, and risk-of-bias assessments in the supplied data.

## Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is frequently accompanied by proteinuria, an indicator of glomerular injury and an adverse prognostic marker. Statins are principally prescribed to reduce atherosclerotic cardiovascular risk, but experimental and clinical observations have suggested possible effects on glomerular inflammation, endothelial function, and urinary protein loss. Whether these effects produce a measurable reduction in proteinuria among adults with CKD who do not require dialysis remains uncertain.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among adults with non-dialysis CKD, what is the effect of statin therapy, compared with placebo or no statin treatment with co-interventions applied equally, on urinary protein excretion at the end of randomized treatment?

The eligible outcome was the final end-of-treatment value for 24-hour urinary protein excretion or, only when it was the study’s sole measure of urinary protein loss, 24-hour urinary albumin excretion. The intended common unit was g/24 h. The last on-treatment assessment before washout, crossover, or withdrawal was the target time point; baseline values and change-from-baseline values were not eligible.

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies compared an HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor—such as pravastatin, atorvastatin, rosuvastatin, fluvastatin, lovastatin, cerivastatin, or simvastatin—with placebo or no statin in adults with CKD not requiring dialysis. Other treatments had to be applied equally between randomized groups. Studies of dialysis populations were outside the review question.

The outcome hierarchy prioritized 24-hour total urinary protein excretion. Twenty-four-hour urinary albumin excretion was eligible only when it was the study’s sole measure of urinary protein loss. Data extraction was intended to use each arm’s final mean, standard deviation (SD), and analyzed sample size. Values reported in mg/24 h or mg/day were to be divided by 1,000 to obtain g/24 h; standard errors were to be converted using SD = SE × √n after checking the report’s notation. Eligible figure-only results could be digitized, with error bars interpreted according to the article’s methods or figure legend. For duplicate or companion reports, one trial-level dataset from the report containing the full randomized sample was to be retained.

## Meta-Analytic Result

Five studies with 299 participants were pooled using a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The pooled **Mean Difference (MD)** was **−0.18661092317055 g/24 h** (95% CI **−0.458 to 0.084**; **p=0.17703393312130147**).

Using the prespecified direction and the supplied `favors` field, this point estimate favors statin therapy. It corresponds to approximately 0.187 g/24 h less urinary protein excretion at the end of treatment in the statin groups than in the placebo or no-statin groups. However, the confidence interval crossed the null value of 0 and ranged from a potentially meaningful reduction of 0.458 g/24 h to a possible increase of 0.084 g/24 h. The result therefore did not demonstrate a statistically conclusive reduction in proteinuria.

A cumulative meta-analysis plot was generated at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`. The supplied data did not state the ordering variable used in that plot, so apparent cumulative changes should not be interpreted as temporal trends unless the plot’s ordering is verified.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was considerable:

- Cochran’s Q = **34.811**;
- reported Q-test p-value = **0.0** (as encoded in the supplied output; this should be understood as below the output’s numerical display precision, not as a literal probability of zero);
- I² = **87.03%**; and
- τ² = **0.066329** on the MD scale.

An I² of 87.03% indicates that a large proportion of the observed dispersion was attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error under the conventional interpretation of I². This weakens the adequacy of a single average effect as a summary of all settings. Potential sources include differences in CKD cause and severity, baseline proteinuria, statin type and dose, treatment duration, concurrent antiproteinuric therapy, outcome definition, and laboratory measurement; however, the supplied dataset did not permit these explanations to be tested.

## Moderators

No quantitative moderator or subgroup analyses were supplied. Consequently, there is no evidence from this dataset about whether effects differ by statin agent, statin intensity, treatment duration, baseline proteinuria, diabetic versus non-diabetic CKD, CKD stage, control type, or background renin–angiotensin system blockade.

A bubble chart was generated at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, but no moderator coefficient, standard error, confidence interval, p-value, or residual heterogeneity statistic accompanied it. The chart is therefore descriptive only and should not be treated as evidence of effect modification.

Given only five pooled studies, any meta-regression would in any event have very low power and a high risk of unstable or spurious findings.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−1.1968** with **p=0.8538**. Thus, the supplied analysis found no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k0=0**, indicating that no studies were imputed. The supplied trim-and-fill output also reported an estimate of **−0.7195**; because its scale was not identified as the native MD scale and it differs materially from the primary pooled MD, it should not be interpreted as an adjusted g/24 h effect without verification.

The overall publication-bias risk was classified in the supplied results as **low**. Nevertheless, tests for funnel-plot asymmetry are poorly powered with only five studies, and absence of statistical evidence is not proof that reporting or publication bias is absent. The trim-and-fill algorithm also relies on assumptions that can be violated when genuine between-study heterogeneity is substantial, as it was here.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This meta-analysis of five studies and 299 participants produced a pooled Mean Difference of −0.18661092317055 g/24 h, favoring statin therapy according to the prespecified direction. The 95% CI (−0.458 to 0.084) included no effect, and the model p-value was 0.17703393312130147. The evidence therefore does not establish that statins reduce end-of-treatment proteinuria in adults with non-dialysis CKD, although a potentially important reduction remains compatible with the confidence interval.

The very high heterogeneity (I²=87.03%) is central to interpretation. It suggests that the average MD may combine materially different effects across populations, interventions, durations, or measurement practices. Without study characteristics and arm-level outcome data, it is not possible to determine whether the inconsistency reflects true clinical effect modification, outcome-scale issues, data extraction differences, or other methodological variation.

### Clinical interpretation

The result concerns urinary protein excretion only. It does not address estimated glomerular filtration rate, serum creatinine, progression to kidney failure, cardiovascular events, mortality, lipid outcomes, adverse events, or urinary podocytes. A reduction in proteinuria cannot be assumed to translate into improved kidney or cardiovascular outcomes without direct outcome evidence.

The analysis also pertains specifically to non-dialysis CKD and should not be extrapolated to maintenance dialysis populations. Because the statin agents, doses, CKD phenotypes, and treatment durations were not reported in the supplied dataset, the average result cannot support agent-specific or dose-specific recommendations.

### Statistical interpretation

The random-effects REML model appropriately allows the underlying treatment effect to vary across studies. Nonetheless, with only five studies, τ² and the pooled confidence interval may be imprecise. No prediction interval was supplied; therefore, the likely range of effects in a new comparable setting cannot be quantified from the available output.

The non-significant pooled result should not be interpreted as demonstrating equivalence or absence of effect. No equivalence margin was prespecified, and the confidence interval includes both potentially beneficial and slightly harmful values. Conversely, the favorable point estimate alone is insufficient to claim efficacy.

## Data Quality and Validation Notes

1. The pipeline identified eight studies but provided only five effect sizes. Reasons for non-contribution of the other three studies were not supplied, preventing verification of whether exclusions arose from ineligibility, unavailable data, or duplicate reporting.
2. Trial-level final means, SDs, analyzed arm counts, original measurement units, and unit-conversion records were unavailable. The requested extraction rules—including conversion from mg/day and conversion of SE to SD—could not be independently validated.
3. The study-level effect list was presented as standardized estimates, whereas the primary synthesis metadata identified native pooling with a Mean Difference in g/24 h. These two representations must not be conflated. Their numerical relationship could not be checked from the supplied data.
4. The trim-and-fill estimate of −0.7195 was not accompanied by a confirmed native-scale label and should not be compared directly with the pooled MD of −0.18661092317055 g/24 h without checking the underlying analysis object.
5. No risk-of-bias assessment, funding information, conflicts of interest, or protocol/registration record was supplied.
6. The Q-test p-value was encoded as 0.0. This reflects numerical rounding or display precision and should not be reported as a mathematically exact zero probability.

## Limitations

This report is limited by the content of the supplied aggregate output. First, the search strategy and screening history were incomplete, precluding a fully reproducible PRISMA flow and assessment of search comprehensiveness. Second, bibliographic and clinical characteristics of the included trials were unavailable. Third, the absence of arm-level endpoint data prevented verification that all values represented final on-treatment measurements, that albumin excretion was used only under the specified hierarchy, and that all values were correctly standardized to g/24 h.

Fourth, no formal risk-of-bias judgments were available. Fifth, only five studies were pooled, limiting precision, publication-bias assessment, and the feasibility of moderator analyses. Sixth, heterogeneity was substantial, yet the available data did not permit exploration of its causes. Seventh, no prediction interval, influence analysis, leave-one-out analysis, or alternative variance-estimation analysis was supplied. Finally, the mismatch between the standardized study-level entries and the native-scale pooled MD requires verification against the source extraction and analysis files before publication.

## Conclusion

In adults with non-dialysis CKD, statin therapy was associated with a pooled end-of-treatment Mean Difference in proteinuria of **−0.18661092317055 g/24 h** versus placebo or no statin (95% CI **−0.458 to 0.084**; p=**0.17703393312130147**), with the point estimate favoring statin therapy. The confidence interval included no effect, and heterogeneity was considerable (I²=**87.03%**). Accordingly, the available synthesis is inconclusive and does not establish a definitive antiproteinuric benefit. Better-reported randomized evidence with standardized endpoint measurement, transparent arm-level data, and sufficient information to explain clinical heterogeneity is needed.
