# Immediate Effects of Psychosocial Interventions on PTSD Symptom Severity After Rape or Sexual Assault: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Psychosocial interventions are widely used to treat post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) symptoms after rape or sexual assault, but their immediate post-treatment effect relative to inactive controls requires quantitative synthesis.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of psychosocial interventions, compared with wait-list, usual care, no treatment, or assessment-only control conditions, on continuous total PTSD symptom severity at the first assessment after treatment ended in adults who had experienced rape or sexual assault.

**Methods:** Randomised controlled trials in the supplied evidence corpus were eligible. The preferred outcome was a clinician-administered total PTSD severity score; when unavailable, the principal self-report total score was to be used. Effects were expressed as Hedges' *g*, with negative values indicating fewer PTSD symptoms with psychosocial intervention. A restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) random-effects model was used. The supplied pipeline contained 15 study records, of which seven comparisons contributed effect sizes, representing 500 analysed participants.

**Results:** The seven effects ranged from Hedges' *g* = −1.914639 to 0.392170. The pooled Hedges' *g* was −0.815564715049413 (95% CI −1.456 to −0.175; *p* = 0.012578782811237582), favoring psychosocial intervention. Heterogeneity was considerable: *Q* = 60.83, *I*² = 90.47%, and τ² = 0.660756 (reported *Q*-test *p* = 0.0). Egger's intercept was 1.1894 (*p* = 0.0339), indicating funnel-plot asymmetry and possible small-study effects. Trim-and-fill imputed no studies (*k*₀ = 0) and left the estimate unchanged at −0.8156.

**Conclusions:** Psychosocial interventions were associated with lower immediate post-treatment PTSD symptom severity than inactive controls, but the magnitude varied substantially across comparisons. Very high heterogeneity, statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry, the small number of effects, and incomplete study-level reporting require cautious interpretation.

# Search/Screening

## Eligibility criteria

The review question was framed around the following criteria:

- **Population:** Adults who had experienced rape or sexual assault. For mixed-trauma samples, a separately reported sexual-assault subgroup was preferred; otherwise, the whole trial sample was eligible.
- **Intervention:** A psychosocial intervention intended to address rape- or sexual-assault-related PTSD symptoms, including cognitive-behavioural therapy, prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, eye movement desensitisation and reprocessing, memory reconsolidation, and other psychosocial approaches.
- **Comparator:** An inactive control condition—wait-list, usual care, no treatment, or assessment only.
- **Design:** Randomised controlled trial.
- **Outcome:** Continuous total PTSD symptom severity at the first assessment after treatment ended. The prespecified hierarchy preferred a clinician-administered PTSD scale and otherwise used the principal self-report scale, such as the CAPS, PSS-I, PSS-SR, PDS, IES-R, or PCL.
- **Analysis population:** Reported analysed data were eligible, with intention-to-treat estimates preferred and completer or per-protocol estimates accepted when they were the only available results. Standard errors were to be converted to standard deviations where necessary.

Later follow-up assessments were excluded from this synthesis.

# Effect Sizes

The confirmed effect measure was **Hedges' *g***. It was calculated under the raw direction convention of intervention minus control, with higher PTSD scores denoting worse symptoms. Accordingly:

- a **negative** Hedges' *g* indicates lower PTSD symptom severity and favors psychosocial intervention;
- a **positive** Hedges' *g* indicates higher PTSD symptom severity in the intervention arm and favors inactive control.

The effects varied markedly. Five comparisons had confidence intervals entirely below zero and therefore individually favored intervention (R1, R2, R4, R5, and R6). One comparison had a confidence interval entirely above zero and favored inactive control (R3). One was compatible with benefit, harm, or no difference (R8). # Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled the seven immediate post-treatment effects. The **pooled Hedges' *g*** was **−0.815564715049413** (95% CI **−1.456 to −0.175**; *p* = **0.012578782811237582**; *k* = **7**; total analysed **N = 500**). Based on the prespecified direction and the supplied `favors` field, this result favors psychosocial intervention over inactive control.

The pooled estimate suggests lower immediate post-treatment total PTSD symptom severity in intervention groups, on average. However, it should be interpreted as the mean of a widely varying distribution of effects rather than as a uniform treatment effect. # Heterogeneity

Between-comparison heterogeneity was very high:

- Cochran's *Q* = **60.83**;
- reported *Q*-test *p* = **0.0** (a software-level rounded value, effectively indicating a very small *p*-value rather than a literal probability of zero);
- *I*² = **90.47%**;
- τ² = **0.660756**.

The *I*² value indicates that most observed dispersion was beyond that expected from within-comparison sampling error. This is also evident from the range of effects: two small comparisons showed very large negative estimates, five estimates favored intervention to varying degrees, one estimate was close to zero, and one significantly favored inactive control. A graphical summary of heterogeneity is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

No prediction interval was supplied, so one is not reconstructed here. Given *I*² = 90.47% and τ² = 0.660756, the pooled mean should not be assumed to describe the effect expected in every setting, population, intervention, or measurement context.

# Publication Bias

Egger's regression yielded an intercept of **1.1894** with *p* = **0.0339**. This statistically significant result indicates funnel-plot asymmetry and is consistent with possible small-study effects or publication bias. The trim-and-fill procedure estimated **zero** missing studies (*k*₀ = **0**) and produced an adjusted Hedges' *g* of **−0.8156**, unchanged from the rounded unadjusted estimate.

These findings are not contradictory. Trim-and-fill's failure to impute studies does not establish the absence of bias, particularly when only seven effects are available and heterogeneity is extreme. Egger's test also has limited reliability in a meta-analysis this small and may detect asymmetry caused by genuine heterogeneity, selective reporting, methodological differences, or chance rather than publication bias alone. In accordance with the supplied assessment, the overall risk of publication bias is considered **high**, and the pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously.

# Discussion

This synthesis found that psychosocial interventions were associated, on average, with lower total PTSD symptom severity immediately after treatment than inactive control conditions. The pooled Hedges' *g* of −0.815564715049413 was statistically different from zero and favored psychosocial intervention. The result is clinically encouraging in direction, but the supplied data do not permit translation into points on any particular PTSD instrument or determination of the proportion of participants achieving remission or clinically important change.

The principal feature of the evidence is inconsistency. Individual effects ranged from −1.914639 to 0.392170, and *I*² reached 90.47%. Thus, the average benefit coexisted with one comparison that significantly favored inactive control and one imprecise near-null comparison. Differences among therapeutic approaches, treatment dose, participant characteristics, comparator conditions, instruments, attrition, and trial conduct could plausibly contribute, but none could be evaluated using the supplied aggregate results.

The two largest negative effects occurred in relatively small comparisons (N = 38 and N = 36), while the largest comparison (N = 117) favored inactive control. This pattern is compatible with the significant Egger test, although it does not by itself establish selective publication. The absence of trim-and-fill imputations offers little reassurance because the method has limited diagnostic value with few, highly heterogeneous effects.

The review addresses only the **first assessment after treatment ended**. It therefore estimates immediate efficacy relative to inactive control and does not establish durability, comparative effectiveness against an active psychotherapy, acceptability, adverse effects, functional recovery, or longer-term symptom trajectories. Inactive comparators may also yield larger contrasts than credible active controls because they do not balance attention, expectancy, contact, or nonspecific therapeutic factors.

# Data Quality & Validation Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` object was supplied, several limitations of the input data require explicit documentation:

1. The pipeline reported 15 study records but only seven effect sizes; reasons that the remaining eight records did not contribute were unavailable.
2. Study reports were anonymised, preventing linkage to bibliographic citations and independent verification of eligibility, duplicate reports, or trial overlap.
3. Arm-level means, variability measures, sample sizes, PTSD instruments, and analysis populations were not supplied, so extraction choices and any SE-to-SD conversions could not be audited.
4. No risk-of-bias assessments were supplied.
5. Distinct study labels suggest independent comparisons, but trial-level identifiers and covariance data were unavailable to definitively exclude shared inactive controls.
6. The *Q*-test *p*-value was supplied as 0.0, which should be understood as numerical rounding or underflow, not an exact probability of zero.

# Limitations

First, the search and selection process was incompletely documented. Database coverage, search dates, language restrictions, grey-literature searching, duplicate handling, and full-text exclusion reasons were unavailable. Review completeness cannot therefore be independently assessed.

Second, study-level clinical and methodological characteristics were absent. This prevents a conventional included-studies table, risk-of-bias appraisal, sensitivity analyses based on outcome instrument or analysis population, and investigation of the substantial heterogeneity.

Third, only seven effects were available. Publication-bias tests have low power and unstable operating characteristics with such a small evidence base, while the significant Egger result may be confounded by heterogeneity.

Fourth, the pooled estimate combines standardised scores. Although this permits synthesis across PTSD instruments, Hedges' *g* is less directly interpretable clinically than a native-scale mean difference, and no minimally important difference was provided.

Fifth, the immediate post-treatment endpoint does not show whether gains persist. It may also be affected by differential attrition if some trials reported completer rather than intention-to-treat data; the relevant analysis populations were not identified.

Sixth, the evidence concerns inactive controls. The estimate should not be interpreted as demonstrating superiority over another bona fide psychological treatment.

Finally, dependency from multi-arm studies could not be definitively audited. The supplied effects had unique study identifiers and therefore gave no visible indication of a shared control, but trial-level identifiers were unavailable.

# Conclusion

Across seven randomised comparisons comprising 500 analysed participants, psychosocial interventions for rape- or sexual-assault-related PTSD symptoms reduced immediate post-treatment total PTSD symptom severity relative to inactive controls on average. The REML pooled Hedges' *g* was **−0.815564715049413** (95% CI **−1.456 to −0.175**; *p* = **0.012578782811237582**), favoring psychosocial intervention.

Confidence in the magnitude and generalisability of this average effect is constrained by very high heterogeneity (*I*² = **90.47%**), significant funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger's *p* = **0.0339**), only seven effects, and incomplete study-level and screening information. The findings support an immediate average benefit but not a uniform effect across interventions or settings. Future syntheses should provide fully traceable trial-level extraction, account explicitly for correlated comparisons in any multi-arm trials, report risk of bias, examine prespecified moderators, and evaluate longer-term outcomes and active comparators.
