# Psychosocial Interventions versus Inactive Control for Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder Symptoms in Adults Who Experienced Rape or Sexual Assault in Adulthood: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of End-of-Treatment Outcomes

## Abstract

**Background.** Rape and sexual assault sustained in adulthood carry among the highest conditional risks of post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) of any traumatic event. A broad family of psychosocial interventions — trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) including prolonged exposure (PE), cognitive processing therapy (CPT) and stress inoculation training (SIT); behavioural and exposure-based therapies; eye-movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR), narrative exposure, memory-reconsolidation and neurofeedback approaches; and brief or low-intensity programmes such as psychoeducational videos and computerised self-help — has been evaluated against inactive comparators in this population. We quantified the end-of-treatment effect on continuously measured PTSD symptom severity.

**Methods.** We systematically identified randomised and quasi-randomised trials comparing any eligible psychosocial intervention with an inactive control (wait-list, usual care, no treatment or assessment only) in adults whose index trauma was rape or sexual assault experienced in adulthood. The outcome was PTSD symptom severity on a continuous total-score scale at the first post-treatment assessment, one measure per trial, preferring clinician-administered instruments (e.g. CAPS) over the trial's primary self-report scale (e.g. PDS, PCL, PSS-SR, IES-R). Hedges' g was computed from arm-level means, standard deviations and sample sizes and pooled using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. In the sign convention used throughout this report, **negative values of g indicate lower PTSD symptom severity in the intervention arm** and therefore favour the psychosocial intervention. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ²; small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Fifteen study records were retained through full-text screening; ten contributed extractable end-of-treatment continuous PTSD data and were pooled (k = 10; N = 619 participants). The pooled effect was **Hedges' g = -0.929 (95% CI -1.537 to -0.320; p = 0.0028)**, a result that **favours the psychosocial intervention** over inactive control, corresponding to a large reduction in PTSD symptom severity at end of treatment. Between-study heterogeneity was very high and statistically significant (Q(9) = 88.856, p < 0.001; I² = 91.45%; τ² = 0.854, τ = 0.924), and the approximate 95% prediction interval (-2.84 to 0.98) includes zero, indicating that a null or even unfavourable effect cannot be excluded in some future settings. Individual study effects ranged from g = -2.792 (95% CI -3.798 to -1.787) to g = +0.392 (95% CI 0.026 to 0.758); seven of ten estimates favoured the intervention and three favoured the control condition. Effect magnitude was strongly patterned by sample size, with the three trials reporting results in the direction of the control arm being among the larger studies and the four largest effects coming from the four smallest trials (n = 25–38). Egger's regression indicated highly significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = 1.366, p = 0.0005), while trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted g = -0.929); overall small-study-effect risk was rated **high**.

**Conclusions.** Psychosocial interventions, as a heterogeneous class, are associated with a statistically significant and large average reduction in PTSD symptom severity at end of treatment relative to inactive control in adult survivors of rape and sexual assault. However, the certainty of this estimate is substantially limited by extreme between-study heterogeneity, a prediction interval crossing the null, evidence of small-study effects/funnel asymmetry rated high risk, and the inability of the available data to separate high-intensity trauma-focused therapies from brief low-intensity programmes. The pooled value should be regarded as a potentially inflated upper bound rather than a precise estimate of expected clinical benefit.

# 1. Introduction

Rape and sexual assault experienced in adulthood are associated with one of the highest conditional probabilities of developing post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) of any index trauma, with lifetime PTSD prevalence estimates in survivors substantially exceeding those following accidents, disasters or witnessed violence. The resulting symptom burden — re-experiencing, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and hyperarousal — is frequently chronic, is accompanied by high rates of depression, substance misuse and suicidality, and is associated with marked functional impairment.

A broad range of psychosocial interventions has been developed or adapted for this population. These include: (i) trauma-focused cognitive behavioural therapies, notably prolonged exposure (PE), cognitive processing therapy (CPT) and stress inoculation training (SIT); (ii) behavioural and exposure-based therapies more broadly; (iii) eye-movement desensitisation and reprocessing (EMDR), narrative exposure therapy, memory-reconsolidation paradigms and neurofeedback; and (iv) brief or low-intensity interventions delivered soon after assault or at low clinician cost, such as psychoeducational videos and computerised or self-directed programmes. Although general PTSD guidelines recommend trauma-focused psychological therapy as first-line treatment, the evidence base specific to adult-onset sexual assault — where shame, disclosure barriers, forensic processes and revictimisation risk differ materially from other trauma populations — has not consistently been synthesised in isolation.

This report addresses a single, precisely specified question: **in adults who experienced rape or sexual assault in adulthood, what is the effect of psychosocial interventions, compared with an inactive control condition, on PTSD symptom severity measured on a continuous scale immediately after treatment?** By restricting the comparator to inactive controls (wait-list, usual care, no treatment, assessment only) and the timepoint to the first post-treatment assessment, the review isolates the maximal, unattenuated signal of intervention efficacy before natural remission and differential attrition at follow-up complicate interpretation.

# 2. Methods: Search and Screening

## 2.1 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults (≥18 years) whose index traumatic event was rape or sexual assault sustained in adulthood. Trials of mixed-trauma samples were eligible only where adult sexual assault survivors constituted the target population or where data specific to this subgroup were extractable.

**Intervention.** Any psychosocial intervention within the prespecified families: CBT (including PE, CPT, SIT and combinations); behavioural or exposure-based therapy; EMDR, memory-reconsolidation, neurofeedback or narrative exposure approaches; or brief/low-intensity interventions including psychoeducational video and computerised programmes.

**Comparator.** An inactive control: wait-list, treatment as usual, no treatment, or assessment-only/minimal-attention control. Trials whose only comparator was an active psychological or pharmacological treatment were not eligible for this analysis.

**Outcome.** PTSD symptom severity on a continuous scale at the first post-treatment assessment (end of treatment). A single measure was taken per trial, with clinician-administered instruments (e.g. Clinician-Administered PTSD Scale, CAPS) preferred over self-report; where no clinician measure was available, the trial's designated primary self-report PTSD scale was used (e.g. PDS, PCL, PSS-SR, IES-R). Only total scores were extracted; symptom-cluster subscales were not used. End-of-treatment values were preferred over change scores. Where only standard errors were reported, standard deviations were reconstructed as SD = SE × √n. Follow-up assessments at 3, 6 and 12 months were explicitly outside the scope of this synthesis.

**Design.** Randomised and quasi-randomised controlled trials reporting arm-level means, standard deviations (or convertible dispersion statistics) and sample sizes.

## 2.2 Information sources and screening

Records were identified through systematic database searching and retrieval of candidate trial reports, followed by title/abstract screening and full-text assessment against the criteria above. Fifteen study records survived full-text screening as topically and design-eligible. Of these, **ten provided end-of-treatment continuous PTSD severity data in a form permitting computation of a standardised mean difference** and constituted the analytic set. The remaining five eligible records did not contribute a pooled effect estimate, because the required arm-level mean/SD/n triplet for the first post-treatment assessment of a total PTSD score was not reported or recoverable (for example, outcomes reported only as responder proportions, only graphically, only as subscale scores, or only at follow-up). These records are retained in the review narrative but are absent from all quantitative results reported below; this is a data-availability limitation and is addressed in Section 10 and Section 11.

## 2.3 Effect size computation

For each trial, Hedges' g was computed as the difference between intervention and control arm means on the extracted PTSD scale, divided by the pooled within-group standard deviation, with the small-sample correction factor J applied. **Sign convention: g is computed as (intervention − control) on the PTSD severity scale, so negative values denote lower post-treatment symptom severity in the intervention arm and therefore favour the psychosocial intervention; positive values favour the inactive control.** Readers should note that this is the raw statistical convention (treatment minus control) and that, for a symptom-severity outcome, the lower raw value is the clinically better one. Throughout this report the arm favoured by each result is stated explicitly rather than left to be inferred from the sign.

## 2.4 Synthesis methods

Effect sizes were pooled under a random-effects model, with the between-study variance τ² estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) and inverse-variance weighting of study-level estimates. The pooled statistic is reported on its native computational scale, Hedges' g; native pooling on the requested effect measure was used (no standardised fallback was required for this analysis). Heterogeneity was characterised using Cochran's Q, the I² statistic, τ² and τ, with an approximate 95% prediction interval derived from τ² and the standard error of the pooled estimate. Small-study effects and potential publication bias were examined using Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

# 3. Included Studies

Ten trials contributing a total of **619 randomised participants** entered the quantitative synthesis. Sample sizes per trial ranged from 25 to 117 (median ≈ 48.5), with a mean of approximately 62 participants per trial; six of the ten trials randomised fewer than 65 participants and four randomised fewer than 40. The analytic set therefore comprises predominantly small, single-site efficacy trials, with only three trials exceeding 100 participants (n = 109, 117 and 105).

Study identifiers in this report are the de-identified pipeline labels (R1–R12) assigned during extraction; each label corresponds to one independent trial contributing exactly one effect size, so there is no double-counting of participants or of multiple outcome measures from the same trial. The intervention arms span the full prespecified spectrum from multi-session, high-intensity, clinician-delivered trauma-focused therapy (PE, CPT, SIT, EMDR, narrative exposure) to single-session or self-directed brief interventions (psychoeducational video, computerised programmes). Comparators are uniformly inactive: wait-list, usual care, no treatment or assessment-only. Because intervention-family coding, delivery format, session count, time since assault and outcome-instrument type were not available as structured covariates in the extracted dataset, formal subgroup analysis across these dimensions could not be performed (see Section 7).

# 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A single outcome/timepoint group was analysed (PTSD symptom severity on a continuous scale at the first post-treatment assessment); no separate outcome or timepoint groups required independent pooling.

**Pooled result (random-effects, REML; k = 10; N = 619):**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Hedges' g** (native pooling used; no fallback measure) |
| Pooled effect | **-0.929** |
| 95% confidence interval | **-1.537 to -0.320** |
| p-value | **0.0028** |
| Favours | **Psychosocial intervention (CBT/PE/CPT/SIT, exposure-based, EMDR, narrative exposure, neurofeedback, memory reconsolidation, or brief/low-intensity programmes)** |
| Q (df = 9) | 88.856 (p < 0.001) |
| I² | 91.45% |
| τ² (τ) | 0.854 (0.924) |
| Approx. 95% prediction interval | -2.84 to 0.98 |

The pooled Hedges' g of **-0.929 (95% CI -1.537 to -0.320; p = 0.0028)** indicates that, averaged across trials, participants receiving a psychosocial intervention reported or were rated as having substantially **lower** PTSD symptom severity at the first post-treatment assessment than participants in inactive control conditions. The result **favours the psychosocial intervention**. By conventional benchmarks this is a large standardised effect; on a clinician-administered instrument such as the CAPS, with a typical between-person SD in the region of 20 points, g ≈ -0.93 corresponds to an approximate 18–19 point advantage for the intervention arm at end of treatment, which would ordinarily be regarded as clinically meaningful. The confidence interval nonetheless spans effects from small-to-moderate (g ≈ -0.32) to very large (g ≈ -1.54), so the magnitude of average benefit is estimated imprecisely even though its direction is statistically secure at the α = 0.05 level.

Because τ² (0.854) is large relative to the within-study sampling variances (which range from approximately 0.035 to 0.263), random-effects weights are nearly uniform across trials, varying only between roughly 8.6% and 10.8%. The pooled estimate is consequently close to an unweighted average of the ten study effects (arithmetic mean g = -0.984), meaning that the three large trials (n = 105–117) exert little more influence on the summary than the smallest trial (n = 25). This is a direct and important consequence of the heterogeneity documented in Section 6 and should be borne in mind when interpreting the summary value.

The forest plot is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. A cumulative meta-analysis, showing how the running pooled estimate evolves as studies are added, is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`.

# 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **extreme**:

- **Cochran's Q(9) = 88.856, p < 0.001** — the hypothesis of a common underlying effect is decisively rejected.
- **I² = 91.45%** — approximately 91% of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to genuine between-study differences rather than sampling error. This sits far above the conventional threshold (I² > 75%) for "considerable" heterogeneity.
- **τ² = 0.854, τ = 0.924** — the estimated standard deviation of true effects across trials is close to one full standard-deviation unit of PTSD severity, which is of the same order as the pooled effect itself.
- **Approximate 95% prediction interval: -2.84 to 0.98.** This interval, which describes the plausible true effect in a new, exchangeable trial, **includes zero and extends into the region favouring the control condition**. In other words, although the average effect across the existing trials favours the psychosocial intervention, the data do not support confident prediction that a newly conducted trial in this population would show benefit.

The heterogeneity summary visualisation is at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

The sources of this dispersion are readily apparent from Table 1 but cannot be definitively adjudicated with the available covariates. Candidate explanations include: (i) genuine differences in intervention intensity and mechanism, since the analytic set combines multi-session trauma-focused exposure therapies with single-session psychoeducational videos and computerised self-help — interventions that would not be expected to share a common effect; (ii) differences in outcome instrument and assessor blinding, since clinician-administered and self-report scales were mixed and unblinded self-report in wait-list designs is susceptible to expectancy inflation; (iii) differences in time since assault and baseline symptom severity, which determine the scope for natural remission in the control arm; (iv) differences in control-arm management (true no-treatment versus usual care that may include some clinical contact); and (v) small-study effects, discussed in Section 8. Given I² = 91.45%, the pooled estimate should be read as the centre of a wide distribution of true effects, not as a single transportable parameter.

# 7. Moderators

No prespecified moderator variables (intervention family, session count, delivery modality, outcome-instrument type, assessor blinding, time since index assault, mean age, or risk-of-bias domain ratings) were available as structured covariates in the extracted dataset. **Formal meta-regression and subgroup analyses across clinical and methodological moderators could therefore not be performed, and none are reported here.** This is a material gap given the magnitude of unexplained heterogeneity, and it is stated plainly rather than substituted with post-hoc narrative grouping.

The one moderator dimension that could be examined with the available data is **study size**, which functions as a proxy for both precision and methodological scale. The relationship is displayed in the bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, in which marker area is proportional to study weight. The pattern is pronounced:

- The four smallest trials (n = 25, 30, 36, 38) produced the four largest benefits in favour of the intervention (g = -1.185, -2.792, -1.866, -1.915; mean ≈ -1.94).
- The three largest trials (n = 105, 109, 117) produced a markedly more modest and internally inconsistent set of estimates (g = -0.656, -1.133, +0.392; mean ≈ -0.47).
- The two trials whose point estimates were essentially null (R10, g = +0.051, n = 42; R11, g = +0.105, n = 64) were of intermediate size.

The gradient is not perfectly monotonic — R1 (n = 109) reports a large benefit of g = -1.133, and R3 (n = 117) is the only trial favouring the control arm at conventional significance — but the overall association between smaller samples and larger apparent benefit is strong and is corroborated quantitatively by Egger's test (Section 8). Because sample size is confounded with intervention type, era, setting and methodological rigour, this association should be interpreted as a warning signal about the reliability of the pooled estimate rather than as evidence that small trials deliver genuinely superior therapy.

# 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Two complementary diagnostics were applied:

- **Egger's regression test:** intercept = **1.366**, **p = 0.0005**. This indicates highly significant funnel-plot asymmetry, with smaller/less precise trials systematically reporting larger benefits in favour of the intervention.
- **Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill:** **k₀ = 0 studies imputed**; adjusted pooled estimate **g = -0.9287**, i.e. numerically identical to the unadjusted estimate.
- **Overall small-study-effect/publication-bias risk rating: HIGH.**

These two results are discordant and must be interpreted jointly rather than selectively. Egger's test detects a strong precision–effect association; trim-and-fill, however, imputes nothing. This combination is common and does not vindicate the evidence base. Trim-and-fill assumes that bias takes the specific form of studies suppressed from one tail of a symmetric funnel; when asymmetry instead arises from genuine between-study heterogeneity, from small trials differing systematically in design (shorter, more selected samples, unblinded self-report, greater researcher allegiance), or from an asymmetry pattern that does not match the rank-based suppression model, the algorithm can return k₀ = 0 despite clear asymmetry. **The null imputation should therefore not be read as evidence of absence of bias.** Moreover, trim-and-fill performs poorly under high heterogeneity, and with I² = 91.45% its output here carries very little information.

Both the number of studies (k = 10) and the dominance of τ² limit the precision of any bias adjustment. The appropriate conclusion is that the pooled effect of g = -0.929 is **plausibly inflated** by small-study effects and should be treated as an upper bound pending sensitivity analyses that were not available in the current pipeline — specifically limit meta-analysis, PET-PEESE or selection models, and restriction of the synthesis to trials above a prespecified size threshold or at low risk of bias. The internal evidence points the same way: restricting attention to the three trials with n > 100 yields an average effect in the region of g ≈ -0.47, roughly half the pooled estimate, with one of those three favouring the control condition.

# 9. Discussion

Across ten randomised comparisons involving 619 adults whose index trauma was rape or sexual assault sustained in adulthood, psychosocial interventions as a class were associated with a statistically significant and large average reduction in PTSD symptom severity at the first post-treatment assessment relative to inactive control (Hedges' g = -0.929, 95% CI -1.537 to -0.320, p = 0.0028, favouring the psychosocial intervention). The direction of this finding is consistent with the wider PTSD treatment literature and with clinical-guideline recommendations that trauma-focused psychological therapy be offered as first-line treatment. Seven of ten trials showed statistically significant benefit, and the signal is not attributable to any single trial, since the near-uniform random-effects weights mean that no study contributes more than about 11% of the pooled estimate.

Three features of the evidence, however, temper confidence considerably.

First, the **heterogeneity is extreme** (I² = 91.45%, τ² = 0.854) and the prediction interval (-2.84 to 0.98) crosses the null. The analytic set deliberately pools intervention families that differ by an order of magnitude in intensity — from twelve-session prolonged exposure or cognitive processing therapy delivered by trained clinicians, to a single psychoeducational video or an unguided computerised module. On mechanistic grounds these should not be expected to produce a common effect, and the presence of two near-null estimates (g = +0.051, +0.105) alongside estimates exceeding g = -1.8 is consistent with a mixture of genuinely efficacious and genuinely inert interventions being averaged together. The pooled value is therefore best read as a summary of a *class* under a *broad* definition, not as an estimate of what any specific therapy achieves. The practical implication is that the clinically useful question — which of these interventions works, and for whom — remains unanswered by this synthesis.

Second, the **comparator is inactive by design**, which maximises the apparent effect. Wait-list and assessment-only controls do not control for attention, therapeutic alliance, expectancy or the passage of structured time; in unblinded trials using self-report PTSD instruments, wait-list comparisons are known to yield systematically larger effect sizes than comparisons against active or attention-matched conditions. The estimate reported here should accordingly not be transported to clinical decisions framed as "therapy A versus therapy B", nor read as evidence of a specific mechanism.

Third, **small-study effects are strongly indicated** (Egger's p = 0.0005) and the overall bias risk was rated high. The precision–effect gradient documented in Section 7 — with the four smallest trials averaging g ≈ -1.94 and the three largest averaging g ≈ -0.47 — suggests that the true average benefit, if a single average is even a meaningful construct here, is likely smaller than -0.929. A plausible, though unverified, reading of the data is that the robust, replicable component of the effect lies in the moderate range (perhaps g ≈ -0.4 to -0.7), consistent with what larger and more methodologically rigorous trials of trauma-focused therapy report in other populations, with the remainder reflecting small-sample volatility, selective reporting and design-related inflation.

Taken together, these considerations support a qualified conclusion: psychosocial intervention is better than nothing for PTSD after adult sexual assault, with reasonable confidence in the direction of effect but low confidence in its magnitude.

# 10. Data Quality and Completeness Notes

No formal validation flags were raised by the extraction pipeline for this analysis. Three data-completeness observations nonetheless warrant plain statement:

1. **Five of fifteen retained study records did not contribute an effect size.** Fifteen records passed topical and design screening, but only ten yielded the arm-level mean, standard deviation and sample size required at the first post-treatment assessment for a total continuous PTSD score. The five non-contributing records are therefore invisible in every quantitative result above. Their exclusion is on data-availability grounds, not on judged quality, and if their results differ systematically from those of the contributing trials the pooled estimate will be correspondingly biased.
2. **One effect size per trial; no double-counting.** Each of the ten contributing records supplied exactly one effect estimate, selected by the prespecified instrument hierarchy (clinician-administered preferred over self-report; total scores only; no subscales; end-of-treatment values preferred over change scores). No participants or outcome measures are counted twice, and no multi-arm trial contributed more than one comparison.
3. **Effect measure confirmed on its native scale.** The pooled statistic is Hedges' g, computed and pooled on its native scale at explicit user request; native pooling was used, so no standardised fallback measure was substituted and no measure conflict arose. A single outcome/timepoint group was analysed, so no results required separate pooling by effect measure, and no group was set aside for insufficient data.
4. **Moderator covariates were unavailable**, so all subgroup and meta-regression analyses that would ordinarily be used to explain I² = 91.45% could not be run (Section 7).

# 11. Limitations

1. **Extreme, unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 91.45% and τ² = 0.854 mean the pooled estimate describes the centre of a very wide distribution of true effects; the prediction interval (-2.84 to 0.98) includes no effect and effects favouring control.
2. **High risk of small-study effects/publication bias.** Egger's test was highly significant (p = 0.0005). Trim-and-fill's null imputation (k₀ = 0) does not refute this and is unreliable at this level of heterogeneity. No limit meta-analysis, PET-PEESE or selection-model sensitivity analysis was available.
3. **Clinically disparate interventions pooled together.** High-intensity trauma-focused therapies and brief low-intensity psychoeducational or computerised programmes were analysed as a single class because intervention-family coding was unavailable, which almost certainly contributes to heterogeneity and renders the pooled estimate difficult to apply clinically.
4. **Inactive comparators only.** Wait-list, usual-care and assessment-only controls inflate apparent efficacy relative to attention-matched or active comparators; the estimate is not a measure of specific therapeutic ingredient effects.
5. **Mixed and partly unblinded outcome assessment.** Clinician-administered and self-report instruments were combined; in unblinded wait-list trials, self-reported PTSD severity is susceptible to demand characteristics and expectancy effects.
6. **Small and uneven sample sizes.** Six of ten trials randomised fewer than 65 participants and four fewer than 40, producing imprecise individual estimates and heightened vulnerability to chance findings and outlier influence.
7. **Incomplete outcome capture.** Five eligible records could not be pooled; dropout and intention-to-treat handling, adverse events, remission/response rates, and comorbid depression outcomes were outside the extraction scope.
8. **Short-term outcomes only.** By design, only the first post-treatment assessment was analysed; the durability of benefit at 3, 6 and 12 months — arguably the more decisive clinical question — is not addressed.
9. **No formal risk-of-bias synthesis or GRADE certainty rating** was available for incorporation, so study-level quality could not be used either as a moderator or as a basis for sensitivity analysis.
10. **No formal influence diagnostics.** Leave-one-out and outlier-removal analyses were not produced by the pipeline; the cumulative plot provides only an indirect view of estimate stability.

# 12. Conclusion

In adults whose index trauma was rape or sexual assault sustained in adulthood, psychosocial interventions — spanning trauma-focused CBT (prolonged exposure, cognitive processing therapy, stress inoculation training), behavioural and exposure-based therapies, EMDR, narrative exposure, memory-reconsolidation and neurofeedback approaches, and brief or low-intensity programmes — produced a statistically significant and large average reduction in PTSD symptom severity at the end of treatment compared with inactive control (Hedges' g = -0.929, 95% CI -1.537 to -0.320, p = 0.0028; k = 10, N = 619), a result that favours the psychosocial intervention.

This conclusion is directionally consistent with established guidance but is accompanied by substantial uncertainty about magnitude. Heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 91.45%; τ² = 0.854; Q(9) = 88.856, p < 0.001), the 95% prediction interval (-2.84 to 0.98) crosses the null, funnel asymmetry was highly significant (Egger's p = 0.0005) with an overall bias risk rated high, and the evidence base consists largely of small trials comparing heterogeneous interventions against no-treatment conditions. Restricting attention to the three largest trials suggests an average effect roughly half the pooled magnitude.

For practice, the evidence supports offering structured psychosocial treatment rather than nothing to adult survivors of sexual assault presenting with PTSD, while cautioning that the expected benefit is probably more modest than g ≈ -0.9. For research, the priorities are clear: adequately powered trials (n ≥ 150) of individual, clearly specified interventions; blinded clinician-administered outcome assessment; attention-matched or active comparators in place of wait-list; prospective registration with full reporting of end-of-treatment means, SDs and n per arm; and consistent reporting of maintained effects at 6–12 months. Until such trials are available, the pooled estimate reported here should be regarded as a potentially inflated upper bound on the efficacy of psychosocial intervention in this population rather than a precise estimate of expected clinical benefit.
