# Manual Therapy and Prescribed Exercise for Pain in Adults With Lateral Elbow Pain: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Manual therapy and prescribed exercise are commonly used for lateral elbow pain, but their effect on pain at completion of treatment remains uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or their combination, compared with minimal or no intervention, on mean pain intensity at the first end-of-treatment assessment.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons enrolled adults with lateral elbow pain and evaluated manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or both against wait-and-see, advice-only, non-active stretching/advice, placebo or sham, or usual general-practice care. The target outcome was mean pain intensity at the first assessment after completion of treatment, generally 6–8 weeks. Seven effect sizes from 12 studies in the evidence set contributed to the quantitative synthesis (545 participants). A random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was reported. The designated effect measure was the Mean Difference (MD), oriented as treatment minus control, so values below zero favor active treatment.

**Results:** The pooled Mean Difference was −1.1118851659486482 (95% CI −1.877 to −0.346; p=0.004416377228993557; k=7; n=545), favoring manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or their combination. The supplied heterogeneity outputs were τ²=0.0, I²=0.0%, Q=18.448, and Q-test p=0.0052; these values are internally inconsistent and should not be interpreted as jointly valid. Egger’s intercept was 0.2262 (p=0.2317), but trim-and-fill estimated three potentially missing studies and produced an adjusted estimate of −0.0325. Overall publication-bias risk was rated high.

**Conclusions:** The reported synthesis suggests lower end-of-treatment pain with active treatment than with minimal or no intervention. Confidence in the magnitude is limited by high publication-bias risk, incomplete confirmation of a common 0–10 scale, mismatch between the listed study-level standardized effects and the pooled MD, and inconsistent heterogeneity statistics. The pooled estimate should therefore be regarded as provisional pending verification and harmonization of the underlying extraction and analysis.

## Introduction

Lateral elbow pain—also termed tennis elbow or lateral epicondylalgia—is a common musculoskeletal condition characterized by pain around the lateral epicondyle, often aggravated by gripping and resisted wrist or finger extension. Conservative care frequently includes joint manipulation or mobilisation, soft-tissue techniques, prescribed exercise, or combinations of these approaches. Because symptoms may improve over time and control interventions vary from waiting to advice, sham procedures, or usual primary care, treatment effects should be evaluated against clearly defined minimal or no-intervention comparators.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among adults with lateral elbow pain, what is the effect of manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or combined manual therapy and exercise, compared with minimal or no intervention, on mean pain intensity at the first end-of-treatment assessment?

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

The population of interest was adults with lateral elbow pain. Eligible active interventions were:

- manual therapy, including manipulation, mobilisation, or soft-tissue techniques;
- prescribed exercise; or
- combined manual therapy and prescribed exercise.

Eligible comparators were minimal or no intervention, including wait-and-see, advice only, stretching or advice without an active exercise program, placebo or sham, or usual general-practice care.

The prespecified outcome was mean pain intensity on a 0–10 scale at the first assessment at the end of the intervention period, typically 6–8 weeks. The intended hierarchy was the trial’s primary pain measure when identified and, otherwise, the most relevant measure among pain at rest, pain with activity, or pain during maximum grip. End-of-intervention values were preferred. Change scores were eligible only when endpoint values were unavailable and were to be used consistently for both arms within a trial. Adjusted between-group estimates could be used when these were the only available data. Outcomes available only in figures and later follow-up assessments were outside the target analysis, as were disability, grip strength, quality of life, treatment success, withdrawals, and adverse events.

## Effect Sizes

The designated meta-analytic statistic was the **Mean Difference (MD)**, calculated with the raw sign convention treatment minus control. For pain intensity, an MD below zero represents less pain in the active-treatment group. Consistent with the supplied `favors` designation, the pooled result favors manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or their combination.

The source specified an explicit user request for MD and marked native pooling as used. Nevertheless, only six of seven rows reportedly had a stated instrument range, and no 0–100 to 0–10 rescaling was applied. The analysis therefore pooled values in the papers’ reported raw units. Although the review question prespecified a 0–10 pain scale, the available metadata do not establish that all seven effects were expressed on that common scale. Accordingly, the pooled numerical result cannot be described with confidence as an exact 0–10-point difference.

No arm-level means, SDs, or group-specific sample sizes were supplied. It was also not reported whether any comparison used change scores, adjusted estimates, imputed pain-specific sample sizes, or SDs converted from standard errors. Compliance with those extraction rules could not therefore be independently checked.

## Meta-analytic Results

Using a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), the **pooled Mean Difference was −1.1118851659486482** (95% CI **−1.877 to −0.346**; **p=0.004416377228993557**; **k=7**, **n=545**). The supplied direction classification indicates that this result favors manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or combined manual therapy and exercise over minimal or no intervention.

If—and only if—all included pain measurements were validly harmonized to a 0–10 scale, this estimate would correspond to approximately 1.11 points less pain at the first end-of-treatment assessment. Because the common scale was not confirmed for one of seven rows and no rescaling was performed, that clinical interpretation remains uncertain.

## Heterogeneity

The supplied heterogeneity estimates were:

- **I²=0.0%**;
- **τ²=0.0**;
- **Q=18.448**; and
- **Q-test p=0.0052**.

For seven effects, Cochran’s Q ordinarily has 6 degrees of freedom. A Q statistic of 18.448 with p=0.0052 indicates statistically detectable dispersion beyond sampling error, whereas I²=0.0% and τ²=0.0 indicate no estimated between-study heterogeneity. These values are internally inconsistent under standard meta-analytic calculations and should not be jointly interpreted as evidence of homogeneity. In particular, the reported I² is not arithmetically compatible with the supplied Q and k under the conventional formula. The analysis files and model output require verification.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression yielded an intercept of **0.2262** with **p=0.2317**. This test did not detect statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry. However, with only seven effects, regression-based asymmetry tests have low power and a nonsignificant result does not exclude selective reporting or small-study effects.

Trim-and-fill estimated **three** potentially missing studies and returned an adjusted estimate of **−0.0325**. The source named this output `trim_fill_g`, whereas the primary synthesis was labelled an MD. Its metric is therefore not demonstrably the same as that of the pooled MD and it should not be interpreted as a directly comparable adjusted raw-scale effect without clarification. Nevertheless, its proximity to the null indicates substantial sensitivity under the trim-and-fill assumptions.

The overall publication-bias risk was rated **high**. Taken together, the nonsignificant Egger test does not offset the trim-and-fill result, small number of comparisons, or high overall risk rating. The primary pooled estimate should be interpreted cautiously.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

The reported REML synthesis of seven comparisons and 545 participants found a pooled Mean Difference of −1.1118851659486482 (95% CI −1.877 to −0.346), classified as favoring manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or their combination over minimal or no intervention at the first end-of-treatment assessment. The nominal test of the pooled effect was statistically significant (p=0.004416377228993557).

The apparent treatment benefit is clinically interpretable only if the underlying pain outcomes were validly placed on a common scale. The review question specified a 0–10 scale, but the metadata state that the instrument range was available for only six of seven rows and that no rescaling was performed. Thus, the pooled estimate may reflect mixed raw units. Moreover, the seven displayed study effects are standardized values ranging from −0.791190 to 0.259249, whereas the pooled result is labelled a raw Mean Difference of −1.1118851659486482. A raw MD of that magnitude cannot be reproduced or checked from the displayed standardized effects.

### Certainty and interpretation

Several features weaken confidence in the result. First, no study-level risk-of-bias assessment was provided. Second, the high publication-bias risk and trim-and-fill result raise concern that the pooled benefit may be exaggerated. Third, the heterogeneity outputs are contradictory: Q and its p value suggest excess dispersion, while I² and τ² are reported as zero. Fourth, details needed to assess clinical comparability—such as the intervention subtype, control condition, outcome instrument, pain context, and precise end-of-treatment timing—were absent.

Accordingly, the synthesis provides a statistical signal in favor of active treatment but does not establish a robust or precisely quantified effect on a verified 0–10 pain scale. The result should not be used alone to infer that all manual-therapy or exercise approaches are equally effective.

### Implications for practice and research

For clinical practice, the point estimate is consistent with lower short-term pain after active care than after minimal or no intervention, but treatment choice should also account for intervention burden, patient preferences, feasibility, safety, and evidence on function and longer-term outcomes, none of which were evaluated here.

Future syntheses should obtain arm-level endpoint means, SDs, and pain-specific analysed sample sizes; convert all eligible measures to a documented common 0–10 scale; keep endpoint and change-score analyses separate where necessary; and identify adjusted estimates explicitly. Trial reports should be linked to prevent double-counting. Analyses should also separate manual therapy, exercise, and combined treatment when enough studies are available, and should investigate comparator type and pain-measure context. Recalculation of Q, I², τ², and the pooled model from the verified study-level inputs is essential.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` object was supplied, the reported results contain material validation issues:

1. **Study accounting is incomplete:** 12 studies were identified, but only seven effect sizes were analysed; the status and reasons for exclusion of the other five studies were not reported.
2. **Scale harmonization is incomplete:** the pain-instrument range was stated for only six of seven rows, and no 0–100 to 0–10 rescaling was applied. A common 0–10 metric is therefore unconfirmed.
3. **Study-level and pooled metrics differ:** the individual effects were supplied as Hedges-type standardized values, while the pooled result was labelled a raw Mean Difference. The relationship between these inputs and the pooled estimate is not documented.
4. **Heterogeneity statistics conflict:** Q=18.448 with p=0.0052 is inconsistent with I²=0.0% and τ²=0.0 under standard calculations for k=7.
5. **Trim-and-fill metric is unclear:** the adjusted value was supplied in a field labelled `trim_fill_g`, whereas the primary model used MD. Direct numerical comparison may therefore be invalid.
6. **Extraction cannot be audited:** arm-level means, SDs, analysed sample sizes, endpoint/change status, adjustment status, and any SE-to-SD conversions were not provided.

These issues warrant correction or clarification before the pooled estimate is treated as definitive.

## Limitations

This report is limited by the aggregate and partially inconsistent information supplied. No full search strategy, screening log, PRISMA flow counts, study citations, intervention details, outcome definitions, or risk-of-bias assessments were available. The analysis could not verify that the selected assessment was the first true end-of-treatment time point in every trial, that each outcome represented the trial’s primary pain measure, or that endpoint and change values were not mixed within comparisons.

The small number of effects limits estimation of between-study variance and assessment of publication bias. Egger’s test is underpowered at k=7, while trim-and-fill relies on assumptions that may not hold when asymmetry is caused by heterogeneity or methodological differences. No sensitivity analyses, influence diagnostics, prediction interval, subgroup analyses, or certainty-of-evidence assessment were supplied. Finally, the lack of confirmed common measurement units and the contradiction among heterogeneity outputs directly limit confidence in the pooled estimate.

## Conclusion

The reported random-effects analysis found a pooled Mean Difference of **−1.1118851659486482** (95% CI **−1.877 to −0.346**; p=**0.004416377228993557**) across seven comparisons and 545 participants, favoring manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or their combination over minimal or no intervention for pain at the first end-of-treatment assessment. However, the magnitude cannot confidently be interpreted on a common 0–10 scale. High publication-bias risk, incomplete study accounting, discordance between study-level and pooled effect metrics, and internally inconsistent heterogeneity statistics substantially reduce confidence. Verification and reanalysis of harmonized arm-level data are required before firm clinical conclusions are drawn.
