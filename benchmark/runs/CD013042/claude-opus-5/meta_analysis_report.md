# Manual Therapy, Exercise, or Both versus Minimal or No Intervention for Pain Intensity in Adults with Lateral Elbow Pain (Tennis Elbow): A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of End-of-Intervention Pain

## Abstract

**Background.** Lateral elbow pain (lateral epicondylalgia, "tennis elbow") is a common, often self-limiting musculoskeletal condition for which manual therapy and prescribed exercise are widely recommended first-line physical treatments. Whether these treatments reduce pain more than minimal or no intervention at the end of a typical 6–8-week treatment course remains contested.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of manual therapy (manipulation, mobilisation, soft-tissue techniques), prescribed exercise, or combined manual therapy plus exercise, compared with minimal or no intervention (wait-and-see, advice/stretching only, placebo/sham, or usual GP care), on mean pain intensity measured at the first assessment at the end of the intervention period in adults with lateral elbow pain.

**Methods.** Randomised controlled trials comparing an eligible active physical treatment arm with a minimal/no-intervention arm were sought. The outcome was mean pain intensity at end of intervention, expressed on a 0–10 scale (VAS 0–100 mm converted by dividing means and SDs by 10; NRS 0–10 used directly), with preference for each trial's primary pain measure. End-of-treatment values were preferred; change-from-baseline scores or adjusted between-group differences with confidence intervals were used where these were the only data reported. Trials reporting pain only in figures were excluded from pooling, and later follow-up time points were not used. Data were pooled with a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML). The pre-specified effect measure was the Mean Difference (MD) in the trials' raw pain units; negative MD values indicate less pain in the active treatment arm. Heterogeneity was summarised with Q, τ² and I²; small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Twelve records entered data extraction and 8 randomised comparisons (n = 655 participants) contributed poolable end-of-intervention pain data. The pooled effect, pooled natively as a **Mean Difference (MD)**, was **−1.277 (95% CI −7.073 to 4.518; p = 0.6658; k = 8)**. The point estimate **favours manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or combined manual therapy plus exercise**, but the confidence interval is wide and includes no difference, so no effect was demonstrated. Heterogeneity was extreme (Q = 27.187, df = 7, p = 0.0003; I² = 93.49%; τ² = 53.554). Study-level standardized effects ranged from Hedges' g = −0.792 (favouring active treatment) to g = +0.540 (favouring control), with only 2 of 8 individual estimates statistically significant in favour of active treatment and 1 of 8 significant in favour of control. Egger's test was not significant (intercept 0.4654, p = 0.1279); trim-and-fill imputed k₀ = 2 missing studies and shifted the adjusted estimate to −0.0484, i.e. essentially null. Overall small-study-effect risk was rated **moderate**.

**Conclusions.** At the end of a typical 6–8-week intervention period, pooled evidence does not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in pain intensity from manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or their combination relative to minimal or no intervention. The pooled point estimate nominally favours active treatment, but the estimate is imprecise, dominated by extreme and unexplained between-trial heterogeneity (τ² = 53.554 implies a between-trial SD of 7.3 points, which exceeds the width of the intended 0–10 analysis scale and signals residual unit inconsistency across contributing rows), and is attenuated to near zero by trim-and-fill adjustment. The result should be read as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no effect.

## 1. Introduction

Lateral elbow pain — variously termed lateral epicondylitis, lateral epicondylalgia, common extensor tendinopathy or "tennis elbow" — affects an estimated 1–3% of adults annually, with a peak incidence between 40 and 60 years of age. It is characterised by pain over the lateral humeral epicondyle provoked by gripping and resisted wrist extension, and by impairment of grip strength and upper-limb function. Although the natural history is frequently favourable, with a substantial proportion of untreated patients improving within 12 months, the condition is associated with prolonged symptom duration, work absence, and high rates of recurrence in a minority of patients.

Physical therapies are recommended as first-line management in most clinical guidelines. Two families of intervention dominate practice: (i) *manual therapy*, encompassing spinal and elbow joint manipulation, mobilisation with movement (e.g. Mulligan technique), and soft-tissue approaches such as deep transverse friction or myofascial release; and (ii) *prescribed exercise*, principally progressive eccentric or heavy-slow resistance loading of the wrist extensors, often with stretching. Combined manual therapy plus exercise is also common. The mechanistic rationale differs — manual therapy is hypothesised to act through immediate hypoalgesia and restoration of joint and soft-tissue mechanics, exercise through tendon remodelling and progressive load tolerance — but both are ultimately judged on whether they reduce pain faster than the condition's own trajectory.

That comparison is the crux. Because lateral elbow pain often resolves spontaneously, the clinically decisive question is not whether patients improve during treatment, but whether they improve *more* than patients given minimal or no intervention: wait-and-see, advice or stretching alone, placebo/sham treatment, or usual general-practitioner care. Earlier syntheses have reached divergent conclusions, partly because they pooled heterogeneous comparators (including corticosteroid injection and other active treatments), pooled multiple follow-up horizons together, or mixed pain, disability and grip-strength outcomes into a single standardized estimate.

This review therefore addresses a deliberately narrow question: **in adults with lateral elbow pain, what is the effect of manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or combined manual therapy plus exercise, compared with minimal or no intervention, on mean pain intensity at the first assessment at the end of the intervention period?** Restricting the outcome to end-of-intervention pain (typically 6–8 weeks) isolates the period during which the treatment is actually being delivered and avoids the dilution that occurs when short- and long-term time points are averaged.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults (≥18 years) with a clinical diagnosis of lateral elbow pain / lateral epicondylalgia / tennis elbow, of any symptom duration, recruited from primary care, secondary care, sports medicine or community settings.

**Interventions.** Any of: (a) manual therapy — joint manipulation, mobilisation (including mobilisation with movement), or soft-tissue techniques; (b) prescribed exercise — supervised or home-based strengthening, eccentric loading, or stretching programmes prescribed as the index treatment; (c) combined manual therapy plus exercise.

**Comparators.** Minimal or no intervention, operationalised as wait-and-see, advice or stretching only, placebo/sham therapy, or usual GP care.

**Outcome.** Mean pain intensity at the *first* assessment at the end of the intervention period, on a 0–10 scale. VAS 0–100 mm data were converted by dividing both means and SDs by 10; NRS 0–10 data were used directly. Where a trial reported several pain constructs, its own primary pain measure was preferred (pain at rest, pain on activity, or pain on maximum grip, in that order of availability).

**Study design.** Randomised or quasi-randomised controlled trials reporting extractable pain data for at least one eligible active arm and one eligible control arm.

### 2.2 Information sources and screening

Records were identified through a structured search of the bibliographic literature for randomised trials of manual therapy and exercise in lateral elbow pain, supplemented by reference-list screening of prior systematic reviews. Titles and abstracts were screened against the criteria above, and potentially eligible full texts were assessed. Review articles, duplicate reports of the same trial, and reports whose comparator was an active alternative treatment (e.g. corticosteroid injection alone, ultrasound, orthoses) rather than minimal/no intervention were not carried forward to extraction.

### 2.3 Flow of records

**Twelve records** reached the data-extraction stage of the analytic pipeline. Of these, **8 randomised comparisons provided poolable end-of-intervention pain data** and contributed effect sizes to the quantitative synthesis; **4 records did not yield a poolable pain effect size** and were therefore excluded from pooling while remaining part of the narrative review.

Per the extraction protocol, the admissible reasons for non-contribution were: (i) pain data reported only in figures, from which means and SDs could not be recovered; (ii) no pain assessment at the end of the intervention period (only later follow-up); (iii) absence of any usable dispersion statistic (no SD, SE/SEM, or confidence interval) from which a standard error could be reconstructed; and (iv) reporting of a pain outcome that could not be mapped to the 0–10 analysis scale. The extraction dataset available for this report records the aggregate counts (12 extracted, 8 pooled) but does not preserve a per-record attribution of which specific reason applied to each of the 4 non-contributing records; this is stated explicitly in §9 (Data Quality & Validation Notes) rather than being reconstructed post hoc.

**Yield for synthesis: k = 8 comparisons, N = 655 randomised participants.**

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure

The pre-specified and applied effect measure for the primary synthesis is the **Mean Difference (MD)**, pooled in the trials' raw pain units, with the effect-measure selection recorded as an *explicit user request* and **native pooling confirmed (`native_pooling_used = true`)**. The pooled statistic reported in §5 is therefore a genuine native-scale Mean Difference, **not** a standardized fallback.

Direction convention: on the raw pain scale, **MD < 0 indicates less pain in the active treatment arm**, i.e. a treatment benefit.

Alongside the native MD synthesis, standardized study-level effects (Hedges' g) were computed for each comparison to permit visual comparison across trials using differently scaled instruments. These standardized values are presented in §4.2 for descriptive purposes; they are *not* the metric of the primary pooled result, and nothing in this report should be read as reporting a pooled Hedges' g.

A single meta-analytic group was defined (one outcome construct — pain intensity — at one time point — end of intervention). No additional `meta_analysis_groups` entries were present, so there is one pooled estimate to report and no multi-group decomposition.

**Interpretation.** The pooled mean difference of −1.277 lies on the side of the scale that indicates *less pain* in the active treatment arm, and the `favors` field accordingly identifies the result as favouring **manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or combined manual therapy plus exercise**. However, the 95% confidence interval (−7.073 to 4.518) spans nearly 11.6 pain points and comfortably includes zero, and the associated p-value of 0.6658 provides no evidence against the null hypothesis of no difference. In plain terms: **the direction of the point estimate favours active treatment, but the analysis does not demonstrate a benefit.** The confidence interval is compatible with a large benefit, with no effect at all, and with a substantial harm (more pain under active treatment).

The magnitude of the interval also warrants comment on its own terms. On the intended 0–10 analysis scale, a confidence interval running from −7.07 to +4.52 is wider than the scale's own plausible range of between-group differences. This is a direct arithmetic consequence of the between-study variance estimate (τ² = 53.554, i.e. a between-study SD of 7.32 pain points) and is addressed in §6 and §9: it indicates that the pooled MD is being driven by dispersion that cannot be reconciled with a fully harmonised 0–10 metric across all eight contributing rows.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity in this synthesis is extreme and is the single most consequential feature of the result.

- **Cochran's Q = 27.187 on 7 degrees of freedom, p = 0.0003.** The null hypothesis of homogeneity is decisively rejected; observed dispersion among the eight estimates substantially exceeds what sampling error alone would produce.
- **I² = 93.49%**, in the "considerable heterogeneity" band of the Cochrane classification (75–100%). Taken at face value, approximately 93% of the observed variability in effect estimates reflects real between-study differences rather than chance.
- **τ² = 53.554**, corresponding to a between-study standard deviation of τ = 7.32 in the pooling units.

An approximate 95% prediction interval constructed from these quantities (point estimate ±  t₆ × √(τ² + SE²), with SE ≈ 2.96 derived from the pooled CI) spans roughly **−20.6 to +18.1**. An interval of that width cannot describe a genuine 0–10 pain scale and should not be reported to clinicians as a plausible range for the effect in a future trial. Its proper reading is diagnostic: it tells us that the between-trial dispersion captured by τ² is incompatible with the assumption that all eight rows were pooled on a single harmonised metric, and that the pooled MD is consequently not a stable or interpretable summary of a common underlying effect.

Three substantive sources are likely to be contributing jointly:

1. **Residual scale/unit inconsistency.** The effect-measure audit flagged that an explicit instrument range was stated for only **7 of 8 rows**, and that **no 0–100 rescaling was applied** — the MD was pooled in the papers' raw units. If even one comparison entered the pool on a 0–100 mm VAS rather than a divided-by-10 0–10 scale, it would inflate both the pooled dispersion and τ² by roughly two orders of magnitude in variance terms, which is entirely consistent with τ² = 53.554 and with the implausibly wide pooled CI. This is the most parsimonious explanation for the numerical pattern observed and is recorded as a formal data-quality concern in §9.
2. **Genuine clinical diversity.** The eligible interventions span three distinct treatment families (manipulation/mobilisation, soft-tissue techniques, progressive exercise, and combinations) and the eligible comparators span four distinct conditions (wait-and-see, advice/stretching, sham, usual GP care). A sham comparator and a wait-and-see comparator do not define the same contrast: sham controls for attention and expectancy, wait-and-see does not. Pooling across them necessarily introduces heterogeneity in the estimand itself.
3. **Outcome-construct diversity.** Pain at rest, pain on activity and pain on maximum grip were all admissible as a trial's primary pain measure. These constructs respond differently to loading-based treatments, and in particular an exercise programme may increase provoked pain during the loading phase while reducing resting pain.

A graphical summary of the heterogeneity statistics is at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

One internal inconsistency should be stated rather than glossed. An I² computed directly from the reported Q and df (I² = 1 − df/Q = 1 − 7/27.187) would be ≈74.3%, whereas the reported model-based I² is 93.49%. The two quantities are estimated differently (the latter from the REML τ² relative to within-study variances, the former from the Q-statistic), and the divergence is itself a marker of an unstable variance structure across the pooled rows. Both values nonetheless fall in the substantial-to-considerable range, so the qualitative conclusion — that heterogeneity is severe and unexplained — does not depend on which is preferred.

## 7. Moderators

**No formal moderator analysis (subgroup analysis or meta-regression) was conducted.** Two reasons preclude it.

First, with **k = 8** comparisons, a meta-regression has approximately one degree of freedom per candidate covariate before the model becomes unidentifiable; conventional guidance (≥10 studies per covariate) is not met for even a single moderator. Any coefficient estimated from these data would be dominated by the extreme heterogeneity documented in §6 and would carry a very high false-positive risk.

Second, the analytic dataset supplied for this synthesis contains study identifiers, sample sizes and effect estimates but **no trial-level covariate matrix** — no coded intervention subtype (manual therapy vs exercise vs combined), comparator subtype (wait-and-see vs sham vs usual care), symptom duration, pain construct, or risk-of-bias domain scores. The moderators that would be most informative for explaining the observed sign discordance are therefore simply unavailable.

What *can* be stated is a descriptive, hypothesis-generating observation, and it should be read as such rather than as a tested moderation effect:

- **Sample size tracks effect direction.** The three comparisons with the most favourable effects for active treatment are the three smallest (n = 19, 27, 40; g = −0.624, −0.325, −0.792), while three of the four largest comparisons (n = 118–124) estimate effects at or on the control side of null (g = +0.041, +0.259, +0.540). The single large comparison favouring treatment is study_2_R2 (n = 123, g = −0.624). The relationship between precision and effect magnitude is displayed in the bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`.

Two non-exclusive explanations fit this pattern: small-study effects/publication bias (§8), or genuine effect modification whereby smaller, more tightly controlled, often single-technique trials with highly supervised delivery achieve benefits that larger pragmatic trials embedded in usual care do not reproduce. With the available data these cannot be distinguished.

- **A sign-discordance subgroup split was considered and rejected.** Splitting the eight comparisons by observed direction of effect would be a post hoc, outcome-dependent subgrouping and would guarantee two spuriously homogeneous subgroups. It is not reported.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

| Diagnostic | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.4654 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.1279 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 2 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate | −0.0484 |
| Overall small-study-effect risk rating | **Moderate** |

Egger's regression test did not detect statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept = 0.4654, p = 0.1279), so there is no strong *formal* evidence of small-study effects. That null result, however, carries limited weight here: Egger's test is notoriously underpowered when fewer than approximately ten studies are pooled, and with k = 8 and I² = 93.49% the test's assumptions are strained in both respects. A non-significant Egger result in this setting should not be read as evidence of the *absence* of bias.

The trim-and-fill procedure nonetheless imputed **k₀ = 2** hypothetically missing comparisons, indicating mild funnel asymmetry that falls short of statistical detection. Critically, the adjusted pooled estimate after imputation is **−0.0484** — a shift from the unadjusted point estimate of −1.277 to a value that is, for practical purposes, exactly zero. The *direction* of the primary finding is preserved (the adjusted estimate still sits marginally on the treatment-favouring side of null), but its *magnitude* is almost entirely eliminated, and the finding remains non-significant both before and after adjustment. The descriptive size–direction association documented in §7 points in the same direction.

Taken together, these converging but non-definitive signals justify the **moderate** risk rating. The pooled estimate is unlikely to be grossly distorted in the sense of reversing a robust finding — there is no robust finding to reverse — but readers should note that the true effect may be smaller than the unadjusted point estimate suggests, and that trim-and-fill adjustment moves the best estimate of the end-of-intervention pain benefit to essentially nil.

## 9. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following issues were identified during data handling and effect-measure auditing and are reported plainly because they bear directly on how much weight the pooled estimate can carry.

1. **Effect-measure conflict: incomplete instrument-range documentation.** The effect measure was set to Mean Difference at explicit user request and native pooling was confirmed. However, the audit recorded that **an instrument range was stated for only 7 of the 8 contributing rows**, and that **no 0–100 rescaling was applied — the MD was pooled in the papers' raw units**. This means the synthesis cannot be certified as having pooled all eight comparisons on a single, fully harmonised 0–10 pain scale. Given that τ² = 53.554 (τ = 7.32 points) exceeds what is arithmetically possible for genuine between-trial variation in a 0–10 mean difference, residual unit inconsistency is the most likely explanation for a material part of the observed heterogeneity and for the implausibly wide pooled confidence interval. The pooled MD of −1.277 should therefore **not** be quoted to clinicians as "1.3 points of pain relief on a 0–10 scale"; its units are not verifiably uniform.

2. **Four of twelve extracted records contributed no effect size.** Twelve records entered extraction; 8 were pooled. The 4 non-contributing records were excluded from pooling under the protocol's stated grounds (pain reported only in figures; no end-of-intervention pain assessment; no usable dispersion statistic; or a pain outcome not mappable to the analysis scale). The available extraction dataset does not record which specific ground applied to each record, so a per-study exclusion table cannot be provided. Because figure-only reporting was an explicit exclusion rule, at least some poolable-in-principle pain data are known to be missing from the synthesis, and such data are not missing at random with respect to effect size.

3. **Divergent I² estimates.** The reported model-based I² (93.49%) and the Q-derived I² (≈74.3%, from 1 − 7/27.187) do not coincide. Both indicate substantial-to-considerable heterogeneity, but the divergence signals an unstable variance structure across pooled rows and is consistent with issue 1 above.

4. **No per-study weights, risk-of-bias assessments, or trial-level covariates were available** in the analytic dataset supplied for this report. Consequently: (a) the relative contribution of each comparison to the pooled estimate is not reported; (b) no GRADE-style certainty rating or risk-of-bias-stratified sensitivity analysis could be performed; and (c) no planned subgroup or meta-regression analysis could be executed (§7).

5. **Single outcome group.** Only one meta-analytic group was defined (end-of-intervention pain intensity). No group was marked `insufficient_data`, and no secondary outcomes (disability, grip strength, quality of life, treatment success, withdrawals, adverse events) were pooled, consistent with the protocol.

## 10. Discussion

This synthesis of 8 randomised comparisons in 655 adults with lateral elbow pain found that, at the end of a typical 6–8-week intervention period, **manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or their combination did not produce a statistically significant reduction in pain intensity compared with minimal or no intervention** (pooled Mean Difference −1.277, 95% CI −7.073 to 4.518, p = 0.6658). The point estimate's direction favours active treatment, and this is the direction recorded by the analysis, but the confidence interval includes no difference and extends well into the range that would indicate harm.

Three considerations shape how this null result should be read.

**The result is inconclusive, not reassuringly negative.** Extreme heterogeneity (I² = 93.49%, τ² = 53.554, Q p = 0.0003) means the eight comparisons are not estimating a common effect, and the resulting random-effects confidence interval is so wide that it excludes almost nothing. A wide, null-spanning interval of this kind is an admission of ignorance rather than a demonstration of equivalence. Clinicians should not conclude from this analysis that manual therapy or exercise is ineffective for tennis elbow pain; they should conclude that the pooled randomised evidence on end-of-intervention pain, as assembled here, cannot settle the question.

**The disagreement among trials is directional, not merely quantitative.** Five comparisons favour active treatment and three favour the control arm, with two significant benefits and one significant effect in the opposite direction. This pattern is hard to attribute to chance alone and is better explained by the composite nature of both arms. The "treatment" arm pools manipulation, mobilisation, soft-tissue work and progressive loading — interventions with different mechanisms, different dose–response profiles and plausibly different short-term pain trajectories (loading programmes in particular may transiently increase provoked pain while tendon adaptation occurs). The "control" arm pools sham therapy, wait-and-see, advice-only and usual GP care — conditions that differ markedly in the amount of attention, expectancy and co-intervention they deliver. A trial comparing mobilisation with movement against sham and a trial comparing a home exercise sheet against usual GP care are answering different questions; averaging them produces a number that answers neither.

**The size–direction association and trim-and-fill result point the same way.** The largest apparent benefits came from the smallest trials, while three of the four largest comparisons found no benefit or a small advantage to control. Egger's test was formally negative (p = 0.1279) but is underpowered at k = 8; trim-and-fill imputed two missing comparisons and reduced the pooled estimate to −0.0484, essentially nil. Consistent with the known literature on physical-therapy trials, the plausible reading is that small, enthusiastically delivered, sham-controlled trials generate effect estimates that larger pragmatic trials do not reproduce — whether through selective publication, inadequate blinding of outcome assessment in small trials, or genuine differences in delivery fidelity.

**Relation to prior work.** These findings are broadly consistent with the pattern reported in earlier reviews of tennis elbow management: short-term symptomatic gains from physical therapy are inconsistently demonstrated against minimal-intervention comparators, and differences that appear at the end of treatment tend to attenuate relative to the condition's favourable natural history. They also echo a recurrent methodological complaint about this literature — that trials are small, outcome measures are not standardised across the pain constructs of interest, and reporting is frequently insufficient (figure-only pain data, missing SDs, unreported analysed n) to support precise pooling.

**Clinical implications.** Given an imprecise, heterogeneous and bias-susceptible pooled estimate, treatment decisions in lateral elbow pain should not rest on an assumed short-term pain advantage of manual therapy or exercise over watchful waiting. Both remain defensible as low-risk options, particularly where patients prioritise active self-management, function, or confidence in loading, and the present analysis — restricted to end-of-intervention pain intensity and silent on disability, grip strength, function, recurrence and long-term outcomes by design — should not be used to argue against their use. It does, however, argue against promising patients a reliable pain benefit over the first 6–8 weeks relative to doing little.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Effect-measure/unit integrity.** The most serious limitation is the one documented in §9: instrument range was confirmed for only 7 of 8 rows and no rescaling to a common 0–100 (or 0–10) metric was applied, so the pooled MD may not be in uniform units. The implausible magnitudes of τ² (53.554) and the pooled CI width are consistent with this. The pooled MD should be treated as a provisional summary pending re-extraction with verified instrument ranges for every row.

2. **Extreme unexplained heterogeneity.** With I² ≈ 93% and a prediction interval spanning values outside the plausible scale range, no single pooled number adequately represents these trials.

3. **Small number of comparisons.** k = 8 limits every secondary analysis: Egger's test is underpowered, trim-and-fill is unstable, REML τ² is estimated imprecisely, and no meta-regression was feasible.

4. **Composite treatment and comparator definitions.** Lumping three intervention families against four comparator types maximises inclusiveness at the direct cost of a well-defined estimand.

5. **Selective availability of pain data.** Trials reporting pain only in figures were excluded by protocol, and 4 of 12 extracted records contributed no effect size. This is an identifiable, non-random source of missing evidence.

6. **No risk-of-bias assessment or certainty rating.** Domain-level bias assessments were not available in the analytic dataset, so bias-stratified sensitivity analyses and a GRADE certainty rating could not be produced. Blinding of participants to manual therapy and exercise is generally infeasible, so performance and detection bias for a subjective pain outcome should be presumed material across this literature.

7. **Single time point, single outcome.** By design, only the first end-of-intervention pain assessment was analysed. Disability, grip strength, quality of life, treatment success, withdrawals and adverse events were not pooled, and later follow-up was not examined; the review therefore says nothing about durability of effect or about non-pain benefits and harms.

8. **Mixed data types within the pool.** The protocol permitted end-of-treatment values, change-from-baseline scores (where these were the only data reported), and adjusted between-group differences reconstructed from confidence intervals. Although not mixed within any single trial, mixing these data types across trials introduces an additional, unquantified source of between-study variance.

---

## 12. Conclusion

In adults with lateral elbow pain, pooled randomised evidence from 8 comparisons (N = 655) does **not** demonstrate that manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or combined manual therapy plus exercise reduces pain intensity more than minimal or no intervention at the end of a typical 6–8-week intervention period. The pooled **Mean Difference was −1.277 (95% CI −7.073 to 4.518; p = 0.6658)**; the direction of this estimate **favours manual therapy, prescribed exercise, or combined manual therapy plus exercise**, but the interval includes no difference and the result is not statistically significant. Between-trial heterogeneity was extreme and unexplained (I² = 93.49%, τ² = 53.554, Q = 27.187, df = 7, p = 0.0003), individual trials disagreed about the direction of effect, and trim-and-fill adjustment for mild funnel asymmetry (k₀ = 2) reduced the estimate to essentially zero (−0.0484) despite a non-significant Egger's test (p = 0.1279; small-study-effect risk rated moderate). Documentation of instrument ranges was incomplete for one of eight rows and no common-scale rescaling was applied, so the pooled MD's units are not verifiably uniform.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore one of **uncertainty rather than of no effect**. Resolving it requires adequately powered pragmatic trials with clearly specified single intervention families, a single clearly specified minimal-intervention comparator, pre-registered primary pain constructs, and complete numerical reporting of means, standard deviations, instrument ranges and analysed sample sizes at the end of the intervention period.
