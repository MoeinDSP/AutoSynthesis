# Group Educational Interventions and Dermatology-Specific Quality of Life: A PRISMA-Compliant Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of DLQI, CDLQI, IDQOL and Family-Variant Outcomes

## Abstract

**Background.** Group-delivered educational programmes (structured "eczema schools", patient workshops, nurse-led teaching groups and comparable formats) are widely recommended as adjuncts to routine dermatological care, but their effect on dermatology-specific quality of life has not been quantified in a focused synthesis that pools the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) together with its age- and respondent-specific variants.

**Objective.** To estimate the effect of group educational interventions delivered in addition to standard care, compared with standard care only, on dermatology-specific quality of life measured with the DLQI or its variants (CDLQI for children, IDQOL for infants, DFI/FDLQI for family members) in people with a dermatological condition.

**Methods.** Records identified by the systematic search were screened against a single pre-specified outcome definition: dermatology-specific quality of life measured on the DLQI or one of its age/respondent variants, pooled across all such scales. Other quality-of-life instruments, DLQI subscale scores and alternative respondents reported in the source review were excluded by protocol. Where a trial reported results separately for two age-stratified sub-populations (children and infants, as in Liang 2017), both strata were extracted as separate comparison rows within this single outcome. Effects were pooled on the native Mean Difference (MD) scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** Four studies contributing five comparisons (N = 757 participants) were included. The pooled effect was a **Mean Difference (MD) of −1.685 points** (95% CI −3.396 to 0.026; p = 0.054; k = 5). On DLQI-family scales a lower score denotes better quality of life, and the pooled result **favours the group educational intervention (plus standard care)**; however, the 95% confidence interval crosses the line of no effect and the result does not reach conventional statistical significance. The effect measure was explicitly specified as a Mean Difference and native-scale pooling was used (native_pooling_used = true), so the pooled value is a confirmed native-scale result rather than a standardized fallback. Heterogeneity was substantial: Q = 15.339 (df = 4, p = 0.004), I² = 83.32%, τ² = 2.998. Individual standardized comparison-level estimates ranged from g = +0.061 (n = 98) to g = −1.525 (n = 23). Egger's test indicated statistically significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = 0.2645, p = 0.040; overall bias risk graded **high**), whereas trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0, adjusted estimate −0.3068).

**Conclusions.** Group educational interventions added to standard care produce a small-to-moderate average improvement in dermatology-specific quality of life that favours the intervention but is not statistically significant at the 5% level, is smaller than the commonly cited 4-point DLQI minimal clinically important difference, and is accompanied by substantial unexplained heterogeneity (I² = 83.32%) and significant small-study asymmetry. The evidence base (k = 5 comparisons from 4 studies, two of which are age strata of a single trial) is too sparse to support firm practice recommendations or formal moderator analysis, and the pooled estimate should be regarded as potentially optimistic.


## 1. Introduction

Chronic dermatological conditions — atopic dermatitis, psoriasis, chronic urticaria, acne and others — impose a burden that extends well beyond visible signs. Itch, sleep disruption, embarrassment, treatment burden and social withdrawal mean that the patient-perceived impact of skin disease is frequently discordant with clinician-rated severity. The Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) and its family of derivative instruments — the Children's Dermatology Life Quality Index (CDLQI), the Infants' Dermatitis Quality of Life Index (IDQOL), the Dermatitis Family Impact questionnaire (DFI) and the Family Dermatology Life Quality Index (FDLQI) — were developed precisely to capture this burden in a dermatology-specific, respondent-appropriate way. All are scored such that **higher values indicate greater impairment**; an improvement in quality of life therefore appears as a *reduction* in score.

Education is a cornerstone of chronic disease management in dermatology. Group-delivered educational interventions — structured multidisciplinary "eczema schools", nurse-led teaching sessions, parent workshops and peer-group programmes — are attractive because they are resource-efficient, allow peer modelling and social support, and can address the practical skills (emollient technique, topical corticosteroid phobia, trigger avoidance, scratch–itch cycle management) that individual consultations often lack time for. National and international guidance in atopic dermatitis frequently recommends such programmes. Yet the quantitative evidence that group education improves *patient-reported* dermatology-specific quality of life, over and above standard care, has not been synthesised in a single focused analysis spanning the whole DLQI instrument family.

This review addresses one precisely bounded question: **what is the effect of group educational interventions delivered in addition to standard care, compared with standard care only, on dermatology-specific quality of life measured with the DLQI or its infant, child and family variants, pooled across all such scales?** Other quality-of-life definitions, DLQI subscale scores and alternative respondents reported in the source review were deliberately excluded so that the synthesis addresses a single, homogeneous construct.

## 2. Methods: Search and Screening

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

This report follows the PRISMA 2020 reporting guidance for systematic reviews incorporating meta-analysis. The review question, intervention and comparator definitions, outcome definition and effect-direction convention were fixed before synthesis.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

| Element | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **Population** | People with a diagnosed dermatological condition (any age), including, where the instrument requires a proxy respondent, their parents/family members |
| **Intervention** | Group educational intervention delivered **in addition to** standard care |
| **Comparator** | Standard care only (no group educational component) |
| **Outcome** | Dermatology-specific quality of life measured with the DLQI or an age/respondent variant (CDLQI, IDQOL, DFI, FDLQI), pooled across all such scales |
| **Design** | Controlled trials providing between-arm comparative data on the outcome above |
| **Excluded outcomes** | Generic or non-dermatology-specific quality-of-life instruments; DLQI *subscale* scores; alternative respondents to the pre-specified one — all of which appear in the source review but are outside this outcome definition |

### 2.3 Screening and selection

Records retrieved by the search were screened at title/abstract level against the population, intervention and comparator criteria, and full texts were then assessed against the outcome definition. The decisive screening step was the outcome filter: many otherwise-eligible reports contributed only generic quality-of-life measures, subscale breakdowns, or a respondent other than the pre-specified one, and were therefore not carried into the quantitative synthesis for this outcome.

### 2.4 Data extraction and unit-of-analysis handling

For each eligible comparison we extracted the intervention and control arm means, dispersion statistics, and analysed sample sizes on the DLQI-family scale. One pre-specified unit-of-analysis rule applied: **where a trial reported DLQI-family results separately for two age-stratified sub-populations (children and infants — as in Liang 2017), each stratum was extracted as its own comparison row** within this single outcome. This yields more comparison rows (k = 5) than independent studies (4) and is recorded explicitly below because it has consequences for the independence assumptions of the random-effects model and of the publication-bias tests.

### 2.5 Effect measure, direction convention and synthesis

The effect measure was **explicitly specified by the review protocol as the Mean Difference (MD)** (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`), and **native-scale pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`). The pooled statistic reported below is therefore a confirmed native-scale Mean Difference in DLQI-family points, not a standardized fallback, and must be named as such. For descriptive purposes, individual comparison rows are additionally displayed as standardized effect estimates (Hedges' g) with 95% confidence intervals, as generated by the extraction pipeline.

**Direction convention.** Raw effect estimates retain the treatment-minus-control sign convention. Because lower DLQI-family scores indicate *better* quality of life, a **negative** raw value corresponds to benefit from the intervention. The direction reported throughout is taken from the analysis `favors` field rather than inferred from the sign: the pooled result **favours the group educational intervention (plus standard care)**.

Pooling used a random-effects model with REML estimation of τ². Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was examined with Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill method. All five comparisons addressed the same outcome construct and timepoint definition, so a single meta-analytic group was specified; no separate outcome/timepoint subgroups were required.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was specified (all five comparisons address the same DLQI-family quality-of-life outcome), so one pooled estimate is reported.

**Table 2. Random-effects pooled result (REML)**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Mean Difference (MD)** (`measure_label`) |
| Native-scale pooling | Yes (`native_pooling_used = true`) — confirmed native-scale result |
| Comparisons (k) | 5 (from 4 studies) |
| Total participants | 757 |
| **Pooled Mean Difference** | **−1.685 DLQI-family points** |
| 95% CI | −3.396 to 0.026 |
| p-value | 0.054 |
| Favours (`favors` field) | **Group educational intervention (plus standard care)** |
| τ² | 2.998 |
| I² | 83.32% |
| Q (df = 4) | 15.339, p = 0.004 |
| Estimator | REML random-effects |

**Interpretation.** The pooled **Mean Difference (MD) is −1.685 points** on the DLQI-family scale. Because lower scores denote better dermatology-specific quality of life, and consistent with the analysis `favors` field, this result **favours the group educational intervention delivered alongside standard care**. However:

- The **95% confidence interval (−3.396 to 0.026) includes zero**, and p = 0.054 does not cross the conventional 5% threshold. The pooled estimate is therefore best described as *suggestive of benefit but not statistically significant*. It sits at the boundary where a single additional trial — or a different heterogeneity estimator — could move the inference either way, and should not be reported as a demonstrated effect.
- The magnitude requires calibration against clinical anchors. The widely cited minimal clinically important difference for the adult DLQI is approximately 4 points (commonly quoted as 3–4). A pooled improvement of 1.685 points is therefore **below the conventional threshold for a clinically meaningful change at the group-average level**, even taking the point estimate at face value. The upper plausible bound of the interval (−3.396) approaches but does not clearly reach that threshold.
- The confidence interval describes uncertainty about the *average* effect only. Given τ² = 2.998 (τ ≈ 1.73), the approximate 95% prediction interval for the effect in a new, exchangeable setting spans from roughly −7.1 to +3.7 points — that is, the available data are compatible with a substantial benefit in some settings and a modest *worsening* in others. This derived interval should be read as an approximation, as prediction intervals are notoriously unstable when k = 5.

The cumulative analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows how the pooled estimate evolves as comparisons accumulate and makes the influence of the small, large-effect comparison on the running estimate visible; the estimate has not stabilised across the accumulation sequence.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity is substantial and statistically significant:

- **Cochran's Q = 15.339 on 4 degrees of freedom, p = 0.004** — the null hypothesis of a common underlying effect is rejected. This is notable because Q is typically underpowered with few studies; its significance here indicates genuinely discrepant results rather than a borderline signal.
- **I² = 83.32%** — approximately five-sixths of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to real between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. By the conventional Cochrane bands (25% / 50% / 75% for low / moderate / considerable), this falls firmly in the **considerable** range.
- **τ² = 2.998** (τ ≈ 1.73 points on the pooled MD scale) — the standard deviation of true effects across settings is of the same order as, indeed larger than, the pooled effect itself.

The heterogeneity summary figure (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`) presents these statistics graphically.

**Plausible sources.** With k = 5 none of the following can be tested, but each is a credible contributor:

1. **Instrument heterogeneity within the DLQI family.** By design the synthesis pools DLQI, CDLQI, IDQOL and family variants. These instruments share a scoring philosophy and a 0–30 range but differ in item content, respondent (patient vs proxy vs family member) and recall framing. Pooling them on a native MD scale assumes a point on one instrument is interchangeable with a point on another — an assumption that is convenient but not strictly true, and that directly inflates between-study variance.
2. **Population and age mix.** Infant, child, adolescent and adult populations are combined, as are proxy-reported and self-reported scores. Baseline impairment, and therefore room for improvement, differs systematically across these groups; floor effects in mildly affected cohorts attenuate observable change.
3. **Intervention dose and content.** "Group educational intervention" covers programmes ranging from a single session to multi-week multidisciplinary curricula, with differing facilitator training, group size and inclusion of practical skills training.
4. **Standard care contrast.** The comparator is defined only as standard care, which varies enormously across health systems. Where standard care already includes substantial individual education, the incremental contribution of a group programme is necessarily smaller.
5. **Small-study effects.** The inverse size–effect relationship documented in §4 and §8 is itself a source of the dispersion: removing the single smallest comparison would be expected to reduce both the pooled magnitude and I² considerably.

Given I² = 83.32%, the random-effects model is the appropriate specification, but the pooled estimate should be read as the *centre of a broad distribution of effects* rather than as an estimate of a single common effect.

---

## 7. Moderators

**No formal moderator analysis was conducted, and none is defensible with these data.** With k = 5 comparisons derived from 4 studies, any meta-regression would have at most two or three residual degrees of freedom, far below the commonly recommended minimum of approximately ten studies per covariate; subgroup analyses by instrument variant (DLQI vs CDLQI vs IDQOL vs family version), by age band, or by intervention dose would each reduce to strata of one or two comparisons. Reporting such analyses would risk presenting noise as explanation.

Two observations are offered descriptively only:

- **Comparison size** is the one covariate for which a visible gradient exists (bubble chart, `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`): the smallest comparison shows the largest benefit and the largest comparison one of the smallest. This is examined formally as small-study asymmetry in §8 rather than as a substantive moderator.
- **Within-trial age stratification.** The two Liang 2017 strata (children and infants) are, by construction, matched on trial design, setting and intervention protocol while differing in age and instrument. They constitute the only quasi-controlled contrast available, but a single within-trial comparison cannot support inference about age as a moderator.

Identifying which patients, which instruments and which programme formats drive benefit remains the principal open empirical question, and requires either considerably more trials or individual participant data.

## 8. Publication Bias

**Table 3. Small-study effects and bias assessment**

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.2645 |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.040** |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **0** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate | −0.3068 |
| Overall bias risk rating | **High** |

Egger's test indicates **statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.040)**, consistent with small-study effects and supporting an overall rating of **high risk of publication bias**. On this reading the pooled effect may be inflated by the preferential availability of small trials reporting large benefits.

Against this, **trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0)**, so its bias-adjusted estimate is identical to the observed one. This discordance between a significant Egger's test and a null trim-and-fill is common and interpretable: trim-and-fill has low power in small or heterogeneous meta-analyses and detects only certain patterns of asymmetry, so a null result from it is weak evidence of the absence of bias rather than evidence of absence. The reported trim-and-fill adjusted value of −0.3068 is on the standardized scale used by that routine and is not directly comparable with the native-scale pooled Mean Difference of −1.685 points; it should not be read as an alternative native-scale estimate.

Three further caveats temper any strong conclusion about selective publication:

1. **With k = 5, funnel-based diagnostics are severely underpowered.** Egger's test is generally considered uninformative below roughly ten studies, and a single influential point (here, the n = 23 comparison) can drive the regression slope. The significant p-value should be read as a flag warranting caution, not as an established finding of reporting bias.
2. **Asymmetry and heterogeneity are confounded.** With I² = 83.32%, genuine between-study differences — particularly if smaller trials used more intensive interventions, more impaired populations, or less blinded outcome assessment — can generate funnel asymmetry in the complete absence of selective publication.
3. **Non-independence.** Two of the five rows come from a single trial, which violates the independence assumption underlying both Egger's test and trim-and-fill and makes their nominal p-values approximate.

**Bottom line:** the asymmetry may reflect genuine selective reporting, but it could equally stem from between-study heterogeneity or methodological differences among the smaller trials. Either way, the pooled estimate should be treated as **potentially optimistic** pending sensitivity analyses and additional trials. This reinforces the already-cautious reading of a non-significant pooled result whose magnitude lies below the DLQI minimal clinically important difference.

## 9. Discussion

Across four studies and five comparisons (N = 757), group educational interventions added to standard care were associated with a pooled **Mean Difference of −1.685 DLQI-family points (95% CI −3.396 to 0.026; p = 0.054)**, a result that **favours the group educational intervention** but does not achieve statistical significance and falls short of the ~4-point minimal clinically important difference conventionally applied to the DLQI.

**How should this be read clinically?** Three readings are defensible and should be held simultaneously. First, the *direction* of evidence is reasonably consistent — four of five comparisons favour group education — which is what one would expect of an intervention with a genuine but modest effect. Second, the *magnitude* is below the threshold at which an average patient would be expected to notice a difference, implying that group education alone is unlikely to transform dermatology-specific quality of life even if the point estimate is accurate. Third, the *dispersion* (τ ≈ 1.73; approximate prediction interval −7.1 to +3.7) leaves open that some well-designed programmes in well-selected populations deliver clinically meaningful benefit while others deliver none. The practical implication is not that group education should be abandoned — it is inexpensive, generally acceptable, and may deliver benefits (knowledge, adherence, treatment confidence, carer burden) that the DLQI family was never designed to capture — but that it should not be promoted as a reliable means of improving dermatology-specific quality of life on the strength of this evidence.

**Consistency with the wider literature.** The pattern here — a modest, directionally favourable, statistically fragile effect with high heterogeneity — mirrors what is typically found for educational and self-management interventions in other chronic conditions. Two structural features of this literature plausibly attenuate measured effects: group education is added to a comparator (standard care) that itself usually includes some education, and the DLQI family measures disease *impact*, which is dominated by symptom severity and may respond only indirectly to improved knowledge and self-management behaviour.

**Methodological reading.** The combination of a borderline pooled estimate (p = 0.054), considerable heterogeneity (I² = 83.32%) and significant funnel asymmetry (Egger's p = 0.040) means that each of the three main sources of uncertainty — sampling error, true between-setting variation, and possible selective availability of results — is non-trivial here. Such a configuration should not be resolved by emphasising the favourable point estimate; the honest summary is that the evidence is *suggestive but inconclusive*.

## 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were returned by the synthesis pipeline for this outcome. For transparency, the following structural features of the dataset are nonetheless recorded, as each bears on interpretation:

- **Unit of analysis / non-independence.** Five comparison rows derive from four studies. The additional row arises from the pre-specified rule that an age-stratified trial (Liang 2017) contributing separate children and infant DLQI-family results has both strata extracted as separate comparisons within this single outcome. These two rows share a trial, protocol and setting and are therefore not fully independent; the random-effects model, Egger's test and trim-and-fill all nominally assume independence, so their p-values are approximate.
- **Outcome-scope exclusions.** By protocol, other quality-of-life definitions, DLQI *subscale* scores and alternative respondents that appear in the source review were **not** included in this synthesis. The pooled estimate therefore speaks only to total-score DLQI-family quality of life as defined in §2.2, and is not interchangeable with the broader quality-of-life evidence in the source review.
- **Cross-instrument pooling.** DLQI, CDLQI, IDQOL and family variants are pooled on a common native scale as specified. This is intentional and pre-planned, but it assumes scale interchangeability and is a probable contributor to the observed heterogeneity.
- **Mixed reporting scales in the outputs.** Comparison-level rows are displayed as standardized estimates (Hedges' g) while the pooled synthesis is on the native Mean Difference scale as explicitly requested; the trim-and-fill adjusted value (−0.3068) is likewise on the standardized scale. Pooled and comparison-level numbers are therefore not on the same metric and should not be compared point-for-point.
- **Bias diagnostics underpowered.** With k = 5, Egger's test and trim-and-fill fall well below the sample sizes at which they behave reliably; their discordance here (significant asymmetry, zero imputed studies) is reported as-is rather than reconciled.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base.** Four studies and five comparisons provide limited power to detect a modest effect and leave the pooled estimate sensitive to any single comparison; the borderline p-value (0.054) is a direct expression of this fragility.
2. **Substantial unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 83.32% with τ² = 2.998 means the pooled value summarises a wide distribution of true effects; no subgroup or meta-regression analysis could be performed to explain it.
3. **Non-independent comparisons.** Two rows come from one trial, mildly overweighting that trial and violating independence assumptions in the model and the bias tests.
4. **Cross-instrument pooling.** Combining DLQI, CDLQI, IDQOL and family variants treats points on different instruments and from different respondents as equivalent.
5. **High risk of publication bias.** Significant Egger's asymmetry (p = 0.040) with an overall risk rating of *high* indicates the pooled effect may be inflated; trim-and-fill's null result (k₀ = 0) provides only weak reassurance given its low power here.
6. **Heterogeneous interventions and comparators.** Neither "group educational intervention" nor "standard care only" is standardised across studies, limiting the specificity of any practice recommendation.
7. **Risk of bias within studies not quantified here.** Blinding of participants is impossible in educational trials, and the outcome is self- or proxy-reported, creating scope for performance and response bias that is plausibly non-differential across studies but unmeasured in this synthesis.
8. **Timepoints and durability.** The synthesis pools post-intervention quality-of-life scores without modelling follow-up duration; nothing can be said about whether any benefit is sustained.
9. **No grey-literature or language-restriction analysis is reported**, so the asymmetry detected cannot be attributed to, or exonerated from, selective availability of unpublished results.

Overall certainty in the pooled estimate, judged informally against GRADE domains (imprecision, inconsistency, publication bias all serious), is **low to very low**.

---

## 12. Conclusion

Pooling four studies and five comparisons (N = 757), group educational interventions delivered alongside standard care yielded a **Mean Difference of −1.685 DLQI-family points (95% CI −3.396 to 0.026; p = 0.054; k = 5)** compared with standard care only. The result **favours the group educational intervention**, and because this was a pre-specified native-scale Mean Difference analysis (`native_pooling_used = true`) the value is a confirmed native-scale estimate rather than a standardized fallback. However, the confidence interval includes no effect, the magnitude is smaller than the conventional ~4-point DLQI minimal clinically important difference, heterogeneity is considerable (I² = 83.32%; τ² = 2.998; Q = 15.339, df = 4, p = 0.004), and Egger's test indicates significant small-study asymmetry with a high overall risk of publication bias (p = 0.040), albeit with no studies imputed by trim-and-fill (k₀ = 0).

The appropriate conclusion is therefore one of **promising but unproven benefit**. Group education remains a low-cost, acceptable adjunct that may well help some patients and carers, but current DLQI-family evidence does not establish a clinically meaningful average improvement in dermatology-specific quality of life. Priorities for future research are: adequately powered trials reporting total DLQI-family scores with defined follow-up; prospective registration and full reporting to address the asymmetry flagged here; consistent use of a single age-appropriate instrument per population with pre-specified MCID anchors; explicit description of what "standard care" includes in the comparator arm; and ideally individual participant data pooling, which is the only realistic route to identifying which patients and which programme formats actually benefit.
