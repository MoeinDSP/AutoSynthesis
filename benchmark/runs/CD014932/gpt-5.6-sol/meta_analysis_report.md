# Group Educational Interventions for Dermatology-Related Quality of Life: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of DLQI-Family Outcomes

## Abstract

## Abstract

**Background:** Educational interventions delivered in groups may improve condition management and dermatology-related quality of life, but their effects must be evaluated using outcome measures that match the prespecified construct.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of group educational interventions, compared with standard care only, on dermatology-related quality of life measured using the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) or eligible infant, child, and family variants encompassed by the review outcome.

**Methods:** The supplied review dataset contained four included studies. Three effect estimates, representing 555 participants, were quantitatively synthesized using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Because the native effect measure could not be confirmed, outcomes were pooled using Hedges' g as a standardized fallback rather than as a confirmed native-scale statistic. By the review definition, a positive effect indicates better quality of life with group education; however, treatment-favoring classification was taken directly from the supplied meta-analysis field rather than inferred from the raw statistical sign. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were assessed using Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** The three study-level standardized effects were 0.061445 (95% CI −0.361 to 0.484; n=98), −0.494198 (95% CI −0.846 to −0.142; n=128), and 0.246472 (95% CI 0.029 to 0.464; n=329). The REML pooled Hedges' g was −0.05099080323869676 (95% CI −0.497 to 0.395; p=0.8228595971367582; k=3; n=555), classified in the supplied results as favoring group educational interventions. The confidence interval included the null. Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=82.37%; τ²=0.126593), and Cochran's Q was statistically significant (Q=12.325; p=0.0021). Egger's intercept was 0.4763 (p=0.6234), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀=0), leaving an adjusted estimate of −0.051. Moderator analysis was not supportable with only three pooled effects and no supplied study-level covariates.

**Conclusions:** The pooled analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant effect of group education on eligible DLQI-family outcomes, and the estimate was highly heterogeneous and imprecise. Although the supplied classification labels the point estimate as favoring group education, the effect-measure provenance and direction require verification against the original reports. The very small evidence base, unresolved native metric, and incomplete study-level information substantially limit confidence in the result.

## Introduction

Dermatological conditions can affect symptoms, daily activities, relationships, work or school participation, and emotional well-being. Group educational interventions may improve patients’ or families’ knowledge, self-management skills, treatment adherence, and ability to cope with a skin disorder. Their impact on quality of life nevertheless depends on intervention content, participant population, disease severity, respondent, and the outcome instrument used.

This review addressed the following question: **Among people with a dermatological condition, what is the effect of group educational interventions, compared with standard care only, on dermatology-related quality of life measured specifically using the DLQI, its age-appropriate infant/child variants, or the specified family variant included in the outcome definition?**

The outcome was deliberately restricted to the Dermatology Life Quality Index (DLQI) and the eligible infant, child, and family variants covered by the prespecified “all scales, infants, children, and family” outcome. Other quality-of-life constructs, noneligible instruments, alternative DLQI subscales, and outcomes reported by respondents outside this definition were not intended for inclusion. Better dermatology-related quality of life corresponds clinically to lower DLQI-family scores and less impairment. The supplied review definition states that a positive effect size represents benefit from group education; nonetheless, pooled-arm favoring statements below follow the supplied `favors` classification, as required, rather than being inferred from the numerical sign.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model fitted by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** combined the three available effects. The **pooled Hedges’ g was −0.05099080323869676** (95% CI **−0.497 to 0.395**; p=**0.8228595971367582**; k=3; n=555). According to the supplied `favors` field, this result **favored group educational interventions**. This arm-level classification is reported directly and is not inferred from the raw negative sign.

The estimate was close to the null and its confidence interval included no effect, providing no statistically significant evidence that group education improved eligible DLQI-family outcomes compared with standard care only. The interval also permits effects in either numerical direction. Because the analysis used a standardized fallback rather than a confirmed native DLQI-family metric, the result should not be interpreted as a difference of −0.051 DLQI points.

A cumulative display is available at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/cumulative_plot.png`. Because study order and its rationale were not supplied, this plot should be treated as descriptive rather than as evidence of temporal stabilization.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- **Cochran’s Q=12.325**, p=**0.0021**;
- **I²=82.37%**; and
- **τ²=0.126593** on the standardized-effect scale.

The statistically significant Q test indicates that the dispersion among effect estimates exceeded that expected from sampling error alone. I² suggests that a large proportion of observed variation was associated with between-study inconsistency, although I² is imprecisely estimated when only three effects are available. The τ² estimate likewise needs cautious interpretation because REML estimation is unstable in a very small meta-analysis.

The heterogeneity summary figure is located at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/heterogeneity_summary.png`. The wide dispersion in the study-level estimates is also visible in the forest and distribution plots.

Potential explanations could include differences in dermatological condition, participant age, instrument variant, respondent, educational content, session number or duration, usual-care comparator, and follow-up time. These remain hypotheses only because the relevant study-level data were not supplied.

## Moderators

No valid moderator analysis could be conducted. Only three effects were pooled, and no study-level covariates were provided. With such a small k, meta-regression would be severely underpowered, susceptible to false findings, and unable to distinguish correlated study characteristics.

A bubble chart was generated at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/bubble_chart.png`; however, the context does not identify the covariate plotted or provide a meta-regression coefficient, confidence interval, or p value. ## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **0.4763** with p=**0.6234**. Thus, the test did not identify statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀=0** missing studies and yielded an adjusted Hedges’ g of **−0.051**, effectively unchanged from the unadjusted estimate. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **low**.

This finding should be interpreted very cautiously. With only three pooled effects, Egger’s test has extremely limited power and standard guidance generally discourages formal funnel-plot asymmetry testing in such small meta-analyses. Similarly, trim-and-fill cannot reliably exclude selective publication or selective outcome reporting when the evidence base is this sparse. “No detected asymmetry” is not equivalent to evidence that reporting bias is absent.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across three comparisons and 555 participants, group educational interventions did not show a statistically significant pooled effect on dermatology-related quality of life measured through eligible DLQI-family outcomes. The REML pooled Hedges’ g was −0.05099080323869676 (95% CI −0.497 to 0.395; p=0.8228595971367582), and the supplied analysis classified the point estimate as favoring group educational interventions. The confidence interval crossed the null and was broad enough to encompass effects in either numerical direction.

The most prominent feature of the evidence was inconsistency. Individual estimates ranged from −0.494198 to 0.246472, and heterogeneity was substantial (I²=82.37%; Q p=0.0021). This makes the average effect an incomplete summary: interventions, populations, outcome variants, respondent types, or assessment times may not have been sufficiently comparable. No subgroup or moderator analysis could clarify these possibilities.

### Clinical interpretation

DLQI-family instruments are scored such that lower raw scores generally indicate less quality-of-life impairment. Yet the current pooled estimate is not a native-scale mean difference. It is a standardized fallback generated because the effect measure could not be confirmed. Accordingly, the analysis does not establish how many DLQI points, if any, might change with group education, nor whether any change would meet an accepted threshold for clinical importance.

Moreover, the prespecified convention says that positive effects represent better outcomes with group education, whereas the supplied `favors` field classifies the negative pooled value as favoring group education. This likely reflects an unresolved raw-sign or arm-order convention. In accordance with the analysis instructions, the report uses the supplied `favors` classification, but the discrepancy must be resolved against original group means, arm coding, and scale direction before clinical claims are made.

### Certainty and applicability

The available evidence does not support a confident conclusion of benefit or harm. Confidence is reduced by the very small number of pooled comparisons, high heterogeneity, imprecision, absent study-level clinical characteristics, unavailable risk-of-bias judgments, and uncertainty about the effect measure and sign convention. Applicability to specific diagnoses, age groups, intervention formats, family respondents, or follow-up periods cannot be determined.

The absence of detected publication bias offers little reassurance because statistical tests are underpowered with k=3. Similarly, the fourth included study’s lack of a quantitative estimate raises the possibility of unavailable or unusable outcome data, but the reason was not reported.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` object was supplied, the results themselves reveal issues requiring verification:

1. Four studies were reported as included, but only three effect sizes were available for meta-analysis; the reason for the missing quantitative contribution was not supplied.
2. The native effect measure was unresolved, so Hedges’ g was used as a standardized fallback (`native_pooling_used: false`) rather than as a confirmed native-scale effect.
3. The prespecified positive-benefit convention does not align transparently with the negative pooled sign and the supplied classification that the result favors group education. Original arm coding, score direction, and transformations should be audited.
4. No study identities or scale-variant details were available, preventing confirmation that all pooled observations used only eligible DLQI infant, child, adult, or specified family outcomes.
5. The required separate child and infant age-stratified extraction for Liang 2017 could not be checked because neither Liang 2017 nor its stratum-specific data were identified in the supplied effect-size records.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations:

- The search strategy, screening flow, exclusion reasons, and protocol information were unavailable, preventing full verification of PRISMA search and selection reporting.
- Only three effect estimates were pooled, limiting precision, heterogeneity estimation, publication-bias assessment, and any moderator analysis.
- Four studies were described as included, but one did not contribute a reported effect; the reason is unknown.
- Study identifiers and clinical characteristics were absent, preventing assessment of population, intervention, comparator, follow-up, and outcome comparability.
- Risk-of-bias judgments were not supplied and could not be incorporated into interpretation or sensitivity analysis.
- The exact DLQI-family instrument and respondent for each effect were not reported, so adherence to the narrowly specified outcome definition could not be independently confirmed.
- The effect measure was not confirmed. Standardized fallback pooling limits clinical interpretability and prevents expression of the result in DLQI points.
- A direction-coding ambiguity remains between the stated positive-benefit convention, the numerical pooled sign, and the supplied arm-favoring classification.
- No prediction interval was supplied. Given k=3 and high heterogeneity, any newly calculated prediction interval would be unstable and was not invented.
- Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill are not dependable with only three effects; their negative findings do not rule out reporting bias.
- No bibliographic details for the included studies were provided, so study-specific citations cannot be supplied.

## Conclusion

The available random-effects meta-analysis of three comparisons (555 participants) found a pooled Hedges’ g of **−0.05099080323869676** (95% CI **−0.497 to 0.395**; p=**0.8228595971367582**), classified by the supplied results as favoring group educational interventions. The result was not statistically significant, and between-study heterogeneity was substantial (**I²=82.37%; τ²=0.126593; Q=12.325, p=0.0021**). No small-study effects were detected, but publication-bias analyses were severely underpowered.

Overall, the evidence does not establish that group educational interventions improve eligible DLQI-family outcomes compared with standard care only. Verification of the original studies, eligible instruments and respondents, age-stratified Liang 2017 results, arm coding, and native effect measure is necessary before the pooled result can be regarded as definitive or clinically interpretable.
