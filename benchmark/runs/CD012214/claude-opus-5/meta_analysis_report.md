# Metformin plus megestrol acetate versus megestrol acetate alone for histological regression of endometrial hyperplasia: a systematic review and meta-analysis of randomised comparisons

## Abstract

**Background.** Megestrol acetate (MA) is a standard fertility-sparing treatment for endometrial hyperplasia with or without atypia, but a substantial minority of women fail to regress. Metformin has been proposed as an adjunct on the basis of its insulin-sensitising and anti-proliferative effects on endometrial tissue. We synthesised randomised evidence on whether adding metformin to MA increases the rate of complete histological regression to normal (proliferative, secretory or atrophic) endometrium.

**Methods.** We systematically identified randomised comparisons of metformin plus megestrol acetate versus megestrol acetate alone in women with endometrial hyperplasia (with or without atypia). The prespecified outcome was complete histological regression at the end of the treatment course (approximately 3–6 months), defined as the number of women whose end-of-treatment endometrial biopsy or curettage showed normal endometrium, out of the number randomised/analysed per arm; persistence, progression and partial response were not counted as events. Counts for hyperplasia with and without atypia were summed where reported separately, percentages were converted to counts using arm sizes, and randomised group sizes were used where analysed Ns were not reported. Effects were pooled as odds ratios (OR) on the native log-odds scale using a random-effects model with REML estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression test and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Four randomised comparisons contributing four effect estimates and 281 randomised women were included. The pooled Odds Ratio (OR) for complete histological regression with metformin plus megestrol acetate versus megestrol acetate alone was **2.400 (95% CI 1.143 to 5.042; p = 0.0208; k = 4; n = 281)**. Native-scale (odds ratio) pooling was used, so this is a confirmed native-scale result rather than a standardized fallback. Under the prespecified convention for this review — an OR above 1 denotes more complete regression in the combination arm — the point estimate and the fact that the confidence interval excludes 1 are consistent with a benefit of adding metformin; however, the automated direction field for this analysis returned **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**, and this non-committal classification is reported as the formal direction label. Between-study heterogeneity was low (Q = 4.316, df = 3, p = 0.2293; I² = 11.75%; τ² = 0.080489), although three of the four study-level estimates favoured the combination and one did not. Egger's test showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept −0.3247, p = 0.1969) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized estimate 0.4801, essentially unchanged), supporting a low risk-of-publication-bias rating.

**Conclusions.** In this small body of randomised evidence, adding metformin to megestrol acetate was associated with an approximately two-and-a-half-fold increase in the odds of complete histological regression of endometrial hyperplasia at the end of treatment, with low statistical heterogeneity and no detectable small-study effects. The evidence base comprises only four trials and 281 women, the confidence interval is wide and extends close to the null, and one trial showed the opposite direction of effect; the finding should therefore be regarded as promising but not definitive, and confirmation in adequately powered trials with pre-specified histological endpoints and longer follow-up for relapse is required.


## 1. Introduction

Endometrial hyperplasia is a spectrum of abnormal endometrial proliferation that, in its atypical form (endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia), carries a substantial risk of concurrent or subsequent endometrioid endometrial carcinoma. Hysterectomy remains definitive management for atypical disease, but conservative, fertility-sparing treatment with high-dose progestins — most commonly oral megestrol acetate (MA) or a levonorgestrel-releasing intrauterine system — is widely used in young women wishing to preserve the uterus and in women who are poor surgical candidates. Reported complete regression rates with progestin monotherapy vary widely, and a clinically important proportion of women show persistent or progressive disease at the end of a standard 3–6 month treatment course.

The dominant risk-factor profile for endometrial hyperplasia — obesity, polycystic ovary syndrome, insulin resistance, type 2 diabetes — points to hyperinsulinaemia and activation of the insulin/IGF-1–PI3K–AKT–mTOR axis as drivers of endometrial proliferation. Metformin lowers circulating insulin, activates AMP-activated protein kinase, inhibits mTOR signalling and has shown anti-proliferative and pro-apoptotic effects in endometrial cell lines and in short-term pre-surgical window studies. These observations provide a coherent biological rationale for adding metformin to progestin therapy in order to raise the proportion of women achieving histological regression.

Several randomised trials have compared metformin plus megestrol acetate with megestrol acetate alone, but individually they are small and their results are not uniformly concordant. The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was therefore to determine whether, in women with endometrial hyperplasia with or without atypia, metformin plus megestrol acetate increases the rate of complete histological regression to normal (proliferative, secretory or atrophic) endometrium at the end of treatment compared with megestrol acetate alone.

**Review question (PICO).**
- **Population:** Women with histologically confirmed endometrial hyperplasia, with or without atypia.
- **Intervention:** Metformin plus megestrol acetate.
- **Comparator:** Megestrol acetate alone.
- **Outcome:** Complete histological regression of endometrial hyperplasia at the end of the treatment course (approximately 3–6 months), defined as return of the endometrial biopsy/curettage to normal endometrium (proliferative, secretory or atrophic).
- **Study design:** Randomised comparisons.

**Prespecified effect direction.** For this review an odds ratio above 1 indicates that metformin plus megestrol acetate produced *more* complete regression of endometrial hyperplasia to normal endometrium than megestrol acetate alone.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

The review and its reporting follow the PRISMA 2020 framework. The analysis plan — population, intervention, comparator, outcome definition, event-counting rules, effect measure and synthesis model — was fixed before synthesis. No amendments to the analysis plan were made after the effect estimates were inspected.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

Records were eligible if they reported a randomised comparison of metformin plus megestrol acetate against megestrol acetate alone in women with endometrial hyperplasia (atypical, non-atypical, or a mixed population), and reported, or allowed the derivation of, the number of women achieving complete histological regression at the end of the treatment course together with the number randomised or analysed in each arm. Narrative reviews, systematic reviews, editorials, duplicate publications of the same trial, and single-arm or non-randomised series were excluded. Where more than one report described the same randomised cohort, a single report was retained to avoid double-counting participants.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

Bibliographic databases (MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials), trial registries and the reference lists of relevant reviews were searched for randomised studies combining terms for endometrial hyperplasia or endometrial intraepithelial neoplasia with terms for metformin and for megestrol acetate or progestin therapy. No language restriction was applied at the screening stage.

### 2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Records were screened at title/abstract level and then at full text against the eligibility criteria. For each included comparison the following were extracted: trial identifier, arm sizes as randomised and as analysed, the number of women with complete histological regression per arm at the end of treatment, and the duration of the treatment course. Pre-specified data-handling rules were applied: (i) where hyperplasia with and without atypia were reported separately within a trial, event and denominator counts were summed into a single trial-level 2×2 table; (ii) where regression was reported only as a percentage, counts were back-calculated from the reported arm size; (iii) where the number analysed was not reported, the number randomised was used as the denominator, giving an intention-to-treat-like denominator. Only *complete* regression was counted as an event; persistence, progression and partial response were treated as non-events.

### 2.5 Effect measures and synthesis

The prespecified effect measure was the **Odds Ratio (OR)** for complete histological regression, specified explicitly in the review question (effect-measure source: explicit user request; no conflict with the measure reported by the primary studies was detected). Pooling was performed on the **native log-odds scale** (`native_pooling_used = true`); the reported pooled value is therefore a confirmed native-scale odds ratio and not a standardized fallback converted from another metric.

Effects were combined in a **random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of the between-study variance τ², with Wald-type 95% confidence intervals for the pooled log-odds ratio, back-transformed for presentation. Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q with its p-value, the I² statistic and τ². For graphical display and for the bias diagnostics, study-level estimates were additionally expressed on the standardized (Hedges' *g*) scale; these standardized values are presentational conversions of the same underlying 2×2 data and are clearly labelled as such throughout.

### 2.6 Publication bias and small-study effects

Small-study effects were assessed by Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and by the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. Both diagnostics are acknowledged to have low power when fewer than approximately ten studies are available.

### 2.7 Moderator analyses

Pre-specified moderator analyses (by atypia status, treatment duration, metformin dose and baseline metabolic phenotype) were planned, with formal meta-regression contingent on a sufficient number of contributing studies (conventionally at least ten studies per covariate).

## 3. Search and Screening Results

The search and screening process identified **four eligible randomised comparisons**, which together contributed **four effect estimates** and **281 randomised women** to the quantitative synthesis. Records were removed at the screening and full-text stages for the standard reasons: wrong design (non-randomised or single-arm), wrong comparison (metformin against a non-MA comparator, or progestin formulations other than megestrol acetate), review or commentary article type, duplicate reporting of an already-included randomised cohort, and absence of extractable end-of-treatment histological regression counts.

The pipeline record retained for this synthesis preserves the number of included studies and effect estimates but does **not** preserve the stage-by-stage record counts (records identified, duplicates removed, titles/abstracts screened, full texts assessed, exclusions by reason). We state this plainly rather than reconstructing a PRISMA flow diagram with fabricated numbers: the quantitative basis for all results below is the final set of four included randomised comparisons (k = 4, n = 281), and a complete PRISMA flow diagram with stage-level counts cannot be rendered from the available record.

Four randomised comparisons were included. Study-level identifiers in the analytic dataset are anonymised (`study_1_R1` through `study_4_R4`), each corresponding to one randomised comparison of metformin plus megestrol acetate versus megestrol acetate alone with an end-of-treatment histological regression endpoint assessed at approximately 3–6 months.

The four comparisons span a wide range of sample size, from 16 to 125 women, with a median of approximately 70. The largest comparison (study_1_R1, n = 125) and the smallest (study_3_R3, n = 16) both favour the combination; the second-largest (study_4_R4, n = 84) is the single comparison in which the point estimate favours megestrol acetate alone, though its confidence interval comfortably includes the null. All four comparisons were analysed as a single outcome family — complete histological regression at the end of a 3–6 month treatment course — so no separate outcome or timepoint groups were required, and all four contribute to one pooled estimate.

**Risk of bias.** Formal domain-level risk-of-bias ratings (RoB 2) were not retained in the analytic record for this synthesis and are therefore not tabulated. Readers should note the generic risk-of-bias concerns that apply to this literature: open-label administration of oral metformin against progestin monotherapy makes blinding of participants and clinicians difficult; histological outcome assessment may or may not have been performed by pathologists blinded to allocation; and gastrointestinal intolerance of metformin can generate differential attrition. The data-handling rule of using randomised group sizes where analysed Ns were not reported was applied to mitigate, but cannot eliminate, attrition-related bias.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Individual effect estimates were derived from end-of-treatment 2×2 tables of complete histological regression versus non-regression (persistence, progression or partial response) in each arm. On the standardized scale used for graphical display, the four estimates were 0.3798, 0.9447, 1.1453 and −0.2077 (Table 1), i.e. three positive values favouring the combination and one modestly negative value favouring megestrol acetate alone. Three of the four 95% confidence intervals exclude the null; the fourth (study_4_R4) spans the null.

The spread of estimates is visible in the effect distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`) and the relationship between estimate magnitude and study size is shown in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`). There is no simple monotone relationship between precision and effect magnitude: the single largest effect comes from the smallest trial (n = 16), as is common, but the most precise trial (n = 125) also yields a positive estimate whose interval excludes the null, while the negative estimate arises from a mid-sized trial (n = 84).

The pooled Odds Ratio (OR) for complete histological regression of endometrial hyperplasia with metformin plus megestrol acetate versus megestrol acetate alone is **2.400 (95% CI 1.143 to 5.042), p = 0.0208**, based on k = 4 comparisons and 281 randomised women. The effect measure was pooled natively on the odds-ratio scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), so this value is a confirmed native-scale odds ratio; it is not a standardized conversion or fallback estimate.

**Direction.** The formal direction field accompanying this analysis reads **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**, and that is the direction label we report. The field is non-committal because, for a ratio measure, whether a value above 1 is favourable depends entirely on which event was counted. In this review the event counted was explicitly *complete regression to normal endometrium* — a desirable event — and the prespecified convention states that an OR above 1 means metformin plus megestrol acetate produced more complete regression. Read against that prespecified convention, a pooled OR of 2.400 with a confidence interval lying entirely above 1 is consistent with greater complete regression in the combination arm. We report both facts side by side rather than overwriting the direction field: the automated classifier did not adjudicate a favoured arm, while the prespecified interpretive convention for this outcome points to the metformin-plus-megestrol-acetate arm.

**Magnitude in context.** An odds ratio of 2.40 corresponds, for an illustrative control-arm regression rate of 60%, to a combination-arm rate of approximately 78% (risk difference ≈ 18 percentage points; number needed to treat ≈ 6); for a control-arm rate of 75% it corresponds to approximately 88%. The lower confidence limit of 1.143 corresponds to only a marginal increase (roughly 60% → 63% at a 60% baseline), so the interval is compatible with anything from a clinically trivial to a large benefit.

The forest plot for this analysis is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. A cumulative meta-analysis, ordered by study contribution, is shown at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; it illustrates that the pooled estimate remains above the null throughout accumulation, with the interval narrowing but never excluding modest effects, and with the single discordant comparison pulling the cumulative estimate toward, but not across, the null.

**Note on multiple outcome groups.** The analytic record contains a single meta-analysis entry and no `meta_analysis_groups` with more than one member. All four included comparisons address the same outcome at the same timepoint (complete histological regression at the end of a 3–6 month course), so there is one pooled estimate only; no separate per-group k, effect, CI or heterogeneity statistics are applicable, and none have been invented.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was low and not statistically significant:

- **Cochran's Q** = 4.316 on 3 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.2293** — no significant evidence that the true effects differ across studies.
- **I²** = **11.75%** — only about 12% of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than within-study sampling error; conventionally interpreted as "low" or "might not be important".
- **τ²** = **0.080489** on the log-odds scale, corresponding to a between-study standard deviation of τ ≈ 0.284 log-odds units. A 95% prediction interval constructed on this basis is appreciably wider than the confidence interval and would include values below 1, meaning that the effect in a future, exchangeable trial cannot be guaranteed to be beneficial.

These statistics should be read against the fact that only four studies contribute. With k = 4, Q has very low power to detect genuine heterogeneity, I² is estimated with wide uncertainty (its own confidence interval would extend from 0% to a high value), and REML estimates of τ² are known to be downwardly unstable in small samples. The reassuring heterogeneity statistics therefore mean "no *detectable* inconsistency", not "demonstrably consistent effects".

It is also worth stating explicitly that the low I² coexists with a qualitative discordance: three comparisons estimate benefit and one estimates a small harm. Statistically this is reconcilable — study_4_R4's interval (−0.637 to 0.222 on the standardized scale) overlaps those of the other trials — but it is clinically relevant, and it is the main reason the pooled interval remains wide. A graphical summary of the heterogeneity diagnostics is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 8. Moderator Analyses

No moderator or subgroup analyses were performed. With k = 4 studies and a single covariate requiring at least ten studies by conventional guidance, meta-regression would be severely over-fitted and its coefficients uninterpretable; subgroup analyses by atypia status, metformin dose, treatment duration or baseline body-mass index/insulin-resistance status would each rest on one or two studies per subgroup. The pre-specified moderator analyses are therefore reported as **not estimable** rather than being attempted.

This is a substantive limitation, not merely a technical one. The two clinically most important potential modifiers — whether the hyperplasia was atypical, and whether the participants were insulin-resistant — are precisely the variables on which a metabolic adjunct such as metformin might be expected to show differential efficacy. Because this review's data-handling rule summed events for hyperplasia with and without atypia where trials reported them separately, the pooled estimate is an average across atypia strata and cannot distinguish between them. The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) displays effect magnitude against study size and provides only an informal, non-inferential view of whether smaller studies report larger effects.

## 9. Publication Bias

Small-study effects were assessed with two complementary methods:

- **Egger's regression test:** intercept **−0.3247**, **p = 0.1969** — no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry.
- **Trim-and-fill:** **k₀ = 0** studies imputed; the adjusted standardized estimate is **0.4801**, essentially identical to the unadjusted standardized equivalent of the pooled OR (ln 2.400 ÷ (π/√3) ≈ 0.483). The pooled estimate is therefore unchanged after adjustment.
- **Overall risk-of-publication-bias rating: low.**

Taken together, Egger's test and trim-and-fill provide no evidence of small-study effects or selective publication in this dataset. The near-exact agreement between the trim-and-fill adjusted value (0.4801) and the unadjusted standardized equivalent of the native pooled OR also serves as an internal consistency check on the two pooling scales.

These conclusions must be heavily qualified by power. Both Egger's test and trim-and-fill are unreliable with fewer than approximately ten studies; with k = 4 the regression has one residual degree of freedom and essentially no ability to detect asymmetry, and trim-and-fill will almost always impute zero studies. The "low risk" label should therefore be read as *no detectable* bias rather than demonstrated absence of bias. Additional, non-statistical considerations reinforce caution: small open-label trials of a cheap, widely available repurposed drug are susceptible to selective reporting of the most favourable histological endpoint, and trials with null results in this field may remain unpublished or confined to conference abstracts. A funnel plot with four points is not informative and is not presented as a standalone inferential object.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 Principal findings

Pooling four randomised comparisons and 281 women, adding metformin to megestrol acetate was associated with a pooled Odds Ratio (OR) of **2.400 (95% CI 1.143 to 5.042; p = 0.0208)** for complete histological regression of endometrial hyperplasia to normal endometrium at the end of a 3–6 month treatment course. Statistical heterogeneity was low (I² = 11.75%; Q p = 0.2293; τ² = 0.080489), and there was no detectable publication bias (Egger p = 0.1969; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0). Under the review's prespecified convention that OR > 1 denotes more complete regression with the combination, this constitutes a nominally statistically significant and clinically appreciable advantage for metformin plus megestrol acetate, while the formal `favors` field for the analysis returned "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)" and did not adjudicate a favoured arm.

### 10.2 Interpretation and biological plausibility

The direction and approximate magnitude of the pooled estimate are consistent with the mechanistic rationale for metformin in hyperplastic endometrium. Insulin resistance and hyperinsulinaemia are near-universal in the obese and polycystic-ovary-syndrome populations in whom endometrial hyperplasia arises, and insulin/IGF-1 signalling converges on the PI3K–AKT–mTOR pathway that also mediates progestin resistance. Metformin's AMPK-dependent and AMPK-independent inhibition of mTOR, its reported restoration of progesterone-receptor expression in endometrial tissue, and its systemic effects on insulin and androgen levels all offer plausible routes by which it could convert progestin non-responders into responders. The observation that three of four trials, including the largest, point in this direction strengthens the case.

Equally, several features counsel restraint. First, the lower confidence limit (1.143) is close to the null, so the data are compatible with a benefit too small to change practice. Second, one trial (study_4_R4, n = 84) estimated a point effect in the opposite direction; with only four trials, the pooled estimate is sensitive to how this trial is weighted, and a sensitivity analysis excluding it would necessarily yield a larger and more uniform apparent benefit — which is a reason to distrust over-interpretation, not a reason to exclude it. Third, complete histological regression at 3–6 months is a surrogate for the outcomes women care about: durable remission, avoidance of hysterectomy, live birth, and prevention of progression to carcinoma. None of these downstream endpoints is addressed by the present synthesis. Fourth, control-arm regression rates with high-dose megestrol acetate are already high in several series, which compresses the achievable absolute benefit and makes odds ratios appear large relative to modest risk differences.

### 10.3 Comparison with existing literature

The present estimate sits comfortably within the range reported by earlier syntheses of metformin as an adjunct to progestin therapy in endometrial hyperplasia and early endometrial cancer, which have generally reported pooled odds or risk ratios favouring combination therapy with intervals that either marginally exclude or marginally include the null depending on the exact set of trials and the handling of atypia strata. The consistency of direction across syntheses, together with the mechanistic rationale, is reassuring; the persistent width of the intervals across syntheses reflects the common underlying problem — a literature of small, single-centre, open-label trials.

### 10.4 Implications for practice and research

For practice, these data do not establish metformin plus megestrol acetate as standard care, but they provide a reasonable basis for considering metformin as an adjunct in women with insulin-resistant phenotypes who are undergoing fertility-sparing or conservative progestin management, particularly given metformin's low cost, familiar safety profile and independent metabolic benefits in this population. Such use should be framed as an adjunct of uncertain incremental benefit rather than an established one, and should not displace standard histological surveillance.

For research, the priorities are clear: adequately powered multicentre randomised trials with (i) a pre-specified, centrally and blindly adjudicated definition of complete histological regression; (ii) pre-specified stratification by atypia status and by baseline insulin resistance; (iii) reporting of both end-of-treatment and durable (12-month and later) regression, relapse and hysterectomy-free survival; (iv) complete reporting of analysed denominators and reasons for attrition, including metformin intolerance; and (v) registration with published protocols to allow assessment of selective outcome reporting.

## 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No validation flags were emitted by the analytic pipeline for this synthesis — that is, the record contains no logged exclusions of review articles or duplicate reports, no unclassifiable outcomes, no effect measures requiring separate pooling, and no trial reports flagged for removal to avoid double-counting. The absence of logged flags should be read as "none recorded", not as positive verification that no such problems exist in the underlying literature. The following data-quality observations are stated plainly for transparency:

1. **Screening counts are not recoverable.** The record preserves k = 4 included comparisons and n = 281 participants but not the stage-wise PRISMA counts; a complete flow diagram cannot be produced without fabricating numbers, and so none is presented.
2. **Study identities are anonymised** in the analytic dataset (`study_1_R1`–`study_4_R4`); the per-study estimates in Table 1 are therefore not linked to named publications in this report.
3. **Two reporting scales coexist.** The authoritative pooled result is a native odds ratio (`native_pooling_used = true`, `measure_label` = "Odds Ratio (OR)"); the per-study values and the trim-and-fill adjusted value (0.4801) are expressed on the standardized Hedges' *g* scale for display and bias diagnostics. These are two presentations of the same data; the standardized values are not, and should not be reported as, the primary effect measure.
4. **The direction field is non-committal.** The `favors` field returns "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)". This is reported as-is, alongside the review's prespecified interpretive convention, rather than being replaced with an inferred direction.
5. **Denominator conventions.** Where analysed Ns were unavailable, randomised arm sizes were used, and where regression was reported only as a percentage, counts were back-calculated from arm sizes. Both conventions introduce a small amount of reconstruction error into the 2×2 tables.
6. **Atypia strata were merged.** Counts for hyperplasia with and without atypia were summed where reported separately, so the pooled estimate averages over a prognostically heterogeneous population.
7. **Risk-of-bias assessments are not available** in the retained record and are therefore described only generically in Section 4.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base.** Four comparisons and 281 women provide limited precision; the 95% CI (1.143 to 5.042) spans a fourfold range and extends close to the null.
2. **Low power of heterogeneity and bias diagnostics.** With k = 4, Q, I², Egger's test and trim-and-fill are all underpowered; the low I² (11.75%) and low bias risk should be read as absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence.
3. **Unstable between-study variance.** REML estimation of τ² (0.080489) is downwardly biased and imprecise at k = 4, so the random-effects confidence interval may be optimistically narrow; a prediction interval would include non-beneficial values.
4. **One discordant trial.** study_4_R4 (n = 84) estimates an effect in the opposite direction; the pooled result is correspondingly sensitive to the inclusion and weighting of a single study.
5. **Surrogate, short-term endpoint.** Complete histological regression at 3–6 months does not capture durability of response, relapse, progression to carcinoma, hysterectomy avoidance or live birth.
6. **Clinical and methodological heterogeneity not modelled.** Atypia status, metformin dose and duration, megestrol acetate dose, sampling method (pipelle versus curettage versus hysteroscopic biopsy) and baseline metabolic phenotype all varied or were unreported, and could not be examined as moderators.
7. **Blinding and attrition.** Trials of this type are typically open-label, and metformin's gastrointestinal side effects can produce differential dropout; outcome assessors' blinding to allocation is frequently unreported.
8. **Reconstructed denominators and merged strata** (Section 11) introduce minor imprecision into the extracted 2×2 tables.
9. **Unavailable risk-of-bias ratings** prevent certainty grading of the body of evidence; under GRADE, the combination of small size, imprecision and probable performance/detection-bias concerns would most plausibly place this evidence at **low** certainty.
10. **Possible unpublished null trials.** Small trials of a repurposed generic drug are at elevated risk of remaining unpublished, a risk the available diagnostics cannot exclude.

---

## 13. Conclusion

Across four randomised comparisons including 281 women with endometrial hyperplasia with or without atypia, metformin added to megestrol acetate yielded a pooled **Odds Ratio (OR) of 2.400 (95% CI 1.143 to 5.042; p = 0.0208)** for complete histological regression to normal endometrium at the end of a 3–6 month treatment course, pooled natively on the odds-ratio scale using a REML random-effects model. Heterogeneity was low (Q = 4.316, df = 3, p = 0.2293; I² = 11.75%; τ² = 0.080489), and no small-study effects were detectable (Egger intercept −0.3247, p = 0.1969; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted standardized estimate 0.4801; overall bias risk rated low). The formal direction field for this analysis returned "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"; under the review's prespecified convention that an OR above 1 denotes more complete regression with the combination, the pooled estimate and its confidence interval are consistent with greater regression in the metformin-plus-megestrol-acetate arm.

The overall picture is of a plausible, consistently directioned and potentially clinically meaningful benefit resting on a thin evidence base: four small trials, one of them discordant, a confidence interval whose lower bound approaches the null, bias and heterogeneity diagnostics that are too underpowered to be reassuring, and a short-term surrogate endpoint. Metformin plus megestrol acetate is a reasonable candidate for further evaluation and may be a defensible adjunct in insulin-resistant women undergoing conservative management, but adequately powered, stratified, protocol-registered randomised trials reporting durable regression, relapse and hysterectomy-free outcomes are needed before it can be recommended as standard care.
