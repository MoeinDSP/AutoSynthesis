# Communication-Only Information Interventions and Knowledge About Mammography: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Women considering mammography need balanced information about screening benefits and harms. Communication-only decision aids and information interventions may improve knowledge without using an explicit values-clarification exercise.

### Objective
To estimate, among women eligible for breast cancer screening, the effect of randomized communication-only interventions providing information about mammography benefits and harms, compared with usual information, a standard leaflet, or no intervention, on overall continuous knowledge at the first post-intervention assessment (immediately after intervention to approximately one month).

### Methods
The supplied review dataset contained five studies, of which four contributed an eligible continuous knowledge effect size. Post-intervention knowledge was synthesized as the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD), with higher scores indicating greater knowledge. A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were assessed using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill, while recognizing the severe limitations of these procedures with only four studies.

### Results
Four studies involving 1,733 participants contributed to the meta-analysis. Individual SMDs ranged from 0.128034 to 1.073391. The pooled SMD was 0.4428904122818846 (95% CI 0.044 to 0.841; p=0.029397227340858142), favoring the communication-only intervention. Heterogeneity was considerable (Q=29.039, I²=93.51%, τ²=0.153069; reported Q-test p=0.0). Egger’s intercept was −0.579 (p=0.126). Trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study and produced an adjusted estimate of 0.5494. Publication-bias risk was classified as moderate.

### Conclusions
Communication-only breast cancer screening decision aids or information interventions improved early post-intervention knowledge on average compared with usual information, a standard leaflet, or no intervention. However, the very high heterogeneity, small number of studies, incomplete contribution of one study in the supplied dataset, and limited reliability of publication-bias analyses warrant cautious interpretation. The evidence supports an average knowledge benefit but does not establish that effects are consistent across interventions or settings.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model fitted using **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** included four studies and 1,733 participants. The **pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** was **0.4428904122818846** (95% CI **0.044 to 0.841**; p=**0.029397227340858142**). The result favors the **communication-only breast cancer screening decision aid or information intervention without explicit values clarification**.

This pooled estimate indicates a statistically detectable average improvement in early post-intervention knowledge. Because it is standardized, the estimate is expressed in pooled standard-deviation units rather than in points on any single knowledge questionnaire. It should not be interpreted as a common absolute increase in the number or percentage of correct answers.

The confidence interval is wide relative to the point estimate. Its lower bound is close to the null, while its upper bound is compatible with a substantially larger average effect. Therefore, although the estimated mean effect favors intervention, its magnitude is imprecisely estimated.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was very high:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 29.039
- **Q-test p-value:** 0.0 as reported in the supplied output
- **I²:** 93.51%
- **τ²:** 0.153069

The I² value indicates that a very large proportion of the observed dispersion was attributed to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error under the model. The nonzero τ² likewise indicates material variation in underlying effects. This is visible in the range from an SMD of 0.128034 in `study_2_R2` to 1.073391 in `study_4_R4`.

The pooled effect should therefore be interpreted as an average across meaningfully different study effects, not as a single effect expected uniformly in every setting. Plausible sources could include differences in information content, presentation of numerical risk, delivery format, participant characteristics, comparator information, knowledge instruments, or assessment timing. These explanations remain hypotheses because the study-level characteristics required to test them were not supplied.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression yielded an intercept of **−0.579** with p=**0.126**. This test was not statistically significant and therefore did not provide strong evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry. Trim-and-fill estimated **one** potentially missing study and gave an adjusted estimate of **0.5494**. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **moderate**.

These findings do not rule out publication bias. With only four observed studies, Egger’s test has low power and may produce unstable results; funnel-plot-based procedures are generally difficult to interpret with such a small evidence base. Moreover, asymmetry can reflect heterogeneity or other small-study effects rather than selective publication, and trim-and-fill relies on assumptions that may not hold when heterogeneity is extreme. The adjusted value should consequently be treated as a sensitivity estimate rather than a corrected or definitive effect.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This meta-analysis found that randomized communication-only interventions providing information on mammography benefits and harms were associated with better knowledge at the first assessment after intervention than usual information, a standard leaflet, or no intervention. Across four studies and 1,733 participants, the pooled SMD was 0.4428904122818846, and its 95% confidence interval excluded the null narrowly. Three of four study estimates favored intervention with confidence intervals excluding zero; the fourth was smaller and statistically inconclusive.

The central interpretive issue is the very high heterogeneity. In particular, the SMD of 1.073391 in `study_4_R4` was considerably larger than the estimates of 0.128034 to 0.325040 in the other studies. The REML random-effects model accommodates between-study variability statistically, but it does not explain that variability. As a result, the pooled estimate summarizes the center of a dispersed evidence base and should not be assumed to apply identically across specific communication formats, populations, or screening contexts.

### Clinical and policy interpretation

Knowledge is a key prerequisite for informed decision-making about mammography because screening involves trade-offs among potential reductions in breast cancer mortality, false-positive findings, overdiagnosis, and downstream testing or treatment. The observed average benefit suggests that presenting balanced benefit-and-harm information can improve early understanding even without an explicit values-clarification exercise.

Nevertheless, this analysis addresses knowledge only. It does not demonstrate improved informed choice, better alignment between preferences and behavior, reduced decisional conflict, changes in screening uptake, or changes in health outcomes. Nor does it establish that communication-only tools are superior to decision aids containing explicit values-clarification exercises, because that was not the comparison synthesized.

### Strengths

The synthesis used a focused intervention definition, a clearly delimited comparator, a single outcome domain, and a narrow early follow-up window. Restricting the analysis to one overall continuous knowledge score per trial reduced the risk of double-counting multiple subscales or correlated outcomes. Use of the SMD permitted synthesis across potentially different knowledge instruments, and a REML random-effects model was appropriate given the observed between-study variation.

No formal `validation_flags` were included in the supplied context. Nonetheless, two data-completeness issues are evident from the provided results:

1. The pipeline listed five studies but supplied only four analyzable effect sizes. The reason the fifth study did not contribute to this outcome analysis was not reported.

These points are stated as limitations of the available dataset rather than inferred exclusions.

## Limitations

First, only four studies were pooled. This limits precision, makes estimation of between-study variance uncertain, and prevents reliable moderator analysis. It also substantially weakens the interpretability of Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

Second, heterogeneity was extreme (I²=93.51%). Although the REML model incorporated heterogeneity, the reasons for it could not be investigated. The pooled result may therefore conceal intervention- or context-specific differences.

Third, one of the five studies in the pipeline did not contribute an analyzable effect, and the reason was unavailable. Selective availability of outcome data cannot be assessed.

Fourth, the supplied context contained no risk-of-bias assessment. Potential limitations in randomization, allocation concealment, attrition, outcome measurement, or selective reporting therefore could not be incorporated into interpretation or certainty assessment.

Fifth, the report lacked complete search and screening documentation. Search comprehensiveness, language or publication-status restrictions, duplicate handling, and reasons for full-text exclusions could not be evaluated. Full PRISMA flow reporting was consequently not possible from the available information.

Sixth, standardized effects improve comparability across instruments but reduce direct clinical interpretability. Without the original knowledge scales and arm-level descriptive statistics, the pooled SMD cannot be translated into a common number of correct responses.

Seventh, the analysis was restricted to the first assessment from immediately after intervention to approximately one month. It establishes neither persistence of knowledge gains nor effects at longer follow-up.

Finally, the review deliberately excluded outcomes other than knowledge. No conclusions should be drawn here about informed choice, decisional conflict, anxiety, screening intentions, mammography uptake, or clinical outcomes.

## Conclusion

Randomized communication-only breast cancer screening decision aids or information interventions improved knowledge at the first post-intervention assessment, on average, compared with usual information, a standard leaflet, or no intervention. The pooled SMD was **0.4428904122818846** (95% CI **0.044 to 0.841**; p=**0.029397227340858142**), favoring the communication-only intervention.

Confidence in a broadly applicable effect magnitude is limited by substantial between-study heterogeneity, the small number of studies, incomplete information for one study in the pipeline, absent risk-of-bias data, and limited publication-bias diagnostics. Future trials and syntheses should report validated total knowledge scores, complete arm-level data, longer-term assessments, detailed intervention components, and sufficient study characteristics to investigate why effects vary.
