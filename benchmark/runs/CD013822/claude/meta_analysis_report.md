# Communication-Only Interventions and Knowledge About Mammography Screening: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Continuous Knowledge Scores

## Abstract

**Background.** Women invited to mammography screening are frequently poorly informed about the balance of benefits and harms, including overdiagnosis and false-positive results. Decision aids and information materials that present benefit-and-harm information without an explicit values-clarification exercise ("communication-only" interventions) are widely used, but their effect on knowledge has not been quantified separately from full decision-support packages.

**Objective.** To estimate the effect of communication-only interventions about mammography screening, compared with usual information, a standard leaflet, or no intervention, on knowledge about breast cancer screening measured as a continuous score in women eligible for screening.

**Methods.** Randomised and cluster-randomised trials in screening-eligible women were eligible. The outcome was a continuous knowledge score (number or percentage of correct answers covering benefits, harms, overdiagnosis, false positives and screening interval), extracted as the post-intervention mean, SD and number analysed per arm at the first assessment after the intervention (immediately post-intervention up to approximately one month); later follow-ups were not used. One overall knowledge score per trial was taken, preferring total scores over sub-scales. SDs were derived from confidence intervals, p-values or standard errors where necessary (SD = SE × √n; SD ≈ √n × CI width ÷ 3.92), and cluster-randomised sample sizes were deflated by the design effect 1 + (average cluster size − 1) × ICC. Standardized mean differences (Hedges' g bias-corrected) were pooled under a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Heterogeneity was summarised with Q, τ² and I².

**Results.** Five records were carried through the screening and extraction pipeline, of which 2 trials (n = 684 women analysed) contributed usable continuous knowledge data to the quantitative synthesis. Study-level effects were g = 0.325 (95% CI 0.116 to 0.534; n = 355) and g = 0.128 (95% CI −0.088 to 0.344; n = 329). The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 0.228 (95% CI 0.035 to 0.422; p = 0.020), favouring the communication/information-only intervention. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate but very imprecisely estimated (Q = 1.644, df = 1, p = 0.1997; I² = 39.18%; τ² = 0.007603, τ = 0.087). Publication bias could not be assessed with k = 2 and no moderator analysis was estimable.

**Conclusions.** Communication-only interventions produced a small, statistically significant improvement in knowledge about breast cancer screening relative to usual information or no intervention. The lower confidence limit lies close to the null and the evidence base comprises only two trials, so the magnitude and consistency of the benefit remain uncertain. The effect measure was pooled natively on the SMD scale as requested.

## 1. Introduction

Population mammography screening programmes invite millions of women each year, and in most jurisdictions attendance is encouraged through invitation letters and accompanying information materials. Over the past two decades a substantial literature has documented that women's understanding of the quantitative balance of screening benefits and harms is poor: mortality benefit is typically overestimated, while overdiagnosis, overtreatment and the cumulative risk of false-positive recall are often unknown or substantially underestimated. Because informed choice — rather than maximal uptake — is now an explicit policy goal in several screening programmes, interventions that improve knowledge have become an outcome of interest in their own right.

Decision support for screening is heterogeneous in design. Some interventions combine information provision with an explicit values-clarification exercise (for example, structured weighing of attributes, rating scales or explicit preference elicitation), whereas others deliver information only — a plain-language leaflet, an evidence-based decision aid without a preference exercise, or a clinician-delivered consultation presenting numerical benefits and harms. These two intervention classes may plausibly differ in their effect on knowledge: values clarification occupies cognitive and consultation resources without adding factual content, whereas information-only formats concentrate entirely on content delivery. Pooling the two classes together, as many earlier reviews have done, therefore risks obscuring the specific contribution of the informational component.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses a deliberately narrow question: **among women eligible for breast cancer screening, what is the effect of communication-only interventions about mammography screening — decision aids, leaflets or consultations presenting information on benefits and harms, without an explicit values-clarification exercise — compared with usual information or no intervention, on knowledge about breast cancer screening measured as a continuous score?**

A positive Standardized Mean Difference in this review means that women receiving the communication-only intervention had **better (higher) knowledge** about breast cancer screening than women receiving usual information, a standard leaflet, or no intervention — that is, the effect favours the intervention.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Women eligible for breast cancer (mammography) screening, including women approaching the age of first invitation, women within the routine invitation age range, and women attending screening programmes or primary care for screening-related discussion.

**Intervention.** Any communication- or information-only intervention about mammography screening: an evidence-based decision aid, printed or online leaflet, booklet, web page, or clinician-delivered consultation that presented information on the benefits and harms of screening **without** an explicit values-clarification exercise.

**Comparator.** Usual information (e.g. the standard programme invitation material), a standard/control leaflet, or no intervention.

**Outcome.** A continuous knowledge score about breast cancer screening or mammography — for example the number or percentage of correct answers on a questionnaire covering benefits, harms, overdiagnosis, false-positive results, and the screening interval. One overall knowledge score per trial was used, preferring a total score (e.g. conceptual plus numerical knowledge combined) over individual sub-scales.

**Study design.** Randomised controlled trials, including cluster-randomised trials.

**Timepoint.** The first assessment after the intervention (immediately post-intervention up to approximately one month). Later follow-up assessments were not used, so that the synthesis reflects the immediate informational effect rather than knowledge decay.

### 2.2 Information sources and selection

Records identified through the review pipeline were screened against the criteria above at title/abstract and then at full-text level. Studies were excluded where the intervention incorporated a values-clarification exercise, where knowledge was reported only dichotomously (e.g. proportion "adequately informed") with no recoverable continuous score, where no eligible comparator arm existed, or where the knowledge assessment fell outside the pre-specified post-intervention window.

### 2.3 Flow of records

Five study records entered the extraction stage of the pipeline. Of these, **two randomised trials provided extractable post-intervention continuous knowledge data** (means, SDs or recoverable dispersion statistics, and numbers analysed per arm) and were carried into the quantitative synthesis; the remaining three did not yield a usable continuous knowledge effect size at the pre-specified first post-intervention timepoint and therefore contribute to the narrative description of the evidence base only. The synthesis thus rests on `k = 2` effect sizes and `n = 684` women analysed.

### 2.4 Data extraction and preparation

For each trial we extracted the post-intervention mean knowledge score, its SD, and the number of women analysed in each arm. Where SDs were not reported directly they were reconstructed from reported confidence intervals, p-values, or standard errors using the standard conversions SD = SE × √n and SD ≈ √n × CI width ÷ 3.92. For cluster-randomised designs, arm sample sizes were deflated by the design effect 1 + (average cluster size − 1) × ICC, so that the precision assigned to such trials reflects the clustered allocation rather than the raw number of participants.

## 4. Effect Sizes

Study-level effects were expressed as standardized mean differences with the Hedges' *g* small-sample bias correction, computed from post-intervention means, SDs and arm sizes (after design-effect deflation where applicable). The sign convention is treatment minus control, so positive values indicate higher knowledge in the communication-only arm.

- **study_1_R1:** g = 0.32504, 95% CI [0.116, 0.534], n = 355. This is a small-to-moderate effect whose confidence interval excludes zero.
- **study_2_R2:** g = 0.128034, 95% CI [−0.088, 0.344], n = 329. The point estimate is in the same direction but smaller, and the interval is compatible with no effect and with a small effect in either direction.

Implied standard errors, derived from the reported interval widths, are approximately 0.107 (study_1_R1) and 0.110 (study_2_R2); corresponding sampling variances are approximately 0.0114 and 0.0121. With the estimated between-study variance added, the random-effects weights are almost exactly equal (approximately 50.5% and 49.5%), so the pooled estimate sits close to the unweighted average of the two study effects.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of the between-study variance was fitted to the two effect sizes. The effect measure was the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, specified by explicit user request; **native pooling on the SMD scale was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the pooled value below is a confirmed result on the requested effect-measure scale and not a converted fallback.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Number of studies (k) | 2 |
| Total participants analysed | 684 |
| **Pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** | **0.228** |
| 95% confidence interval | 0.035 to 0.422 |
| p-value | 0.020 |
| Estimation method | REML random-effects |
| Favours | Communication/information-only intervention about breast cancer screening (decision aid, leaflet, or consultation with benefit-and-harm information, no values-clarification exercise) |

The pooled Standardized Mean Difference of 0.228 (95% CI 0.035 to 0.422; p = 0.020) **favours the communication/information-only intervention**: women who received a decision aid, leaflet or consultation conveying benefit-and-harm information scored higher on continuous knowledge measures than women receiving usual information, a standard leaflet, or no intervention. By conventional benchmarks (0.2 small, 0.5 moderate, 0.8 large) this is a small effect. The lower confidence limit of 0.035 lies close to the null, so the data are also compatible with a knowledge gain that is negligible in practical terms; the upper limit of 0.422 is compatible with an effect approaching moderate size.

The forest plot of individual and pooled estimates is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. A cumulative meta-analysis, showing how the pooled estimate evolves as each study is added, is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; with only two studies this plot is descriptive rather than inferential and simply shows the estimate shrinking from 0.325 toward 0.228 when the second trial is added.

Because only one pooled analysis was specified (a single outcome construct at a single, pre-specified timepoint), no separate outcome or timepoint groups were analysed; the single estimate above applies to the whole synthesis.

## 6. Heterogeneity

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 1.644 |
| Degrees of freedom | 1 |
| p-value for Q | 0.1997 |
| I² | 39.18% |
| τ² | 0.007603 |
| τ | 0.087 |

The point estimate of I² = 39.18% falls in the conventionally "moderate" band, indicating that roughly two-fifths of the observed variability in effects might reflect genuine between-study differences rather than sampling error. Cochran's Q was not statistically significant (Q = 1.644, df = 1, p = 0.1997), so the null hypothesis of homogeneity is not rejected. The estimated between-study standard deviation, τ = 0.087, is small relative to the pooled effect of 0.228.

These heterogeneity statistics must be interpreted with great caution. With k = 2 studies there is a single degree of freedom for the Q test, and both τ² and I² are estimated with extremely low precision; their confidence intervals would be very wide and would span from zero (complete homogeneity) to substantial heterogeneity. The apparent difference between the two study effects (0.325 vs 0.128) is entirely consistent with sampling variation around a common underlying effect. Accordingly, the moderate I² should not be read as substantive evidence of real effect-size variation across settings, intervention formats or knowledge instruments. A graphical summary of the heterogeneity diagnostics is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 7. Moderator Analyses

No moderator or subgroup analyses were performed. Meta-regression requires, at minimum, more studies than covariates and, in practice, a substantially larger k before estimates are interpretable; with two effect sizes any moderator model would be saturated or non-identifiable. Pre-specified moderators of potential interest — intervention format (printed leaflet vs interactive decision aid vs consultation), delivery setting (screening programme vs primary care), presence or absence of explicit numerical overdiagnosis information, instrument content and scoring, and whether the trial was individually or cluster randomised — therefore remain unexamined.

A bubble chart was generated as part of the standard output set (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`); with k = 2 it should be read purely as a descriptive display of effect size against precision and not as evidence of any moderator relationship.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Formal assessment of publication bias and small-study effects was **not possible**. Funnel-plot asymmetry, Egger's regression test, Begg's rank correlation test and trim-and-fill all require a minimum of approximately 10 studies to have any useful power; with k = 2 these procedures are uninformative and were not attempted. No conclusion — positive or negative — can therefore be drawn about whether the available estimate is inflated by selective reporting or selective publication.

Two contextual considerations are worth noting. First, knowledge is often a secondary outcome in screening decision-aid trials, which may make selective non-reporting of null knowledge findings somewhat less likely than for primary outcomes, but this is speculative. Second, three of the five records that entered extraction did not yield a usable continuous knowledge score at the pre-specified timepoint; if the availability of extractable knowledge data is correlated with the size or direction of the knowledge effect, the present estimate could be biased in an unknown direction. This is an availability-of-data limitation rather than publication bias per se, but it acts on the pooled estimate in a similar way.

## 9. Discussion

Across two randomised trials including 684 women, communication-only interventions about mammography screening were associated with a small but statistically significant improvement in knowledge about breast cancer screening compared with usual information, a standard leaflet or no intervention (pooled SMD 0.228, 95% CI 0.035 to 0.422, p = 0.020, favouring the intervention). Both trials pointed in the same direction, and the near-equal weighting of the two studies means the pooled value is not driven by a single trial.

The finding is coherent with the broader literature on screening decision support, which has repeatedly shown that well-constructed, evidence-based information materials can raise measured knowledge of screening benefits and harms. What this analysis adds is that the knowledge gain does not depend on a values-clarification exercise: delivering benefit-and-harm information alone — through a leaflet, a decision aid without a preference exercise, or a structured consultation — was sufficient to shift continuous knowledge scores. That is practically relevant, because information-only formats are cheaper to produce, easier to embed in existing invitation mailings, and less demanding of consultation time than full decision-support packages.

The magnitude, however, warrants restraint. An SMD of roughly 0.23 corresponds to a shift of under a quarter of a standard deviation in questionnaire score — on a typical multi-item knowledge instrument this might represent well under one additional correct answer on average. Whether that translates into a meaningfully better-informed choice depends on where on the score distribution the gain falls: moving women from substantial misconception to approximate accuracy about overdiagnosis would matter far more than an equivalent-sized gain on peripheral items. The included trials' total scores cannot distinguish these possibilities, because sub-scale detail was deliberately set aside in favour of one overall score per trial.

The measurement window also shapes interpretation. By design, only the first post-intervention assessment (immediately post-intervention up to approximately one month) was used. This isolates the immediate informational effect but says nothing about durability. Evidence from other screening contexts suggests knowledge gains attenuate over months, so the present estimate should be read as an upper bound on sustained effect rather than as a durable change in understanding.

Finally, the comparator deserves emphasis. The control condition in these trials was usual information or a standard leaflet in at least some instances, not an informational vacuum. Where the control material itself contains some benefit-and-harm content, the contrast measures the *incremental* value of an enhanced communication intervention, which will naturally be smaller than the contrast against no information at all. A small pooled effect against an active comparator is therefore less disappointing than the same effect against a true no-information control.

## 10. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base.** Only two trials contributed effect sizes. The pooled estimate is consequently fragile: removing either study changes the result materially (to 0.325 or 0.128 respectively), and the lower confidence limit of 0.035 sits close to the null.
2. **Heterogeneity cannot be characterised.** With one degree of freedom, I² = 39.18% and τ² = 0.007603 are estimated with negligible precision and should not be interpreted substantively. The non-significant Q test (p = 0.1997) neither establishes nor rules out real between-study variation.
3. **No moderator analysis.** Differences in intervention format, delivery channel, setting, instrument content and randomisation unit could not be examined, so the conditions under which communication-only interventions work best remain unknown.
4. **Publication bias unassessable.** No funnel-plot or regression-based assessment is meaningful at k = 2; the possibility of small-study or selective-reporting effects cannot be excluded.
5. **Incomplete data capture.** Three of the five records entering extraction did not provide a usable continuous knowledge score at the pre-specified first post-intervention assessment and are not represented in the pooled estimate. The direction of any resulting bias is unknown.
6. **Derived dispersion statistics and design-effect adjustment.** Where SDs were reconstructed from confidence intervals, p-values or standard errors, the reconstruction assumes normality and correct reporting of the source statistic. Cluster-randomised sample sizes were deflated by the design effect 1 + (average cluster size − 1) × ICC, which depends on an assumed or reported ICC; an inaccurate ICC would mis-state that trial's weight.
7. **Standardization across instruments.** The SMD scale allows pooling across different knowledge questionnaires but assumes that a one-SD shift means the same thing on each instrument. Instruments differ in item count, difficulty and content coverage (e.g. whether overdiagnosis is assessed numerically), so this assumption is only approximate.
8. **Short-term outcome only.** Later follow-up assessments were excluded by protocol, so nothing can be said about retention of knowledge gains.
9. **Risk of bias and certainty of evidence.** Blinding of participants to an informational intervention is generally impossible, and knowledge is self-reported via questionnaire; combined with imprecision and the very small k, the overall certainty of evidence for this outcome should be regarded as low.

## 11. Conclusion

Pooling two randomised trials of 684 screening-eligible women, communication-only interventions about mammography screening — decision aids, leaflets or consultations presenting benefits and harms without a values-clarification exercise — produced a small, statistically significant improvement in continuous knowledge scores relative to usual information, a standard leaflet, or no intervention (pooled Standardized Mean Difference 0.228, 95% CI 0.035 to 0.422, p = 0.020, favouring the communication-only intervention; I² = 39.18%, τ² = 0.007603, Q = 1.644, df = 1, p = 0.1997). The effect measure was pooled natively on the requested SMD scale. The direction of effect is consistent across both trials, but the magnitude is modest, the lower confidence bound is close to the null, heterogeneity cannot be meaningfully quantified at k = 2, and publication bias cannot be assessed. The finding is best described as tentative evidence that information provision alone can improve women's knowledge about screening in the short term. Larger trials reporting full continuous knowledge data, with pre-specified sub-scales for overdiagnosis and false-positive risk and with follow-up beyond one month, are needed before the size and durability of this benefit can be stated with confidence.
