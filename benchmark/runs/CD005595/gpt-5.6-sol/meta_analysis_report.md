# Removable Versus Non-removable Ankle Support After Surgical Management of Ankle Fracture: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis of End-of-Treatment Function

## Abstract

### Background
Removable ankle supports may facilitate earlier movement after operative treatment of ankle fractures, but their effect on activity limitation and ankle-related function at the end of the assigned treatment period is uncertain.

### Objective
To evaluate end-of-treatment activity/function among patients receiving a removable ankle support compared with a non-removable support after surgical management of an ankle fracture.

### Methods
Continuous activity/function outcomes measured with the Olerud-Molander Ankle Score, Mazur score, or comparable instruments were harmonized so that higher values represented better function and pooled as Standardized Mean Differences (SMDs). A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were assessed using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill, while recognizing the severe limitations of these methods with only three studies.

### Results
The supplied pipeline identified seven studies, but only three study-level effect sizes, comprising 185 participants, were available for quantitative synthesis. Individual SMDs were 0.618907 (95% CI 0.078 to 1.160; n = 55), 0.340101 (95% CI −0.381 to 1.061; n = 30), and −0.146910 (95% CI −0.539 to 0.246; n = 100). The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was 0.23277293202663307 (95% CI −0.262 to 0.728; p = 0.35672227224258074), favoring removable ankle support, but the confidence interval included the null. Heterogeneity was moderate to substantial (I² = 60.61%; τ² = 0.11495; Q = 5.349, p = 0.0689). Egger’s intercept was −0.629 (p = 0.4029), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0), leaving the adjusted estimate unchanged at 0.2328.

### Conclusions
The available evidence does not establish a statistically significant end-of-treatment functional advantage of removable over non-removable ankle support after ankle fracture surgery. The point estimate favors removable support, but it is imprecise, heterogeneous, and based on only three studies. The apparent low risk of publication bias should be interpreted cautiously because bias-detection methods have very low power at this evidence volume.

## Introduction

Ankle fractures treated surgically are commonly immobilized during early recovery to protect fixation and support soft-tissue healing. Non-removable devices such as casts provide continuous immobilization, whereas removable supports may permit wound inspection, hygiene, and controlled ankle movement. These practical differences could influence stiffness, activity limitation, and ankle-related function by the end of the prescribed support period. Conversely, greater mobility or inconsistent use of a removable device could potentially diminish protection during healing.

This review addressed the following question: among patients undergoing surgical management of an ankle fracture, what is the effect of a removable ankle support, compared with a non-removable ankle support such as a cast, on activity limitation or functional activity at the end of treatment?

The prespecified effect direction was that a positive effect represents better activity/function—equivalently, less activity limitation—with removable ankle support. Because eligible studies could use different continuous instruments, including the Olerud-Molander Ankle Score (OMAS), Mazur score, or comparable measures, outcomes were harmonized so that higher scores indicated better function and were synthesized using the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD).

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) combined the three studies. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was **0.23277293202663307** (95% CI **−0.262 to 0.728**; p = **0.35672227224258074**; k = 3; total n = 185). As designated in the supplied analysis, this result favors **removable ankle support after surgical fracture management**. However, the 95% confidence interval includes zero and extends from a possible disadvantage to a possible moderate benefit; the null hypothesis was therefore not rejected.

Because the outcome was standardized across instruments, the pooled estimate is expressed in standard-deviation units rather than points on the OMAS, Mazur, or another native scale. The analysis used the requested native pooling approach for this cross-instrument question (`native_pooling_used = true`); it is not an unconfirmed fallback measure.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was moderate to substantial:

- **Cochran’s Q = 5.349**, with p = **0.0689**;
- **I² = 60.61%**; and
- **τ² = 0.11495** on the SMD scale.

The non-significant Q test does not establish homogeneity because Q has low power when only three studies are available. The I² estimate indicates that a meaningful proportion of observed variability may reflect between-study differences rather than sampling error, although I² itself is imprecisely estimated with such a small number of studies. The τ² estimate likewise indicates non-zero dispersion in underlying effects.

The observed inconsistency is evident in the range from 0.618907 in `study_1_R1` to −0.146910 in `study_5_R5`. Plausible sources could include differences in fracture severity, surgical management, rehabilitation, support type, treatment duration, adherence, outcome instrument, and timing of assessment, but the supplied study-level characteristics do not permit these explanations to be tested. ## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−0.629** with p = **0.4029**, providing no statistically significant evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies, and its adjusted pooled estimate was **0.2328**, unchanged from the rounded unadjusted estimate. The supplied pipeline classified publication-bias risk as low.

This classification requires strong qualification. With k = 3, Egger’s test has very low statistical power, funnel-plot geometry is not reliably interpretable, and trim-and-fill may fail to identify missing studies. The absence of a detected signal is therefore not evidence that reporting or publication bias is absent. In addition, four of the seven studies in the pipeline did not contribute to the meta-analysis for unspecified reasons, raising a separate concern about availability of outcome data that cannot be resolved from the supplied information.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across three studies and 185 participants, removable ankle support produced a pooled SMD of 0.23277293202663307 in end-of-treatment activity/function relative to non-removable support. The analysis designates this point estimate as favoring removable support, but the result was not statistically significant (p = 0.35672227224258074), and the confidence interval ranged from −0.262 to 0.728. Thus, the evidence is compatible both with little or no difference and with effects in either direction.

The studies did not yield a uniform pattern. The smallest positive estimate was uncertain, one study found a positive estimate whose confidence interval excluded zero, and the largest study had a small negative estimate with an interval crossing zero. This dispersion led to I² = 60.61% and τ² = 0.11495, supporting the use of a random-effects analysis and cautioning against treating the pooled estimate as a single universally applicable treatment effect.

### Clinical interpretation

An SMD permits pooling across different functional instruments but does not directly indicate how many points patients would gain on the OMAS, Mazur score, or another clinical scale. Translating the pooled estimate into a native score or a minimally important clinical difference would require representative scale standard deviations and an established threshold, neither of which was supplied. Accordingly, the clinical importance of the point estimate cannot be determined from these results alone.

The estimate applies specifically to activity/function at the end of the assigned treatment period. It should not be extrapolated to earlier recovery, long-term function, pain, quality of life, return to work, adverse events, wound complications, fixation failure, or reoperation. A support strategy could conceivably have different effects at different recovery stages or across different benefit–harm outcomes.

### Certainty and applicability

A formal GRADE assessment could not be completed because study design details and risk-of-bias judgments were unavailable. Nevertheless, confidence in the pooled finding is constrained by the small evidence base, the wide confidence interval, moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity, missing descriptive information, and the inability to determine why four pipeline studies did not contribute. Applicability to particular fracture patterns, surgical techniques, support protocols, and patient populations remains uncertain.

### Implications for research

Future trials should clearly define the removable and non-removable devices, standardize or fully describe weight-bearing and exercise protocols, report adherence and crossover, and assess function at prespecified short- and long-term time points. Investigators should report group sizes, means, and standard deviations for validated instruments, together with complications and patient-important outcomes. Prospective registration, complete outcome reporting, and enough trials to support credible subgroup analysis would improve the evidence base.

## Limitations

This meta-analysis has several important limitations. First, only three studies were pooled, limiting precision, heterogeneity estimation, and bias detection. Second, the pooled confidence interval was wide and included both possible harm and possible benefit. Third, observed heterogeneity was substantial enough to question whether one summary effect adequately represents all clinical settings.

Fourth, standardization improves comparability across instruments but reduces direct clinical interpretability and assumes the included scales measure sufficiently comparable constructs. Fifth, no information was available about allocation methods, masking, missing data, selective reporting, adherence, or other risk-of-bias domains. Sixth, the absence of search details and exclusion reasons prevents independent assessment of review completeness and eligibility decisions. Seventh, publication-bias analyses based on three studies are not dependable despite their non-significant results. Finally, because the analysis concerns only the end of the assigned treatment period, it does not determine whether either strategy improves the trajectory of recovery or longer-term outcomes.

## Conclusion

For patients undergoing surgical management of an ankle fracture, the pooled Standardized Mean Difference in end-of-treatment activity/function was 0.23277293202663307 (95% CI −0.262 to 0.728; p = 0.35672227224258074), favoring removable ankle support according to the supplied analysis. This does not provide statistically conclusive evidence that removable support improves function relative to a non-removable support. The estimate is imprecise and accompanied by moderate-to-substantial heterogeneity (I² = 60.61%). Larger, rigorously reported trials and complete systematic-review documentation are required before a definitive functional advantage can be claimed.
