# Removable versus Non-Removable Ankle Support After Surgical Fixation of Ankle and Lower Limb Fractures: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Activity Limitation and Ankle Function at the End of the Immobilisation Period

## Abstract

**Background.** After internal fixation of ankle (or other lower limb) fractures, the postoperative immobilisation device may be non-removable (plaster or synthetic cast) or removable (functional brace, orthosis, walker boot). Removable devices permit early range-of-motion exercise, hygiene and wound inspection, but may offer less protection against premature loading. Whether they improve activity limitation and ankle function by the end of the immobilisation period remains uncertain.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of postoperative immobilisation with a removable ankle support compared with a non-removable support on activity limitation / ankle function at the end of the treatment (immobilisation) period in adults treated surgically for ankle or lower limb fractures.

**Methods.** A systematic search of controlled trials comparing removable with non-removable postoperative immobilisation after surgical fracture fixation was conducted. Eligible outcomes were patient-reported or clinician-rated activity/function instruments (Olerud–Molander Ankle Score, Mazur/AOFAS score, Foot and Ankle Outcome Score, Foot and Ankle Ability Measure, Baird–Jackson score or similar), all oriented so that higher scores indicate better function before pooling. Effect sizes were expressed as standardized mean differences (Hedges' g correction applied to individual study estimates) and pooled in a random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Seven studies met the inclusion criteria; six contributed extractable data for the end-of-treatment function outcome (k = 6; N = 804 participants). Individual standardized effects ranged from g = −0.147 (95% CI −0.539 to 0.246) to g = 0.619 (95% CI 0.078 to 1.160). The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 0.224 (95% CI −0.001 to 0.449; p = 0.0515), a point estimate that favours the removable ankle support group but whose confidence interval includes no difference. Heterogeneity was moderate and not statistically significant (Q = 7.741, df = 5, p = 0.1711; I² = 41.26%; τ² = 0.030456). Egger's regression showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept = −0.0555, p = 0.2676) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted SMD unchanged at 0.2238), consistent with a low risk of small-study/publication bias.

**Conclusions.** In adults undergoing surgical management of ankle or lower limb fractures, removable postoperative immobilisation was associated with a small advantage in activity limitation / ankle function at the end of the treatment period relative to non-removable casting, but the effect did not reach conventional statistical significance and the pooled confidence interval is compatible with no difference. The evidence base is small (six pooled comparisons, dominated by one large trial), and the finding is best read as showing that removable supports are at least non-inferior to casts on function at end of immobilisation, with a plausible but unconfirmed small benefit.


## 1. Introduction

Surgical fixation of ankle fractures is one of the most frequently performed procedures in orthopaedic trauma. Once fixation has been achieved, the limb is conventionally protected for a period of weeks while soft tissues heal and bone union progresses. The classical approach is a non-removable below-knee plaster or synthetic cast, which maximises protection and guarantees adherence, but which also immobilises the tibiotalar and subtalar joints completely, prevents wound inspection, and is associated with muscle atrophy, joint stiffness and skin problems. The alternative is a removable support — a functional brace, orthosis, air-stirrup or walker boot — which can be taken off for hygiene, wound care and, in many protocols, for supervised or self-directed range-of-motion exercise.

The clinical trade-off is explicit. Removable devices may accelerate the recovery of ankle motion and functional capacity and may therefore reduce activity limitation at the point at which immobilisation ends. Conversely, they transfer responsibility for protection to the patient, and concerns persist about premature weight-bearing, loss of reduction and wound complications. Because the two strategies differ in mechanism rather than in surgical technique, the most informative comparison is the patient's functional state at the moment the protective device is discontinued — the end of the treatment period — before later convergence of recovery trajectories masks any difference.

Published trials in this field are individually small, use a variety of function instruments (Olerud–Molander Ankle Score, Mazur/AOFAS ankle score, Foot and Ankle Outcome Score, Foot and Ankle Ability Measure, Baird–Jackson score), and report mixed results. A quantitative synthesis on a common standardized scale is therefore required to establish whether the direction and magnitude of effect are consistent.

**Research question.** In adults undergoing surgical management of ankle (or lower limb) fractures, what is the effect of postoperative immobilisation with a removable ankle support compared with a non-removable support on activity limitation / ankle function at the end of the treatment period?

**Effect direction convention.** All instruments were oriented so that higher scores denote better function before pooling. A positive standardized effect therefore indicates that the removable ankle support group had better activity/function (less activity limitation) at the end of treatment than the non-removable (cast) group.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

This review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. The review question was framed using the PICO structure set out below.

| PICO element | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Adults undergoing surgical management (internal or external fixation) of ankle or other lower limb fractures |
| **Intervention** | Removable ankle support after surgical fixation — removable brace, orthosis, walker boot, functional removable immobilisation |
| **Comparator** | Non-removable ankle support after surgical fixation — plaster or synthetic cast, or other non-removable immobilisation |
| **Outcome** | Activity limitation / ankle function at the end of the treatment (immobilisation) period, measured with patient-reported or clinician-rated ankle function instruments (OMAS, Mazur/AOFAS, FAOS, FAAM, Baird–Jackson or similar) |
| **Study design** | Controlled trials with a concurrent comparison group and extractable continuous outcome data |

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they (i) enrolled adults treated surgically for an ankle or lower limb fracture; (ii) randomised or otherwise allocated participants to a removable versus a non-removable postoperative immobilisation device; (iii) reported a patient-reported or clinician-rated activity/function score assessed at the end of the immobilisation period; and (iv) provided sufficient data (means with dispersion measures and group sizes, or statistics convertible to them) to compute a standardized effect size. Narrative reviews, previous systematic reviews, case series without a comparator, and duplicate reports of the same trial cohort were not eligible as primary data sources.

### 2.3 Outcome definition and harmonisation

The target outcome was activity limitation / ankle function at the end of the treatment (immobilisation) period. Because trials used heterogeneous instruments, each instrument was first checked for scoring direction and, where necessary, reversed so that **higher = better function**. Harmonised scores were then converted to a common standardized metric prior to pooling.

### 2.4 Effect size computation and pooling

For each comparison, the standardized mean difference was computed as the difference in mean function score between the removable-support and non-removable-support arms divided by the pooled within-group standard deviation, with the Hedges' g small-sample correction applied to each study-level estimate. The pre-specified summary measure for this synthesis was the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** (effect measure source: explicit review-level specification). Native-scale pooling on the SMD metric was used — that is, the pooled statistic reported below is a confirmed SMD computed on the intended scale and is **not** a fallback conversion from a different effect metric; no effect-measure conflict was detected.

Estimates were combined using a random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), which does not assume a single common true effect and incorporates between-study variance into the standard error of the summary estimate.

### 2.5 Heterogeneity and small-study effects

Between-study heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q (with its p-value), the I² statistic (proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error) and τ² (absolute between-study variance on the SMD scale). Small-study effects and potential publication bias were examined with Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and with the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

## 3. Search and Screening

The systematic search targeted controlled trials of postoperative immobilisation strategy after surgical fixation of ankle and lower limb fractures. Records retrieved were screened at title/abstract level against the eligibility criteria, and potentially relevant reports were assessed in full text for the presence of (a) a removable-versus-non-removable comparison and (b) an extractable end-of-treatment activity/function outcome.

**Screening outcome.** Seven studies satisfied all inclusion criteria and were carried forward into the review. Of these, **six contributed a computable effect size** for activity limitation / ankle function at the end of the treatment period and were included in the quantitative synthesis (k = 6; total N = 804 randomised/analysed participants). One included study (study_4) did not yield an extractable effect estimate for this outcome at this timepoint — for example because function was not reported in a form permitting standardized effect computation at the end of immobilisation — and therefore contributes to the narrative synthesis only. No data were imputed for this study, and it is excluded from every pooled number reported below.

Because all six pooled comparisons address the same construct (activity limitation / ankle function) at the same conceptual timepoint (end of the immobilisation period), the synthesis comprises a **single meta-analytic group**; no separate outcome/timepoint subsets required independent pooling, and no analysis group was flagged as having insufficient data.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Individual study effects, with the orientation "higher = better function", range from g = −0.147 to g = 0.619, i.e. from a small advantage for the non-removable cast to a moderate advantage for the removable support. The spread is modest in absolute terms: the full range of point estimates spans approximately 0.77 standardized units, and the interquartile region of the estimates lies between roughly 0.12 and 0.47.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

### 6.1 Primary pooled estimate

A single random-effects meta-analysis (REML) was conducted on the six available comparisons.

**Table 2. Random-effects pooled result — activity limitation / ankle function at end of the treatment period.**

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Number of studies pooled (k) | 6 |
| Total participants (N) | 804 |
| Effect measure | Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) |
| Native-scale pooling confirmed | Yes |
| Pooled SMD | **0.2238** |
| 95% confidence interval | **−0.001 to 0.449** |
| p-value | **0.0515** |
| Model | Random effects, REML |
| τ² | 0.030456 |
| I² | 41.26% |
| Cochran's Q (df = 5) | 7.741 (p = 0.1711) |
| Favours | Removable ankle support (removable brace, orthosis, walker boot, functional removable immobilisation) after surgical fracture fixation |

The pooled **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 0.224 (95% CI −0.001 to 0.449; p = 0.0515)**. Per the analysis output, the direction of this estimate **favours the removable ankle support** arm — that is, participants immobilised in a removable brace, orthosis or walker boot had, on average, modestly better activity/function scores at the end of the treatment period than those immobilised in a non-removable cast.

Two qualifications must be stated with equal prominence. First, the 95% confidence interval includes zero (its lower bound is −0.001) and the p-value of 0.0515 does not cross the conventional 0.05 threshold; the result is therefore **statistically borderline and not significant** at the 5% level. Second, the magnitude is small by conventional benchmarks (Cohen: 0.2 = small, 0.5 = moderate, 0.8 = large). The data are compatible with anything from a negligible disadvantage for removable supports to a moderate advantage of roughly half a standard deviation.

Expressed less abstractly, an SMD of 0.224 corresponds to approximately a fifth of a within-group standard deviation. On an instrument such as the Olerud–Molander Ankle Score, where between-patient standard deviations in post-fixation cohorts are commonly in the region of 15–20 points, this translates to a difference of roughly 3–4.5 points — a difference at or below most estimates of the minimal clinically important difference for that instrument. The honest summary is therefore that removable supports are, at worst, equivalent to casts for end-of-immobilisation function, and may confer a small benefit that this evidence base is not large enough to confirm.

### 6.2 Cumulative analysis

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows how the running summary estimate evolves as studies are added. The characteristic pattern is an initially larger positive estimate driven by the early small trials, which is progressively attenuated and stabilised toward the final value of 0.224 as the large study_3_R3 and the null-to-negative study_7_R7 enter the pool, with the cumulative confidence interval narrowing but continuing to abut the null. This stabilisation without reversal of sign indicates that the small positive direction is not an artefact of a single study, but also that accumulating evidence has moved the estimate toward, rather than away from, the null.

### 6.3 Prediction interval

With τ² = 0.030456 (τ ≈ 0.175) and only six studies, the approximate 95% prediction interval for the true effect in a new, exchangeable study spans roughly −0.36 to 0.80. This interval is wide and straddles zero, meaning that in a future trial of this comparison a small advantage for the cast cannot be excluded, even though the central tendency favours the removable support. Prediction intervals based on k = 6 are themselves imprecise and should be treated as indicative only.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **moderate but not statistically significant**:

- **Cochran's Q = 7.741** on 5 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.1711** — the null hypothesis of homogeneity is not rejected.
- **I² = 41.26%** — approximately two-fifths of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to genuine between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. By the conventional Cochrane thresholds this falls in the "may represent moderate heterogeneity" band (30–60%).
- **τ² = 0.030456** — the estimated between-study variance on the SMD scale, corresponding to a between-study standard deviation of approximately 0.175 standardized units.

These three statistics are mutually consistent and are visualised in the heterogeneity summary panel (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`). The absolute dispersion is small: a between-study SD of 0.175 means that plausible true effects in individual trials cluster within roughly ±0.35 of the mean, which keeps nearly all the mass of the distribution in the "negligible to moderate benefit for removable support" region.

It is important not to over-read the non-significant Q test. With k = 6, the Q statistic has low power to detect real heterogeneity, so p = 0.1711 should not be interpreted as positive evidence that the trials estimate an identical effect. Equally, I² is estimated imprecisely at this k and its confidence interval would be wide, plausibly extending from 0% to well above 70%. The practical conclusion is that the trials are reasonably but not perfectly consistent, and that the random-effects model (which incorporates τ² into the summary standard error) is the appropriate specification — as used here.

The most obvious candidate explanation for the dispersion present is the single discordant trial, study_7_R7 (g = −0.147, n = 100), which is the only comparison favouring the cast. Its confidence interval (−0.539 to 0.246) nonetheless overlaps substantially with those of the positive studies, so it is a source of dispersion rather than a frank outlier.

## 8. Moderators

No formal moderator analysis (subgroup analysis or meta-regression) was undertaken as a confirmatory component of this synthesis, and none should be: with k = 6 studies and a single pooled outcome group, any meta-regression would have approximately one degree of freedom per covariate against six data points, yielding estimates too unstable to interpret and a high probability of spurious findings. Cochrane guidance recommends at least ten studies per covariate before meta-regression is attempted; this requirement is not met.

Exploratory, descriptive observations are nevertheless worth recording as hypothesis-generating material only:

1. **Study size / precision.** The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) shows an inverse relationship between study size and effect magnitude: the single largest trial (study_3_R3, n = 497) produced the smallest positive estimate (g = 0.112), whereas the smaller trials produced larger positive estimates (g = 0.316 to 0.619). This pattern is typical of small-study effects — which may reflect publication bias, differences in methodological rigour, or genuinely different populations and protocols in smaller single-centre trials. Formal testing (Section 9) did not find statistically significant asymmetry, but the visual gradient is a reason for caution about the upper half of the confidence interval.
2. **Instrument heterogeneity.** The pooled estimate combines several different function instruments (OMAS, Mazur/AOFAS, FAOS, FAAM, Baird–Jackson and similar scales). Standardization places them on a common metric but does not make them interchangeable: instruments differ in their responsiveness, ceiling behaviour and the weight they give to activity limitation as opposed to pain or range of motion. Differential responsiveness is a plausible contributor to the observed τ².
3. **Definition of "end of treatment".** The duration of immobilisation, and therefore the timing of the end-of-treatment assessment, is likely to vary between trials (typically in the region of 4–8 weeks). If removable supports confer an early advantage that narrows over time, trials with longer immobilisation would be expected to report smaller effects. This could not be tested with the available data.
4. **Protocol intensity.** Removable supports are not a single intervention. Some protocols permit only device removal for hygiene; others pair the removable device with a structured early range-of-motion or weight-bearing programme. The latter would be expected to produce larger functional gains. The available data do not permit this distinction to be examined quantitatively.

None of these observations should be treated as established moderators. They are recorded to guide the design of future trials and of a future, larger update of this review.

## 9. Publication Bias

Small-study effects and potential publication bias were assessed by two complementary methods.

**Table 3. Small-study effect diagnostics.**

| Test | Statistic | Value |
|---|---|---|
| Egger's regression | Intercept | −0.0555 |
| Egger's regression | p-value | 0.2676 |
| Trim-and-fill | Studies imputed (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill | Adjusted pooled SMD | 0.2238 (unchanged) |
| Overall risk judgement | — | **Low** |

**Interpretation.** Egger's regression test showed no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.2676), meaning there is no detectable relationship between effect size and study precision that would signal small-study effects. The intercept itself (−0.0555) is close to zero and is, if anything, in the direction opposite to the usual publication-bias signature. Consistent with this, the trim-and-fill procedure imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the pooled estimate required no adjustment and remains unchanged at 0.2238. The overall small-study/publication-bias risk was therefore judged **low**.

**Caveat.** Together these support a low risk-of-bias judgement, though a non-significant result is not positive evidence of no bias — these tests are underpowered when the number of studies is small (typically fewer than 10) or heterogeneity is high, so publication bias cannot be definitively excluded. With k = 6 and I² = 41.26%, both conditions for low power are partially met. Moreover, the descriptive size–effect gradient noted in Section 8 means that the formal null result should be read as "asymmetry not demonstrated" rather than "asymmetry absent". Grey literature, trials reported only in non-indexed sources, and selectively reported timepoints within otherwise published trials remain possible sources of unmeasured bias.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 Principal findings

Across six controlled comparisons comprising 804 adults treated surgically for ankle or lower limb fractures, postoperative immobilisation with a removable ankle support was associated with a pooled Standardized Mean Difference of 0.224 (95% CI −0.001 to 0.449; p = 0.0515) in activity limitation / ankle function at the end of the immobilisation period, a direction that favours the removable support. The effect is small in magnitude, statistically borderline, and accompanied by moderate, non-significant heterogeneity (I² = 41.26%; τ² = 0.030456; Q = 7.741, p = 0.1711). Small-study effects were not detected (Egger's p = 0.2676; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0).

The most defensible reading of these results is twofold. First, there is **no evidence that removable supports compromise functional recovery** at the end of immobilisation relative to casting; on the contrary, five of six point estimates lie on the removable-support side. Second, the **magnitude of any advantage is small and unconfirmed**: the lower confidence bound of −0.001 means the data remain compatible with exact equivalence, and the upper bound of 0.449 means they are also compatible with a clinically worthwhile moderate benefit. A study of this question is best described as having demonstrated non-inferiority with a suggestion of superiority, rather than superiority.

### 10.2 Mechanistic plausibility

A small early functional advantage for removable devices is biologically coherent. Permitting intermittent device removal allows early active and passive ankle dorsiflexion and plantarflexion, which mitigates the capsular contracture, articular cartilage deconditioning and calf muscle atrophy that follow rigid immobilisation. Since the end-of-treatment assessment occurs at precisely the moment when the cast group has had least opportunity for such motion, this timepoint should be where the difference is largest. That the observed difference is nonetheless small suggests either that the functional deficit from rigid immobilisation is quickly recoverable, or that in practice many "removable support" protocols involve relatively limited mobilisation.

The discordant trial (study_7_R7) is a reminder of the countervailing mechanism: if a removable device is removed too often, worn inconsistently, or permits premature loading, protection is lost and function can be worse. Adherence is therefore a plausible effect modifier that trials rarely measure and that this review could not evaluate.

### 10.3 Comparison with the wider literature

The finding that removable functional supports are broadly comparable to, and possibly slightly better than, casting for post-fixation ankle fractures is consistent with the broader trend in fracture rehabilitation toward earlier, more functional aftercare. Previous syntheses in this area have generally reported small short-term advantages for early mobilisation strategies on function and range of motion, frequently at the cost of a higher rate of minor wound complications. The present analysis addresses only the function/activity-limitation outcome at the end of immobilisation; it does not quantify wound complications, loss of reduction, reoperation, venous thromboembolism or return to work, all of which are essential to any clinical recommendation and none of which can be inferred from the present estimate.

### 10.4 Clinical implications

For the clinician choosing a postoperative immobilisation strategy after ankle fracture fixation, these data support the following position: a removable brace, orthosis or walker boot is a reasonable default for patients in whom adherence and protection can be expected, because functional outcomes at the end of immobilisation are at least as good as with a cast and may be slightly better, while removable devices additionally permit wound inspection and hygiene. Conversely, the evidence does not justify asserting that removable supports produce meaningfully better function, and it provides no basis for abandoning casting in patients with concerns about adherence, cognitive impairment, unstable fixation, or complex soft-tissue injury. Decision-making should continue to weigh complication profiles, cost, device availability and patient preference alongside this small functional signal.

### 10.5 Research implications

The dominant need is for adequately powered trials. To detect an SMD of 0.22 with 80% power at α = 0.05 requires approximately 650 participants in total — larger than all but one of the included trials. Future trials should (i) pre-specify a single primary function instrument with established responsiveness in this population, (ii) report the end-of-immobilisation assessment as an explicit, protocol-defined timepoint, (iii) measure and report device-wearing adherence, and (iv) report function jointly with wound complications and loss of reduction so that the benefit–harm trade-off can be assessed on a common cohort. Individual-participant-data synthesis would additionally permit the moderator questions raised in Section 8 to be addressed properly.

## 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were raised for this synthesis. The following data-handling facts are nevertheless reported plainly for transparency:

- **Seven studies were included in the review; six contributed effect sizes.** One included study (study_4) did not yield an extractable effect estimate for activity limitation / ankle function at the end of the treatment period and is therefore absent from every pooled statistic. No values were imputed on its behalf, and the pooled result should be understood as resting on six, not seven, comparisons.
- **Single outcome group.** All six pooled comparisons address the same construct at the same conceptual timepoint, so a single meta-analysis was appropriate. No analysis group was flagged as having insufficient data for pooling, and no results were merged across heterogeneous outcomes or timepoints.
- **Effect measure.** The summary statistic is the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD), specified explicitly at review level. Native-scale pooling was confirmed (`native_pooling_used = true`), meaning the reported pooled value is a genuine SMD computed on the intended metric rather than a standardized fallback conversion. No effect-measure conflict was detected across the contributing studies.
- **Instrument harmonisation.** Multiple different function instruments were combined after reorientation so that higher scores indicate better function. This is methodologically necessary for pooling but introduces construct-level heterogeneity that the SMD metric conceals rather than eliminates.
- **Weight concentration.** One trial (study_3_R3) contributes 497 of 804 participants (61.8%) and exerts disproportionate influence on the summary estimate. Readers should interpret the pooled value as being anchored substantially by that single trial.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Small number of studies.** With k = 6, the pooled estimate, τ², I², Egger's test and the prediction interval are all imprecisely estimated. The non-significant Q test and Egger test are underpowered and cannot establish homogeneity or absence of bias.
2. **Borderline statistical result.** The pooled SMD of 0.224 has a 95% CI of −0.001 to 0.449 and p = 0.0515. The result does not meet conventional significance, and framing it as a positive finding would misrepresent the evidence.
3. **Dominance of one trial.** Study_3_R3 provides nearly two-thirds of the participants and the most precise estimate (g = 0.112). The summary estimate is sensitive to this single trial; removal of it would shift the pooled value upward but widen the interval substantially.
4. **Instrument heterogeneity.** Pooling across OMAS, Mazur/AOFAS, FAOS, FAAM, Baird–Jackson and similar scales assumes comparability of standardized units across instruments with different responsiveness, ceiling effects and content coverage.
5. **Variable definition of "end of treatment".** Immobilisation duration differs between protocols, so the assessment timepoint is conceptually rather than temporally harmonised. Differences in timing may contribute to the observed heterogeneity and were not adjustable.
6. **Intervention heterogeneity.** "Removable support" encompasses braces, orthoses and walker boots used under widely differing mobilisation and weight-bearing instructions; "non-removable support" encompasses plaster and synthetic casts with differing durations. The comparison is therefore between two families of strategies rather than between two standardised treatments.
7. **Unmeasured adherence.** Removable devices depend on patient adherence for their protective effect, and adherence was not quantifiable from the available reports. Effectiveness in trials may differ from effectiveness in routine care in either direction.
8. **Risk of bias within studies.** Blinding of participants and treating clinicians is impossible for this comparison, and blinding of outcome assessors is often infeasible for patient-reported scores. Performance and detection bias cannot be excluded, and could plausibly inflate a patient-reported advantage for the more comfortable, more "modern" device.
9. **Single outcome domain.** This review addresses activity limitation/function only. Wound complications, loss of reduction, reoperation, infection, thromboembolism, pain, cost and longer-term function were not synthesised, so no net benefit–harm conclusion can be drawn.
10. **One study not pooled.** The exclusion of study_4 from the quantitative synthesis means the pooled estimate does not use the full body of included evidence, and the direction of its unreported effect is unknown.

## 13. Conclusion

In adults undergoing surgical management of ankle or lower limb fractures, postoperative immobilisation with a **removable ankle support** (brace, orthosis or walker boot) yielded a pooled **Standardized Mean Difference of 0.224 (95% CI −0.001 to 0.449; p = 0.0515; k = 6; N = 804)** for activity limitation / ankle function at the end of the treatment period, a direction that **favours the removable ankle support** over non-removable casting. Heterogeneity was moderate and not statistically significant (I² = 41.26%; τ² = 0.030456; Q = 7.741, df = 5, p = 0.1711), and there was no detectable small-study or publication bias (Egger's p = 0.2676; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted SMD unchanged at 0.2238; risk judged low).

Because the confidence interval includes no difference and the p-value falls marginally above 0.05, this evidence establishes that removable supports are **not inferior** to casts for end-of-immobilisation function, and is **suggestive but not confirmatory** of a small functional advantage. The magnitude of the point estimate (approximately one-fifth of a standard deviation, or roughly 3–4.5 OMAS points) is at or below usual thresholds of clinical importance. Removable supports can therefore be regarded as a legitimate and probably slightly advantageous option for postoperative immobilisation in appropriately selected patients, but the choice should continue to be made on the combined basis of complication risk, adherence, soft-tissue status, cost and patient preference rather than on the strength of this functional signal alone. Adequately powered trials with harmonised instruments, pre-specified end-of-immobilisation timepoints, measured adherence and joint reporting of harms are needed to resolve the question.
