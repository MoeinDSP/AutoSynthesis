# Lay Health Worker Delivery versus Facility-Based Professional Delivery of Acute Malnutrition Treatment in Children Aged 6–59 Months: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Recovery/Cure Rates

## Abstract

**Background.** Treatment of child wasting (severe and moderate acute malnutrition, SAM/MAM) has historically been delivered by health professionals at health facilities. Task-shifting the full treatment cycle — screening, admission, ready-to-use therapeutic/supplementary food distribution and follow-up — to lay health workers (community health workers, volunteers and other non-professional providers) has been proposed as a way to extend geographic reach, but it remains contested whether treatment outcomes are preserved when delivery is simplified in this way.

**Objective.** To determine whether treatment of wasting in children aged 6–59 months delivered by lay health workers achieves a higher proportion of children recovered/cured by end of treatment than treatment delivered by health professionals at health facilities (standard care).

**Methods.** We systematically identified controlled comparisons (individually randomised and cluster-randomised trials and controlled programme evaluations) in which acute malnutrition treatment delivered by lay health workers was compared with facility-based professional delivery, with recovery/cure (each trial's own definition: attainment of a weight-for-height z-score or mid-upper-arm circumference threshold, or discharge as cured) as the outcome. Programme-wide cure rates were preferred to subgroup rates and intention-to-treat denominators were used where reported; already cluster-adjusted risk ratios with confidence intervals were used as reported. Defaulting, death, relapse, weight gain, length of stay and coverage were not analysed. Effect sizes were pooled by outcome group using random-effects models with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. The pre-specified effect measure was the risk ratio (RR); native-scale pooling was used. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Five studies contributing five effect estimates (total N = 5,324 children in the pooled analysis) met inclusion criteria. Four studies reported recovery on a risk-ratio-compatible scale and were pooled: the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was **1.071 (95% CI 1.037 to 1.106; p = 2.94 × 10⁻⁵; k = 4; N = 5,324)**, estimated by REML on the native RR scale. Between-study heterogeneity was low to moderate and not statistically significant (Q = 5.956, df = 3, p = 0.1138; I² = 24.56%; τ² = 0.000265). Despite the confidence interval excluding the null, the direction-classification field for this analysis returned **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**, and we therefore do not claim a favoured arm on the basis of this pooled estimate. One further study reported recovery as a hazard ratio (time-to-recovery) and constituted a separate outcome group of k = 1; with a single study this group could not be pooled and no pooled value is reported for it (its standardized single effect was g = −0.018). Egger's test indicated significant funnel asymmetry (intercept 0.4501, p = 0.0016) and trim-and-fill imputed two missing studies, giving an adjusted standardized effect of g = 0.2094; overall publication-bias risk was rated **high**.

**Conclusions.** Across four comparisons the pooled risk ratio for recovery was 1.071 with a confidence interval marginally excluding 1, i.e. recovery proportions under lay-health-worker delivery were at most a few percentage points different from, and statistically compatible with near-equivalence to, facility-based professional delivery. The magnitude is small, the direction classification was indeterminate, the evidence base is very small (k = 4), and funnel asymmetry was significant. The evidence is therefore best read as consistent with non-inferiority of lay-health-worker delivery rather than as demonstrating superiority, and should not be used to assert a clinically meaningful advantage for either delivery model.

# Introduction

Child wasting — low weight-for-height or low mid-upper-arm circumference (MUAC), encompassing severe acute malnutrition (SAM) and moderate acute malnutrition (MAM) — affects an estimated 45 million children under five years of age worldwide and carries a substantially elevated risk of death. The community-based management of acute malnutrition (CMAM) model, scaled from the early 2000s, moved the bulk of treatment out of inpatient stabilisation centres into outpatient therapeutic programmes. However, in most national protocols the outpatient programme itself remains anchored at a health facility: admission decisions, anthropometric classification, appetite testing and weekly or fortnightly ration distribution are performed by nurses, clinical officers or other salaried health professionals.

For families living hours from the nearest facility, this design imposes substantial opportunity and transport costs, and contributes to the persistently low treatment coverage observed in many high-burden settings. A logical extension of CMAM is therefore to shift the treatment cycle itself to lay providers — community health workers (CHWs), community volunteers, *agents de santé communautaire*, *relais communautaires* and similar cadres — who can screen with MUAC tapes, admit uncomplicated cases, distribute ready-to-use therapeutic food (RUTF) or ready-to-use supplementary food, and follow children in or near their homes, referring only complicated cases.

The central safety question for this task-shifting agenda is whether treatment *quality* is preserved. Lay providers typically have weeks rather than years of training, may use simplified MUAC-only admission and discharge criteria, and operate with limited supervision. Conversely, they may achieve better adherence and earlier presentation because of proximity and trust. The outcome that most directly captures treatment quality is the proportion of admitted children who recover (are "cured") by the end of treatment, against Sphere and national programme performance standards.

This systematic review and meta-analysis asks a single, directional question: **does treatment of wasting in children aged 6–59 months delivered by lay health workers achieve a higher recovery/cure rate than treatment delivered by health professionals at health facilities?** A risk ratio greater than 1 (or a positive standardized effect) indicates a higher proportion of children recovered under lay-health-worker delivery.

# Search and Screening

## Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met all of the following:

- **Population:** children aged 6–59 months admitted for treatment of acute malnutrition (SAM, MAM, or both);
- **Intervention:** treatment (not screening or referral alone) delivered by lay health workers — CHWs, community volunteers, or other non-professional providers — in the community or at a community distribution point;
- **Comparator:** treatment delivered by health professionals at a health facility, i.e. standard outpatient therapeutic/supplementary feeding care;
- **Outcome:** the proportion of treated children recovered/cured at end of treatment, reported as events out of the number of children treated per arm, using each study's own recovery definition (weight-for-height z-score threshold, MUAC threshold, or discharge as cured), or as an already cluster-adjusted risk ratio with confidence interval;
- **Design:** individually randomised trial, cluster-randomised trial, or controlled (concurrent-comparison) programme evaluation.

Studies reporting only defaulting, mortality, relapse, rate of weight gain, length of stay or treatment coverage as outcomes were excluded, as pre-specified; these outcomes are not analysed anywhere in this report. Uncontrolled before–after evaluations and single-arm programme reports were excluded because they cannot support the contrast of interest.

## Information sources and selection

Records were identified from the bibliographic corpus assembled for this review, screened in two stages (title/abstract, then full text) against the criteria above. At full-text stage, documents were excluded if they were narrative or systematic reviews rather than primary studies, if they duplicated a study already included (multiple reports of the same trial were collapsed to a single record to prevent double-counting), or if the recovery outcome could not be extracted on a per-arm event/denominator basis or as an adjusted ratio with an interval estimate.

Where a single trial yielded more than one eligible recovery statistic, the programme-wide (whole-cohort) cure rate was preferred over disaggregated subgroup rates (e.g. SAM-only or MAM-only strata), and the intention-to-treat denominator was preferred over per-protocol.

**Five studies contributing five independent effect estimates** proceeded to quantitative synthesis. A PRISMA-style flow of records is implied by this count; no additional eligible comparisons were identified.

# Effect Sizes

The pre-specified effect measure for this review was the **Risk Ratio (RR)**, set by explicit user request, and native-scale RR pooling was used for the primary analysis (`native_pooling_used = true`). The per-study values displayed in Table 1 are standardized effects (Hedges' g) produced for cross-study display and for the small-study-effect diagnostics, which operate on a standardized scale; they are not the units of the primary pooled estimate and should not be read as risk ratios.

Inspection of the per-study estimates shows a consistent ordering by precision: the two smallest studies (N = 358 and N = 829) returned near-null effects with confidence intervals spanning zero (g = 0.086 and g = 0.037), the single hazard-ratio study returned a very slightly negative estimate (g = −0.019, CI −0.180 to 0.143), and the two largest studies returned the largest positive estimates with intervals excluding zero (g = 0.201, CI 0.072 to 0.331; g = 0.264, CI 0.180 to 0.347). This pattern — larger studies producing *larger* rather than smaller effects — is the inverse of the classic small-study-effect signature and is revisited in the Publication Bias section, where it manifests as significant funnel asymmetry in the opposite-to-usual direction.

# Meta-Analytic Result(s)

Because the included studies did not all report recovery on the same statistical scale, synthesis was stratified into two outcome groups, which are reported separately below rather than merged.

## Group 1 — Recovery from wasting, risk-ratio scale (k = 4)

- **Pooled Risk Ratio (RR): 1.071 (95% CI 1.037 to 1.106)**
- p = 2.94 × 10⁻⁵
- k = 4 studies; N = 5,324 children
- Model: random effects, REML; computation scale RR; native-scale pooling confirmed
- **Favours: neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)**

The pooled risk ratio of 1.071 corresponds, on its face, to approximately a 7% relative difference in the proportion of children classified as recovered, and the 95% confidence interval (1.037 to 1.106) lies marginally above 1.00 across its whole width, which is why the p-value is small. It is essential, however, to report the direction classification as returned by the analysis: the `favors` field for this result is **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**. We therefore do not assert that this pooled estimate favours lay health workers. Two features justify that caution. First, the absolute magnitude is small: against typical programme cure rates of 80–90%, a risk ratio of 1.071 is a difference of a few percentage points and sits within the band that most non-inferiority margins for CMAM programmes would treat as equivalence. Second, risk ratios for recovery are sensitive to which event is counted (recovery versus non-recovery) and to the definitional threshold each study applied, so a modest ratio above 1 does not translate mechanically into clinical superiority.

The forest plot for this analysis is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`, and the cumulative (precision-ordered) meta-analysis at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`. The cumulative plot shows the running estimate stabilising as the two large studies enter, with the earlier, smaller studies exerting little leverage on the final value.

## Group 2 — Recovery from wasting, hazard-ratio scale (k = 1)

One study (study_3_R3, N = 705) reported recovery as a **hazard ratio** for time to recovery rather than as a risk ratio for recovery by end of treatment. This group contains a single study and is flagged `insufficient_data`: **it cannot be pooled, and no pooled effect, confidence interval or heterogeneity statistic is reported for it.** Its standardized single effect was g = −0.0185 (95% CI −0.180 to 0.143), i.e. essentially null with an interval spanning zero in both directions. This study is excluded from the primary pooled estimate because combining a hazard ratio with end-of-treatment risk ratios would mix fundamentally different estimands (rate of recovery over time versus proportion recovered at a fixed endpoint).

Note that for this group the recorded effect measure remains the user-specified RR while the source paper explicitly reports a hazard ratio; this measure conflict is documented in the Data Quality & Validation Notes below, with the user's explicit request retained as authoritative.

# Heterogeneity

For the primary (RR) group:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 5.956 (df = 3) |
| p (Q) | 0.1138 |
| I² | 24.56% |
| τ² | 0.000265 |
| τ (approx.) | 0.016 (on the log-RR scale) |
| Estimator | REML |

Heterogeneity is **low to moderate and not statistically significant**. I² of 24.56% implies that roughly one quarter of the observed variability in effect estimates exceeds what sampling error alone would produce; the remaining three quarters is attributable to within-study imprecision. The Q-test does not reject homogeneity (p = 0.1138), and τ² = 0.000265 is very small on the log-RR scale, indicating that the true underlying risk ratios across these four studies are tightly clustered. A 95% prediction interval constructed from this τ² would be only slightly wider than the confidence interval itself, implying that a new study conducted in a comparable setting would be expected to produce a risk ratio in the same narrow near-null-to-modestly-positive band.

Two caveats temper this reassuring picture. With only four studies, both Q and I² are estimated with very wide uncertainty, and I² in particular is known to be unstable and downwardly biased at small k; a non-significant Q-test at k = 4 has low power and should not be read as evidence of homogeneity. Furthermore, the visible gradient between small/near-null and large/positive studies (Table 1) suggests that what heterogeneity exists may be systematically related to study size rather than randomly distributed — a pattern that the heterogeneity statistics do not capture but the funnel diagnostics do. A graphical summary of heterogeneity is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

# Moderators

No formal moderator analysis — subgroup analysis or meta-regression — was performed, and none is defensible here. With k = 4 studies in the only poolable group, there are insufficient degrees of freedom to estimate even a single covariate effect reliably; conventional guidance requires approximately ten studies per moderator. No structured covariate data (cadre type, training duration, supervision intensity, SAM versus MAM case mix, admission/discharge criterion, RUTF supply reliability, country, or year) were available in a form permitting quantitative modelling.

The one informal, descriptive pattern worth recording is the **study-size gradient** already noted: effect magnitude increases monotonically with sample size across the four pooled studies (N = 358, g = 0.086; N = 829, g = 0.037; N = 1,393, g = 0.201; N = 2,744, g = 0.264). This is displayed in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`). Because sample size in this literature correlates with programme scale, design (cluster-randomised at scale versus small pilot) and the maturity of the lay-worker cadre, this gradient is confounded and cannot be interpreted causally. It is reported as a descriptive observation and as the likely source of the funnel asymmetry described next — not as a tested moderator.

# Publication Bias

| Diagnostic | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.4501 |
| Egger's p-value | 0.0016 |
| Trim-and-fill: studies imputed (k₀) | 2 |
| Trim-and-fill: adjusted effect (standardized scale) | g = 0.2094 |
| Overall risk-of-bias rating | **High** |

Egger's test indicates **statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.0016)**, consistent with small-study effects, and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed **two** potentially missing studies on the under-represented side of the funnel. Together these support the overall **"high"** risk-of-bias rating for this analysis.

Two interpretive points follow. First, because only two studies were imputed, the adjusted pooled estimate shifts relatively modestly, and both the observed and the adjusted effects should be read side by side as a robustness check. The trim-and-fill adjusted value is **g = 0.2094 on the standardized (Hedges' g) scale**, not on the risk-ratio scale; it is therefore not directly comparable in units to the primary pooled RR of 1.071 and must not be reported as an adjusted risk ratio. What it does indicate is that the direction of the standardized pooled estimate survives imputation of the missing studies.

Second — and more importantly — funnel asymmetry is **not diagnostic of publication bias per se**. It can equally arise from between-study heterogeneity, systematic differences in methodological quality between small and large studies, or genuine effect modification by study size. In this dataset the asymmetry runs in the *unusual* direction: the largest studies report the *largest* effects, which is the opposite of the selective-reporting signature in which small studies with inflated effects are preferentially published. A plausible substantive explanation is that large-scale, mature lay-health-worker programmes (which generate the large studies) have better-developed supervision, supply chains and referral pathways than small pilots, producing genuinely better recovery rates — i.e. real effect modification by programme scale rather than dissemination bias. Finally, both Egger's test and trim-and-fill are **unreliable with fewer than approximately ten studies**; with k = 4 the Egger p-value of 0.0016 is itself fragile and should be interpreted alongside the heterogeneity statistics rather than taken at face value.

# Discussion

Four controlled comparisons encompassing 5,324 children yielded a pooled risk ratio for recovery from wasting of 1.071 (95% CI 1.037 to 1.106) when treatment was delivered by lay health workers rather than by health professionals at health facilities. The confidence interval excludes 1.00, and the associated p-value is 2.94 × 10⁻⁵. Nonetheless, the direction classification returned for this analysis is **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**, and the honest reading of the evidence is one of **near-equivalence rather than demonstrated superiority**.

The reasons to resist a superiority claim are substantive, not merely statistical. A risk ratio of 1.071 applied to a baseline cure rate of, say, 85% corresponds to about six additional children recovered per hundred treated — a difference that falls within the range that most CMAM non-inferiority evaluations pre-specify as acceptable equivalence, and well within the measurement noise introduced by heterogeneous recovery definitions. Those definitions differ materially across studies: some discharge on weight-for-height z-score, others on MUAC, and MUAC-based discharge criteria are known to classify recovery differently (and often sooner) than weight-for-height criteria in the same children. Because lay-worker arms more commonly use simplified MUAC-only protocols, part of any apparent recovery advantage could reflect the discharge criterion rather than the provider cadre. The pre-specified decision to accept each trial's own definition of recovery — necessary for feasibility — makes this a structural feature of the synthesis.

That said, the finding is practically important even under the conservative reading. The policy question that motivates task-shifting is not whether lay providers *outperform* clinicians, but whether treatment quality is *maintained* when delivery moves closer to the child. On that question the evidence here is reasonably clear: across four comparisons, with low and non-significant heterogeneity (I² = 24.56%, Q p = 0.1138, τ² = 0.000265), there is no signal of degraded recovery under lay delivery, and the point estimate sits marginally on the favourable side. The single hazard-ratio study, which could not be pooled, was likewise essentially null (g = −0.0185), reinforcing rather than contradicting this picture of equivalence in time-to-recovery terms.

Because coverage, defaulting and access outcomes were explicitly excluded from this review, the full policy case for or against task-shifting cannot be settled here. If recovery rates are approximately equivalent, then the decisive considerations become the outcomes this review did not examine — treatment coverage, geographic reach, defaulting, cost per child recovered, and the opportunity cost imposed on CHWs' other duties. Readers should not extrapolate from a near-null recovery contrast to conclusions about programme impact at population level, which depends chiefly on how many wasted children are reached at all.

# Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-handling issues arose during extraction and synthesis and are reported plainly:

1. **Effect-measure conflict in one study.** For the second outcome group, the review's pre-specified effect measure was the risk ratio (set by explicit user request), but the source paper explicitly reports a **hazard ratio** for time to recovery. The user-specified measure was retained as authoritative in the metadata, but the study was **not** pooled with the risk-ratio studies, because hazard ratios and end-of-treatment risk ratios estimate different quantities.
2. **Outcomes pooled separately by measure scale.** Consequently, the five included effect estimates were split into two outcome groups keyed by effect measure (`recovery from wasting::measure=RR`, k = 4; `recovery from wasting::measure=HR`, k = 1) rather than merged into a single pooled figure. The headline pooled result therefore covers four of the five included studies (5,324 of 6,029 children).
3. **One group had too few studies to pool.** The hazard-ratio group contains a single study and is flagged as having insufficient data. No pooled effect, confidence interval, or heterogeneity statistic is reported for it; only its standardized single effect (g = −0.0185) is shown.
4. **Reviews and duplicate reports excluded.** Narrative and systematic reviews were excluded at full-text screening as non-primary sources. Where a trial was described in more than one report, the reports were collapsed to one record so that no trial contributes more than one effect estimate to the pooled analysis, avoiding double-counting.
5. **Mixed scales in displayed statistics.** Per-study values displayed as Hedges' g, and the trim-and-fill adjusted value (g = 0.2094), are standardized conversions used for display and funnel diagnostics. The primary pooled estimate is on the native risk-ratio scale. These must not be compared as though they were in the same units.
6. **Cluster adjustment taken as reported.** Where a trial used a cluster design, the authors' own cluster-adjusted risk ratio and confidence interval were used as reported; no independent re-adjustment of design effects was attempted, so the adequacy of clustering adjustment depends on the primary authors.

# Limitations

- **Very small evidence base.** Only four studies could be pooled. At k = 4, random-effects variance estimation is imprecise, I² is unstable, the Q-test has low power, prediction intervals are unreliable, and both Egger's test and trim-and-fill fall well below the ~10-study threshold at which they perform acceptably. Every inferential statistic in this report should be read with that caveat attached.
- **High publication-bias risk.** Egger's test was significant (p = 0.0016) with two studies imputed by trim-and-fill, yielding an overall "high" rating. The asymmetry runs in the atypical direction (largest studies, largest effects), so it may reflect genuine effect modification by programme scale rather than selective dissemination — but either explanation undermines a simple pooled interpretation.
- **Heterogeneous recovery definitions.** Each study's own cure definition was accepted (weight-for-height z-score, MUAC threshold, or discharge as cured). Differential use of MUAC-only criteria in lay-worker arms could bias recovery comparisons independently of true treatment effect.
- **Mixed case severity.** Studies span SAM, MAM, or both, and programme-wide cure rates were preferred to severity-specific strata. The pooled estimate therefore conceals possible differences between SAM and MAM task-shifting, which plausibly differ in risk profile and clinical complexity.
- **Mixed designs.** Eligible designs included cluster-randomised trials and controlled programme evaluations; the latter are vulnerable to confounding by catchment characteristics, baseline burden and secular trend. No formal risk-of-bias-by-design sensitivity analysis was possible at k = 4.
- **Indeterminate direction classification.** The automated direction field for the primary analysis returned "neither", reflecting the small magnitude and the event-definition dependence of a ratio measure. We have reported that classification as returned and have not substituted our own inference from the sign of the estimate.
- **Excluded outcomes.** Defaulting, mortality, relapse, weight gain, length of stay and coverage were outside scope by design. The review consequently cannot speak to safety, sustainability of recovery, or population-level programme impact.
- **One estimand excluded from pooling.** The hazard-ratio study (N = 705) contributes no information to the pooled risk ratio, so 11.7% of the included sample sits outside the headline estimate.

# Conclusion

Across four controlled comparisons involving 5,324 children aged 6–59 months, the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for recovery from wasting with lay-health-worker-delivered treatment versus facility-based professional treatment was **1.071 (95% CI 1.037 to 1.106; p = 2.94 × 10⁻⁵; REML, native RR scale)**, with low and non-significant heterogeneity (I² = 24.56%; Q = 5.956, p = 0.1138; τ² = 0.000265). The direction classification for this result was **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**, and the magnitude is small in absolute terms. A separate outcome group reporting recovery as a hazard ratio contained only one study (N = 705) and could not be pooled; no pooled value is reported for it.

The defensible conclusion is that **lay health workers appear to achieve recovery rates at least comparable to those achieved by health professionals at health facilities**, consistent with non-inferiority, but that the current evidence does **not** establish a clinically meaningful superiority for either delivery model. Given the small number of studies, the high assessed publication-bias risk, and the heterogeneity of recovery definitions, this conclusion is provisional. Priority needs for future research are: adequately powered cluster-randomised trials using harmonised, pre-registered recovery definitions; separate reporting for SAM and MAM; consistent reporting of recovery as both an end-of-treatment proportion and a time-to-event outcome so that evidence is not fragmented across incompatible estimands; and concurrent measurement of coverage, defaulting and cost, without which the task-shifting policy question cannot be answered.
