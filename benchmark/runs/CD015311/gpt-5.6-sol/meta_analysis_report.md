# Lay Health Worker–Delivered Versus Facility Professional–Delivered Treatment for Acute Malnutrition in Children Aged 6–59 Months: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Decentralizing treatment of acute malnutrition to lay health workers may improve access, but its effect on recovery relative to treatment delivered by health professionals in facilities requires quantitative assessment.

### Objective
To estimate the effect of lay health worker–delivered treatment, compared with health professional–delivered facility treatment, on recovery or cure by the end of treatment among children aged 6–59 months with acute malnutrition or wasting, including severe acute malnutrition (SAM) and moderate acute malnutrition (MAM).

### Methods
Five studies contributed five effect sizes and 5,783 participants. Results were separated into two outcome/measure groups rather than combined because the source records distinguished three effects categorized as risk-ratio evidence from two effects associated with reports explicitly described as hazard ratios. Random-effects synthesis using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was reported for the three-study risk-ratio group. Recovery/cure was accepted according to each study’s definition. No search dates, databases, full-text screening counts, protocol details, or study-level risk-of-bias assessments were supplied.

### Results
For the three-study group (n=3,324), the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 1.0881289134586762 (95% CI 1.014–1.168; p=0.019750252701042626). Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=79.66%; τ²=0.002964; Q=6.817, p=0.0331). The supplied favorability field classified this result as neither arm favored/tie or undetermined, and that classification is retained. For the second group (two studies; n=2,459), the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 1.0918495957468304 (95% CI 0.966–1.234; p=0.15865931941478287; I²=68.85%); its favorability was also classified as neither/tie or undetermined. The latter group has an important measure-classification conflict: its papers reportedly presented hazard ratios, whereas the pipeline retained the user-requested RR label and computation. Egger’s intercept was 0.2423 (p=0.6342). Trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study and returned an adjusted quantity of 0.1964 on the pipeline’s g scale; overall publication-bias risk was rated moderate.

### Conclusions
The two separately analyzed groups yielded ratio estimates near 1.09, but only the three-study group’s confidence interval excluded 1. Both groups had substantial heterogeneity, and the supplied classification did not designate either treatment arm as favored. Interpretation is constrained by the small evidence base, incomplete review-process and risk-of-bias information, outcome-definition variation, and the RR-versus-HR classification conflict. The evidence therefore does not support a definitive conclusion that either delivery model is superior.

## Introduction

Acute malnutrition, or wasting, in children aged 6–59 months includes severe acute malnutrition (SAM) and moderate acute malnutrition (MAM). Conventional treatment commonly depends on health professionals working in health facilities. Delivery by community health workers, volunteers, or other non-professional providers may decentralize care and potentially alter access, continuity, and clinical outcomes. Whether this service-delivery strategy changes the probability of recovery or cure by the end of treatment is therefore important for program design.

This review addressed the following question: among children aged 6–59 months with acute malnutrition/wasting, what is the effect of treatment delivered by lay health workers, compared with treatment delivered by health professionals in health facilities, on recovery or cure at the end of treatment? Eligible recovery outcomes used each study’s own cure or recovery definition, such as attaining an anthropometric threshold or discharge as cured. The intended outcome did not include improvement from severe wasting alone, default, death, relapse, weight gain, treatment duration, or coverage.

## Effect Sizes

The prespecified clinical effect was the proportion of treated children who recovered or were cured by the end of treatment. The supplied synthesis used **Risk Ratio (RR)** as its measure label, with native pooling marked as used. Separate pooling was retained for the two outcome/measure groups:

1. `recovery from wasting::measure=RR`: three studies and 3,324 participants.
2. `recovery from wasting::measure=HR`: two studies and 2,459 participants.

The second group requires special caution. Although its key identifies HR evidence and the source conflict states that the papers explicitly reported hazard ratios, the analysis retained the user-requested **Risk Ratio (RR)** label and RR computation as authoritative. A hazard ratio describes an instantaneous event rate over time, whereas a risk ratio compares cumulative event probabilities. They are not generally interchangeable. Accordingly, the second pooled value is reported exactly as supplied but should not be interpreted as a validated transformation of hazard ratios into end-of-treatment risk ratios.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

### Recovery from wasting: three-study RR group

For the three-study group (k=3; n=3,324), the REML random-effects pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** was **1.0881289134586762** (95% CI **1.014 to 1.168**; p=**0.019750252701042626**). The confidence interval excluded the null value of 1.

The supplied `favors` classification was **“neither (tie or undetermined)”**; the closely corresponding overall field described this as **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).”** This report retains that provided classification and does not independently assign the result to either arm.

### Recovery from wasting: two-study group associated with HR reports

For the second group (k=2; n=2,459), the pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** was **1.0918495957468304** (95% CI **0.966 to 1.234**; p=**0.15865931941478287**). Its confidence interval included 1, and the result was not statistically significant at a two-sided 0.05 threshold.

The supplied `favors` classification was again **“neither (tie or undetermined).”** As noted above, this group is labeled as an RR analysis in the results, but the underlying papers reportedly expressed their effects as HRs. The estimate must therefore be interpreted with particular caution.

The two groups were not combined into a single overall estimate. Such merging would obscure the reported distinction in source effect measures and could imply comparability that has not been established. A cumulative analysis figure is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; without the study ordering rule and cumulative numerical estimates, it is treated as descriptive rather than as evidence of temporal stabilization.

## Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity was substantial in both groups.

For the three-study group:

- I² = **79.66%**;
- τ² = **0.002964**;
- Cochran’s Q = **6.817**; and
- Q-test p = **0.0331**.

Thus, the Q test rejected homogeneity at the 0.05 level, while I² indicated that a large proportion of observed variation was attributed to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error. Given only three studies, however, I², τ², and Q are imprecisely estimated.

For the two-study group, I² was **68.85%**, also indicating substantial inconsistency. Neither τ² nor Q and its p value were supplied for this group. With only two studies, estimation of between-study heterogeneity is especially unstable, so the pooled result should not be regarded as a universally applicable common effect.

Potential sources of heterogeneity could include differences in SAM versus MAM populations, cure definitions, anthropometric discharge thresholds, provider training and supervision, treatment protocols, setting, follow-up, and analytic denominator. These explanations are clinically plausible but could not be tested using the supplied data. ## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **0.2423** with p=**0.6342**. This was not statistically significant and provided no clear evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. However, only five effect sizes were available. Tests for funnel-plot asymmetry have low power with such a small evidence base, and a non-significant result cannot establish the absence of reporting bias.

Trim-and-fill estimated **one** potentially missing study and returned an adjusted quantity of **0.1964** on the pipeline’s `g` scale. This quantity is not labeled as a Risk Ratio and should not be compared directly with the pooled RRs. The supplied overall publication-bias risk was **moderate**. Taken together, the analyses do not demonstrate asymmetry, but publication bias and other small-study effects cannot be ruled out.

## Discussion

This synthesis included five studies and 5,783 participants, divided into two groups rather than merged. The three-study group produced a pooled Risk Ratio of 1.0881289134586762, with a 95% confidence interval from 1.014 to 1.168 and p=0.019750252701042626. The second, two-study group produced a pooled Risk Ratio of 1.0918495957468304, with a wider 95% confidence interval from 0.966 to 1.234 and p=0.15865931941478287. Although the numerical point estimates were similar, their precision differed, and the underlying effect-measure conflict prevents treating them as straightforward replications of the same estimand.

In accordance with the supplied favorability fields, both pooled results are described as favoring **neither arm (tie or undetermined)**. This classification is especially consequential because the research protocol otherwise specified that RR values above 1 would indicate a higher recovery probability under lay-health-worker delivery. The available context does not resolve why the automated favorability classification remained undetermined. Accordingly, the report does not override it or claim superiority for lay-worker delivery.

The substantial heterogeneity further limits a simple average-effect interpretation. Community-based treatment models may vary materially in provider selection, training, supervision, referral capacity, supply continuity, and adherence to treatment algorithms. Facility comparators may also differ in staffing and access barriers. In addition, recovery definitions can vary by weight-for-height, mid-upper-arm circumference, edema resolution, program discharge criteria, and follow-up timing. With no study-level characteristics, none of these possible explanations can be evaluated.

The findings should therefore be interpreted as limited quantitative summaries rather than definitive evidence of equivalence or superiority. The three-study confidence interval excluded the null, but statistical significance does not eliminate uncertainty from heterogeneity, sparse studies, outcome variation, or measurement classification. The two-study interval included both the null and potentially meaningful effects in either direction. Moreover, the absence of supplied absolute recovery risks prevents translation of relative effects into absolute numbers of additional or fewer children recovering.

From a policy perspective, treatment-delivery decisions should consider more than pooled recovery alone. Safety, mortality, default, referral failure, equity, acceptability, caregiver burden, coverage, costs, and health-system capacity may be important, but they were outside this outcome and must not be substituted for cure. Future comparative studies should report arm-specific recovered and randomized/treated denominators, prespecified end-of-treatment definitions, intention-to-treat analyses, and both relative and absolute effects. Reports should distinguish cumulative recovery risks from time-to-recovery hazard ratios.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Several data-quality issues were evident in the supplied results:

- Five individual effects were provided in a standardized-effect field, but the pooled analyses were labeled as Risk Ratios. The absence of raw event counts and study-to-group mapping prevented independent validation or conversion.
- The second group’s source papers reportedly presented hazard ratios, yet the pipeline retained the user-requested RR label and computation. This RR-versus-HR conflict is material because the measures represent different estimands.
- Native pooling was marked as used for both groups; no standardized fallback was identified for either pooled estimate. Nonetheless, the source-measure conflict in the second group remains unresolved.
- The favorability fields classified both pooled results as neither/tie or undetermined, despite the protocol’s stated directional rule. The supplied favorability classification was followed without reinterpretation.
- Publication-bias trim-and-fill output was supplied on a `g` scale rather than the RR scale, so it was not used as an adjusted pooled RR.
- Search, screening, study characteristics, recovery definitions, arm-level data, risk-of-bias judgments, and a protocol or registration record were not supplied.

## Limitations

First, the evidence base was small: three studies informed one synthesis and two informed the other. Pooled effects, heterogeneity estimates, and publication-bias tests are consequently unstable.

Second, substantial heterogeneity was present (I²=79.66% and 68.85%). Without study-level covariates, its sources and clinical implications could not be investigated.

Third, the outcome accepted each study’s own definition of recovery or cure. This improves inclusiveness but may combine clinically different discharge criteria and time horizons. The supplied data did not document those definitions or verify use of whole-program and intention-to-treat denominators.

Fourth, one group was associated with papers explicitly reporting hazard ratios but was analyzed and labeled as a Risk Ratio under the user-requested measure. Hazard ratios and risk ratios should not be assumed equivalent. This issue may affect the interpretability of the two-study pooled estimate.

Fifth, no study-level risk-of-bias assessment was available. Bias arising from randomization, allocation concealment, deviations from intended treatment, missing outcome data, outcome ascertainment, or selective reporting could not be evaluated.

Sixth, the search and screening methods were not provided, preventing assessment of search completeness, language or publication restrictions, duplicate reports, and selection decisions. Bibliographic details for the five primary studies were also absent.

Seventh, publication-bias analyses based on five effects are underpowered. Trim-and-fill relies on assumptions that may fail in the presence of genuine heterogeneity, and its supplied adjusted value was not on the RR scale.

Finally, no absolute risks were supplied. Even a valid RR cannot indicate the absolute clinical impact without the baseline probability of recovery in facility care.

## Conclusion

Five studies involving 5,783 children were analyzed in two separate groups. In the three-study group, the pooled Risk Ratio was 1.0881289134586762 (95% CI 1.014–1.168; p=0.019750252701042626), with substantial heterogeneity (I²=79.66%). In the two-study group associated with source HR reports, the pooled Risk Ratio was 1.0918495957468304 (95% CI 0.966–1.234; p=0.15865931941478287), also with substantial heterogeneity (I²=68.85%). The supplied favorability classification designated both results as neither arm favored/tie or undetermined.

Overall, the evidence is too limited and methodologically uncertain to conclude definitively that lay health worker delivery or facility professional delivery produces superior recovery. Better-reported comparative studies, harmonized cure definitions, arm-level event data, explicit intention-to-treat analyses, and correct separation of risk ratios from time-to-event hazard ratios are needed.
