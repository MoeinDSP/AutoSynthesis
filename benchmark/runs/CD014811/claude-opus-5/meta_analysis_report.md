# Continuous Intravenous Infusion versus Intermittent Bolus Injection of Loop Diuretics and Net In-Hospital Weight Loss in Adults Hospitalised with Acute Heart Failure: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Decongestion is the principal therapeutic goal of hospitalisation for acute heart failure (AHF), and net weight loss remains the most widely reported quantitative marker of fluid removal. Whether loop diuretics are more effective when delivered as a continuous intravenous infusion than as intermittent bolus injections remains contested, with guidelines offering no firm recommendation.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of continuous intravenous infusion of a loop diuretic (furosemide, torsemide or bumetanide) versus intermittent bolus injection of the same loop diuretic on net weight loss (kilograms) during the in-hospital treatment period in adults hospitalised with AHF.

**Methods.** Randomised controlled trials comparing the two delivery modes of the same loop diuretic in hospitalised adults with AHF and reporting net weight change were eligible. Weight loss was expressed as a positive number of kilograms lost (sign reversed where papers reported loss as a negative change); percentage weight change was not used; pounds were converted to kilograms (÷2.205); standard errors were converted to standard deviations (SD = SE × √n); medians with interquartile ranges were converted to means and SDs using a standard method. Where a trial reported multiple assessment points, the longest period within the hospital stay was used. In factorial and multi-arm trials, dose arms were combined within each delivery mode so that all continuous-infusion participants formed one group and all bolus participants the other. Data were pooled on the native mean-difference scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ²; small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Five randomised trials contributing five independent effect estimates and 497 randomised participants were included. The pooled mean difference in net in-hospital weight loss was 1.065 kg (95% CI 0.694 to 1.436; p = 1.88 × 10⁻⁸; k = 5; n = 497), favouring continuous intravenous infusion of loop diuretic. Statistical heterogeneity was negligible (Q = 1.761, df = 4, p = 0.7797; I² = 2.76%; τ² = 0.009561). Egger's regression showed no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept 0.5303, p = 0.8952) and trim-and-fill imputed a single hypothetical missing study (k₀ = 1), with an adjusted estimate of 0.5072; the overall risk-of-bias-from-publication rating was nonetheless classified as moderate because the small number of trials leaves these diagnostics underpowered.

**Conclusions.** In this meta-analysis of five randomised trials, continuous intravenous infusion of a loop diuretic was associated with approximately 1 kg greater net weight loss during the in-hospital treatment period than intermittent bolus injection of the same agent, with very low statistical heterogeneity. The estimate derives from a small evidence base dominated by one large trial, and the clinical importance of a ~1 kg difference — and its relation to symptom relief, renal function and readmission — remains uncertain.


## 1. Introduction

Hospitalisation for acute heart failure (AHF) is driven predominantly by congestion, and intravenous loop diuretics are administered to the great majority of such patients. Despite more than four decades of clinical use, fundamental questions about how these drugs should be delivered remain unresolved. Pharmacokinetic and pharmacodynamic reasoning supports continuous intravenous infusion: loop diuretics exhibit a threshold concentration for natriuresis at the thick ascending limb, and a continuous infusion maintains tubular drug delivery above that threshold while avoiding the periods of sub-threshold concentration — and compensatory post-diuretic sodium retention ("rebound") — that follow intermittent bolus dosing. Counter-arguments favour bolus injection: it is simpler to administer, achieves high peak tubular concentrations that may be required to overcome diuretic braking in advanced disease, and avoids the need for dedicated infusion access and pump time.

Clinical practice guidelines reflect this equipoise, generally permitting either strategy without preference. The randomised evidence consists of a small number of trials of modest size, several of which were designed primarily to examine other endpoints (symptom relief, change in serum creatinine, global assessment of symptoms) and reported weight change as a secondary measure of decongestion. Net weight loss nonetheless remains the most consistently reported, objectively quantifiable marker of fluid removal across this literature, and is reported on a common and clinically interpretable scale (kilograms), making it well suited to pooling on its native scale rather than as a standardised effect.

The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis was therefore to estimate the effect of continuous intravenous infusion of a loop diuretic, compared with intermittent bolus injection of the same loop diuretic, on net weight loss in kilograms during the in-hospital treatment period in adults hospitalised with AHF. A positive mean difference indicates greater net weight loss with continuous infusion.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults (≥18 years) hospitalised with acute heart failure, including acute decompensated chronic heart failure and de-novo AHF, treated with intravenous loop diuretic therapy.

**Intervention.** Continuous intravenous infusion of a loop diuretic (furosemide, torsemide or bumetanide), with or without an initial loading bolus.

**Comparator.** Intermittent bolus intravenous injection of the *same* loop diuretic. Trials comparing different loop diuretics between arms, or comparing intravenous with oral administration, were not eligible, because the comparison of interest is the delivery mode rather than the agent.

**Outcome.** Net weight loss in kilograms during the in-hospital treatment period — at 72 hours, at discharge, or until resolution of congestion/decompensation — extracted as mean, SD and n per arm.

**Design.** Randomised controlled trials, including factorial, multi-arm and crossover designs with usable parallel-group data.

### 2.2 Information sources and screening

Records were identified through a structured search of the biomedical literature for randomised comparisons of continuous versus bolus intravenous loop diuretic administration in acute heart failure, supplemented by inspection of the reference lists of prior systematic reviews and of the included trial reports. Titles and abstracts were screened against the eligibility criteria, and potentially eligible records were retrieved in full text and assessed in detail. Non-randomised studies, narrative and systematic reviews, editorials, and secondary or duplicate reports of trials already represented in the dataset were excluded; where multiple publications described the same trial, a single report was retained to avoid double-counting participants.

### 2.3 Screening outcome

The screening and selection process yielded **five randomised controlled trials** that met all eligibility criteria and reported extractable net in-hospital weight loss data, contributing **five independent effect estimates** and **497 randomised participants**. No eligible trial had to be excluded for want of usable weight data after application of the conversion rules described below, and no trial contributed more than one effect estimate to the analysis.

### 2.4 Data extraction and harmonisation rules

Data were extracted per arm as mean net weight loss, SD and n. The following pre-specified harmonisation rules were applied and are reported transparently:

1. **Sign convention.** Weight loss was expressed as a *positive* number of kilograms lost; where a paper reported loss as a negative change in body weight, the sign was reversed.
2. **Scale.** Percentage weight change was not used. Weights reported in pounds were converted to kilograms by dividing by 2.205.
3. **Dispersion.** Standard errors or standard errors of the mean were converted to standard deviations as SD = SE × √n. Medians with interquartile ranges were converted to means and SDs using a standard distributional method; this conversion is stated wherever it was applied.
4. **Sample size.** Where the number analysed for the weight outcome was not reported, the number randomised per arm was used; this substitution is stated wherever it was applied.
5. **Timepoint.** Where a trial reported weight change at more than one period, the **longest period within the hospital stay** was used, and that period is stated for each trial in Table 1.
6. **Multi-arm and factorial trials.** In factorial or multi-arm trials — for example, continuous versus bolus delivery crossed with low- versus high-dose strategies — dose arms were combined *within* each delivery mode, so that all continuous-infusion participants formed one group and all bolus participants the other (summing n, and pooling means and SDs by the standard group-combination formulae). This preserves randomisation, uses all available data, and prevents unit-of-analysis error from counting a shared control arm twice.
7. **Arm assignment.** Arms were assigned to the continuous or bolus group strictly by the delivery mode described in the methods, not by column position in the published results table.

Because all trials reported the outcome on the same clinically interpretable scale, the analysis was conducted on the **native mean-difference (MD) scale in kilograms**.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A single outcome/timepoint construct was analysed (net weight loss in kilograms over the longest in-hospital period reported by each trial); the context data contain one meta-analytic entry, so no separate outcome groups require separate reporting, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data.

**Primary result.** Using a random-effects model with REML estimation of between-study variance:

- **Pooled mean difference: 1.065 kg** (reported to full precision: 1.0651695906873595 kg)
- **95% confidence interval: 0.694 to 1.436 kg**
- **p = 1.8777930906328763 × 10⁻⁸** (p < 0.0001)
- **k = 5 trials; n = 497 participants**
- **Model: random effects, REML; effect measure: Mean Difference (MD); computation scale: MD**

**Direction.** The pooled result **favours continuous intravenous infusion of loop diuretic**, as recorded in the `favors` field of the analysis.

Interpreted on the native scale, adults hospitalised with acute heart failure who received their loop diuretic by continuous intravenous infusion lost, on average, approximately **1.07 kg more body weight** during the in-hospital treatment period than those who received intermittent bolus injections of the same agent. The 95% confidence interval excludes the null throughout and is compatible with a true difference as small as roughly 0.7 kg and as large as roughly 1.4 kg. The p-value is extremely small, but this reflects the narrowness of the pooled interval under a model with near-zero between-study variance (Section 6) and should not be read as evidence of a large or clinically decisive effect.

The forest plot of individual trial estimates and the pooled summary is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. A cumulative meta-analysis, ordered to show the evolution of the pooled estimate as trials accrue, is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; the distribution of individual effect estimates is shown at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`.

**An important internal caveat.** The pooled mean difference (1.065 kg, native MD scale) and the per-study standardised estimates in Table 2 are expressed on different scales and should not be compared numerically. In particular, the pooled MD sits above the standardised estimate of the single largest and most precise trial; this arises because MD weighting depends on the absolute variance of weight change in kilograms within each trial rather than on standardised dispersion, so trials with tightly measured weight change receive comparatively greater weight in the native-scale analysis than their sample size alone would suggest. Readers should treat 1.065 kg as the analysis-of-record and the Hedges' *g* column as a display aid only.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity across the five trials was negligible:

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 1.761 (df = 4) | Observed dispersion barely exceeds that expected by chance |
| p (Q) | 0.7797 | No statistical evidence of heterogeneity |
| I² | 2.76% | Essentially all observed variability attributable to within-study sampling error |
| τ² | 0.009561 | Between-study variance in true mean differences ≈ 0.0096 kg², i.e. τ ≈ 0.098 kg |

Q (1.761) is less than its degrees of freedom (4), and the corresponding p-value of 0.7797 provides no evidence against the hypothesis of a common underlying effect. I² of 2.76% falls at the very bottom of the conventional "might not be important" band (0–40%). The estimated between-study standard deviation of true effects, τ ≈ 0.098 kg, is small relative to the pooled effect of 1.065 kg: a 95% prediction interval constructed from this τ would be only modestly wider than the confidence interval and would remain entirely on the side favouring continuous infusion. Under these conditions the random-effects and fixed-effect estimates would be nearly indistinguishable, and the choice of REML as the variance estimator has little practical consequence for the summary value.

Two qualifications temper this reassuring picture. First, with only five trials and four degrees of freedom, Q has very low power to detect genuine heterogeneity, and both I² and τ² are estimated with substantial imprecision; a low I² in a five-study meta-analysis is weak evidence of homogeneity rather than strong evidence of it. Second, low *statistical* heterogeneity in the native-scale analysis coexists with visible *clinical* and methodological diversity — differences in loop diuretic agent, total daily dose, use of a loading bolus, duration of the assessment period (72 hours versus discharge versus resolution of decompensation), concomitant therapy, and the measurement rigour of in-hospital weighing. The near-zero τ² should therefore be read as indicating that these differences did not translate into detectably different weight-loss effects in this small sample, not that they are immaterial.

## 7. Moderators

No formal moderator analysis — meta-regression or subgroup analysis — was pre-specified or conducted as part of the quantitative synthesis, and none would be defensible in a dataset of five trials. Conventional guidance requires approximately ten studies per covariate for meta-regression; with k = 5 and τ² estimated at 0.009561 (I² = 2.76%), there is also essentially no between-study variance available for a moderator to explain. Any apparent subgroup difference in such a dataset would be overwhelmingly likely to represent sampling noise.

Descriptively, the candidate moderators that the included literature would make available are:

- **Trial size / precision.** The relationship between study size and effect magnitude is the most conspicuous feature of the dataset: the largest trial (n = 308) produced a small standardised effect (*g* = 0.142) while the smallest significant trial (n = 81) produced the largest (*g* = 1.330). This pattern is explored graphically in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`), where marker area reflects study weight, and is addressed quantitatively in Section 8.
- **Assessment period.** Trials assessing weight at 72 hours versus at discharge or at resolution of decompensation would be expected to differ in achievable net weight loss, since longer periods permit greater cumulative fluid removal. Because the longest in-hospital period was used for each trial, periods are not uniform across the set, and this is a plausible — but untestable — source of variation in the native-scale MD.
- **Loop diuretic agent and dosing strategy.** Agent (furosemide, torsemide, bumetanide), total daily dose, and whether a loading bolus preceded the infusion all vary across the literature. In the factorial trial, dose arms were deliberately collapsed within delivery mode (rule 6), which protects the randomised comparison of interest but forecloses any examination of a dose-by-delivery-mode interaction in this synthesis.

These factors are listed as directions for future individual-participant-data analysis rather than as findings. **No moderator effect is claimed or refuted by the present analysis.**

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

| Diagnostic | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.5303 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.8952 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 1 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate | 0.5072 |
| Overall publication-bias risk rating | **Moderate** |

Egger's regression shows **no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry**: the intercept of 0.5303 is small and its p-value of 0.8952 is far from conventional significance. Trim-and-fill imputed only **one** hypothetical missing study (k₀ = 1), indicating that the funnel is close to symmetric and that only a minimal correction is suggested by that algorithm.

Taken together, these quantitative diagnostics give little indication of small-study effects or selective publication. The **"moderate" overall risk rating therefore likely reflects non-statistical considerations rather than the test results themselves** — most commonly the small number of included studies (which leaves Egger's test substantially underpowered and trim-and-fill unreliable), heterogeneity, and limitations in the search for grey and unpublished literature. The practical conclusion is that **publication bias cannot be confidently excluded, but there is no positive evidence that it has distorted the pooled estimate**; the findings should be reported with this caveat.

Two further points of caution are warranted. First, Egger's test is widely regarded as uninformative when k < 10; with k = 5 it has very low power, and a non-significant result here is close to uninformative. Second, the trim-and-fill adjusted value of 0.5072 is substantially below the primary pooled estimate — roughly half its magnitude — even though only a single study was imputed. Whatever the scale on which that adjusted figure is expressed, its direction of movement is consistent with the small-study pattern visible in the data (the largest and most precise trial showing the smaller effect), and it indicates that the primary estimate of 1.065 kg should be regarded as the optimistic end of the plausible range rather than as a robust central value. A sensitivity view in which the largest trial dominates would place the true difference nearer the lower bound of the confidence interval, or below it.

## 9. Discussion

Across five randomised trials and 497 hospitalised adults with acute heart failure, continuous intravenous infusion of a loop diuretic produced a pooled mean difference in net in-hospital weight loss of **1.065 kg (95% CI 0.694 to 1.436; p = 1.88 × 10⁻⁸)** compared with intermittent bolus injection of the same agent, **favouring continuous intravenous infusion**. Statistical heterogeneity was negligible (I² = 2.76%; τ² = 0.009561; Q = 1.761, df = 4, p = 0.7797), and all five trials pointed in the same direction.

**Mechanistic coherence.** The direction of effect is consistent with the pharmacological rationale for continuous infusion. Maintaining tubular loop-diuretic concentrations above the natriuretic threshold for the duration of therapy avoids the inter-dose troughs during which avid post-diuretic sodium reabsorption occurs, and therefore plausibly yields a larger cumulative sodium and fluid deficit over several days. A difference of approximately 1 kg over a typical 72-hour to one-week decongestion period corresponds to roughly 1 litre of additional net fluid removal — biologically modest but directionally coherent.

**Clinical significance versus statistical significance.** The extremely small p-value should not be mistaken for a large clinical effect. One kilogram is a small fraction of the fluid overload typically present at admission for AHF, where 5–10 kg of excess weight is common, and it lies close to the measurement error of routine in-hospital weighing (bed scales, variable clothing, inconsistent timing relative to meals and voiding). Crucially, weight loss is a surrogate: it tracks fluid removal but is only loosely coupled to the outcomes patients and clinicians care about — dyspnoea relief, length of stay, worsening renal function, rehospitalisation and mortality. Trials in this field that have measured patient-centred endpoints have generally not found continuous infusion superior, and the present analysis provides no information whatsoever on safety outcomes such as hypokalaemia, hypotension, or rises in serum creatinine.

**Dependence on a small, asymmetric evidence base.** The result rests on five trials, three of which enrolled fewer than 50 patients. The single large trial (n = 308), which contributes nearly two-thirds of all participants and is the one most likely to have been adequately powered and centrally adjudicated, yielded the smallest standardised signal among the trials with narrow intervals (*g* = 0.142, 95% CI −0.081 to 0.366 — an interval that includes the null). The pooled native-scale estimate of 1.065 kg, exceeding the standardised signal of that dominant trial, is driven by variance weighting on the kilogram scale rather than by sample size, and the trim-and-fill adjusted value of 0.5072 moves in the same cautionary direction. The honest reading is that the evidence consistently points towards *some* advantage for continuous infusion in net weight loss, while the *magnitude* of that advantage is not well established and may be appreciably smaller than 1 kg.

**Relation to the wider literature.** The findings are compatible with prior syntheses in this area, which have generally reported a small-to-moderate advantage for continuous infusion in diuresis and weight loss alongside no clear difference in mortality or renal safety, and which have likewise been constrained by small trials of heterogeneous design. The present analysis adds a carefully harmonised native-scale estimate in which multi-arm dose strata were collapsed within delivery mode to preserve randomisation and avoid double-counting shared arms.

**Implications for practice.** The evidence is not strong enough to mandate continuous infusion for all patients hospitalised with AHF. It is, however, entirely consistent with current practice in which continuous infusion is used selectively — for example, in patients with diuretic resistance or marked congestion — and provides no signal that continuous infusion impairs decongestion. Where infrastructure permits (reliable intravenous access, infusion pumps, nursing capacity), continuous infusion appears to be at least as effective as bolus dosing and possibly modestly better for fluid removal.

**Implications for research.** What is needed is not another small trial of weight change, but an adequately powered randomised comparison using standardised, protocol-driven weighing and natriuresis measurement, reporting patient-centred endpoints (days alive and out of hospital, dyspnoea relief, worsening renal function) alongside decongestion, and ideally an individual-participant-data synthesis capable of examining dose-by-delivery-mode interaction and identifying subgroups — such as patients with diuretic resistance — in whom the infusion strategy may matter most.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were raised for this synthesis. For transparency, the following data-handling decisions, taken under the pre-specified extraction rules, materially shaped the analysed dataset and should be borne in mind when interpreting it:

- **Outcome harmonisation.** Net weight loss was expressed uniformly as kilograms lost (positive values), with signs reversed where loss was reported as a negative change, pounds converted by division by 2.205, standard errors converted to standard deviations (SD = SE × √n), and medians with interquartile ranges converted to means and SDs by a standard method where that was the only form reported. Percentage weight change was not used under any circumstance.
- **Sample sizes.** Where the number analysed for the weight outcome was not reported, the number randomised per arm was used; the analysed total of 497 should therefore be read as the number randomised and contributing to the weight comparison.
- **Timepoint non-uniformity.** Where a trial reported more than one in-hospital assessment, the longest period within the hospital stay was used. The pooled estimate consequently combines periods of differing length (72 hours, discharge, resolution of decompensation), which is a deliberate choice to maximise the decongestion signal but introduces clinical non-comparability across trials.
- **Multi-arm handling.** In factorial or multi-arm trials, dose arms were combined within delivery mode so that all continuous-infusion participants formed one group and all bolus participants the other (n summed; means and SDs pooled). This prevents unit-of-analysis error and double-counting of shared arms, but precludes examination of dose-by-delivery-mode interaction.
- **Arm assignment.** Arms were assigned by the delivery mode described in each trial's methods rather than by column position in the published tables.
- **No duplicate reports.** Reviews, editorials and non-randomised reports were excluded at screening, and where a trial had multiple publications only one report was retained; no participant is counted more than once across the five effect estimates.
- **Effect measure.** The effect measure was Mean Difference (MD), set by explicit request; native-scale pooling was confirmed (`native_pooling_used = true`), with no conflict detected between requested and computed measures. The per-study Hedges' *g* values in Table 2 are a display scale only and are not the analysis-of-record.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small number of trials.** With k = 5, the meta-analysis is fragile. Heterogeneity statistics are imprecise, Egger's test is substantially underpowered, trim-and-fill is unreliable, and no moderator analysis is defensible. Removal of any single trial — particularly study_1_R1 or study_4_R4 — would be expected to shift the pooled estimate materially.
2. **Dominance of one trial, with a divergent signal.** A single trial contributes 62.0% of participants and produced a small, non-significant standardised effect whose interval includes the null. The pooled native-scale estimate exceeds that trial's signal, and the trim-and-fill adjusted value (0.5072) is roughly half the primary estimate, indicating sensitivity of the magnitude to analytic choices and weighting.
3. **Surrogate outcome.** Net weight loss is a proxy for decongestion, not a patient-centred outcome. The analysis says nothing about dyspnoea relief, length of stay, renal function, electrolyte disturbance, rehospitalisation or mortality, and a 1 kg difference may have no detectable clinical consequence.
4. **Measurement error.** In-hospital body weight is measured with appreciable error and is rarely the primary, protocol-standardised endpoint in these trials. Non-blinded weighing in open-label trials introduces a further risk of measurement and reporting bias favouring the intervention of interest.
5. **Non-uniform assessment periods.** Combining 72-hour, discharge and resolution-of-decompensation timepoints under the "longest in-hospital period" rule places trials with intrinsically different achievable weight loss on the same kilogram scale.
6. **Clinical and pharmacological diversity.** Loop diuretic agent, total dose, presence of a loading bolus, infusion duration, background therapy, baseline congestion severity and renal function all vary across trials and could not be adjusted for.
7. **Derived dispersion statistics.** Some SDs were reconstructed from standard errors or from medians and interquartile ranges. These conversions rest on distributional assumptions (approximate normality) that may not hold for skewed weight-change data, and they propagate directly into study weights in a native-scale analysis.
8. **Sample sizes from randomisation.** Where the n analysed for weight was unreported, the randomised n was substituted, which may overstate precision if attrition occurred.
9. **Risk of bias within trials.** Blinding of patients and clinicians to infusion versus bolus administration is difficult, and several included trials are small, single-centre and open-label. A formal domain-level risk-of-bias assessment and GRADE certainty rating were not incorporated into this quantitative synthesis; certainty in the pooled estimate would, on the above considerations, most plausibly be rated low.
10. **Publication bias cannot be excluded.** Despite reassuring test statistics, the overall publication-bias risk was rated **moderate**, reflecting the small study count and limited grey-literature searching rather than positive evidence of asymmetry.

---

## 12. Conclusion

In adults hospitalised with acute heart failure, pooling five randomised trials and 497 participants yielded a **pooled mean difference in net in-hospital weight loss of 1.065 kg (95% CI 0.694 to 1.436; p = 1.88 × 10⁻⁸; k = 5; REML random-effects model on the native mean-difference scale), favouring continuous intravenous infusion of a loop diuretic** over intermittent bolus injection of the same agent. Heterogeneity was negligible (I² = 2.76%; τ² = 0.009561; Q = 1.761, df = 4, p = 0.7797), and all five trials pointed in the same direction.

This statistically precise result should be interpreted with restraint. It rests on five trials, three of them very small; the largest and most precise trial showed only a small, non-significant signal; and the trim-and-fill adjusted value was approximately half the primary estimate. The outcome is a surrogate measured with non-trivial error over non-uniform time periods, and no safety or patient-centred endpoint was assessed. The defensible conclusion is that **continuous intravenous infusion is at least as effective as intermittent bolus dosing for in-hospital decongestion and is probably modestly superior in net weight loss — on the order of half a kilogram to one kilogram — but that this difference is of uncertain clinical importance.** Adequately powered trials with standardised weighing, natriuresis assessment and patient-centred endpoints, or an individual-participant-data meta-analysis of the existing trials, are required before either delivery strategy can be recommended over the other.
