# Prophylactic Skin-Care Interventions During Infancy for Prevention of Eczema by 1–3 Years: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Impaired epidermal barrier function has been proposed as a modifiable contributor to early-life eczema. Prophylactic emollients, moisturizers, barrier-enhancing products, and structured skin-care regimens have therefore been evaluated as primary-prevention strategies during infancy.

### Objective
To determine, among infants aged 12 months or younger, whether prophylactic skin-care interventions applied during infancy reduce the risk of developing eczema by 1–3 years of age compared with standard or usual skin care, placebo or minimal skin care, or no skin-care intervention.

### Methods
The supplied evidence set contained six studies, of which three contributed effect sizes to the quantitative synthesis. These three effect sizes represented 1,521 participants. Native risk ratios were pooled using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger’s test and trim-and-fill, while recognizing the severe limitations of these procedures with only three studies.

### Results
The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 1.0362202513958838 (95% CI 0.684–1.569; p=0.8665371418734427; k=3; total N=1,521). In accordance with the supplied direction classification, the pooled result favored neither arm and was essentially a tie; interpretation of favorability for a ratio also depends on which event was counted. Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=68.01%; τ²=0.085264), although Cochran’s Q did not cross the conventional 0.05 threshold (Q=5.868; p=0.0532). Egger’s intercept was 0.2098 (p=0.6476), providing no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry. Trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study and returned an adjusted estimate of 0.1312 on the scale reported by that analysis. Overall publication-bias risk was classified as moderate.

### Conclusions
The available quantitative evidence did not demonstrate that prophylactic skin care during infancy lowers eczema risk by 1–3 years of age. The pooled estimate was imprecise and compatible with potentially meaningful effects in either direction. Substantial between-study heterogeneity, the small number of analyzable studies, incomplete study-level descriptors, and limited power to evaluate publication bias reduce confidence in a definitive conclusion.

## Search and Screening

The review question was structured around infants aged 12 months or younger who received prophylactic skin care during infancy, including emollients, moisturizers, barrier-enhancing products, or structured skin-care regimens. Eligible comparators were standard or usual skin care, placebo or minimal skin care, or no skin-care intervention. The outcome was incident eczema (atopic dermatitis) assessed between 1 and 3 years of age, whether clinician-diagnosed, criteria-based, or parent-reported, provided that it represented development of eczema after intervention initiation.

The supplied dataset identified six studies. Three studies contributed effect sizes to the meta-analysis, yielding a quantitative sample of 1,521 participants. ## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood produced a pooled **Risk Ratio (RR) of 1.0362202513958838** (95% CI **0.684–1.569**; p=**0.8665371418734427**), based on **k=3** studies and **N=1,521** participants.

The supplied favorability field classified this result as favoring **neither arm**, describing it as essentially a tie and noting that favorability for a ratio depends on which event was counted. This classification is retained rather than inferred from the numerical sign or from whether the RR lies above or below 1. The estimate was very close to the null ratio, and the confidence interval crossed 1 widely. Thus, the synthesis provided no statistically significant evidence that prophylactic skin care during infancy changed eczema risk by 1–3 years of age relative to standard, minimal, placebo, or no skin-care intervention.

The confidence interval is clinically important: it remains compatible with both a potentially meaningful reduction and a potentially meaningful increase in the counted event. Therefore, failure to reject the null should not be interpreted as proof of equivalence or proof that all prophylactic skin-care approaches are ineffective.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 5.868
- **Q-test p value:** 0.0532
- **I²:** 68.01%
- **τ²:** 0.085264
- **Estimator:** REML

An I² of 68.01% indicates that a substantial proportion of observed variability was attributable to between-study differences rather than sampling error under the assumptions of the model. The Q-test narrowly missed the conventional threshold for statistical significance (p=0.0532), but this should not be treated as evidence of homogeneity. With only three studies, Q has low power to detect genuine heterogeneity. Conversely, I² is itself imprecisely estimated when k is small. The nonzero τ² estimate supports the decision to use a random-effects model.

Potential sources of heterogeneity could include differences in infant baseline risk, family history of atopy, intervention ingredients and barrier properties, timing and frequency of application, duration and adherence, co-interventions, comparator skin-care practices, outcome definitions, outcome assessors, and follow-up ages. These explanations are clinically plausible but could not be tested with the data supplied.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression yielded an intercept of **0.2098** with p=**0.6476**. This was not statistically significant and therefore provided no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Nevertheless, a nonsignificant Egger test is weak reassurance when only three studies are available: asymmetry tests generally have poor power in such small evidence bases and are not considered reliable for ruling out publication bias.

Trim-and-fill estimated **one potentially missing study** (`k0=1`) and produced an adjusted estimate of **0.1312** on the scale reported by that procedure. The supplied context did not identify this adjusted value as a native RR or provide a confidence interval. It should therefore not be interpreted as an adjusted pooled risk ratio or directly compared numerically with the principal RR without additional scale information. Trim-and-fill is also sensitive to genuine heterogeneity and cannot establish that an apparently missing study reflects publication bias rather than sampling variation or design differences.

Taken together, the publication-bias risk was classified as **moderate**. The appropriate interpretation is that publication bias cannot be excluded, despite the nonsignificant Egger test.

## Discussion

This synthesis addressed whether prophylactic skin-care interventions initiated during infancy prevent eczema by 1–3 years of age. Across three quantitatively analyzable studies and 1,521 participants, the REML random-effects pooled RR was 1.0362202513958838, with a 95% confidence interval from 0.684 to 1.569. The pooled effect favored neither arm according to the supplied classification and did not show a statistically significant preventive effect.

The principal conclusion is not that prophylactic skin care has been proven equivalent to usual care. Rather, the available estimate is uncertain. Its wide confidence interval encompasses effects that could be clinically relevant in either direction, and heterogeneity was substantial. The result therefore argues against a confident claim of benefit based on the current supplied evidence, while also leaving uncertainty about intervention-specific effects.

The heterogeneity may be especially consequential in this clinical area. “Prophylactic skin care” is a broad category rather than a single standardized treatment. Products may differ in lipid composition, occlusiveness, pH, preservatives, potential sensitizers, and effects on skin microbiota. Regimens may also differ in treatment initiation, frequency, body-surface coverage, bathing advice, duration, and adherence. Pooling such interventions estimates an average effect across potentially different strategies and does not establish that every product or regimen has the same benefit–harm profile.

Outcome heterogeneity is another concern. Clinician-diagnosed eczema, validated criteria-based eczema, and parent-reported eczema do not necessarily have identical sensitivity or specificity. Incidence assessed at 12 months may also differ from cumulative incidence or point prevalence assessed closer to 3 years. Because the exact definitions and time points were not provided, the clinical comparability of the pooled outcomes cannot be verified.

The largest study accounted for most participants and had a standardized pipeline estimate close to zero. The two smaller studies had estimates in opposite directions. Although this pattern may help explain the heterogeneity, the native study-specific RRs and their weights were unavailable, precluding a detailed influence analysis. Similarly, the cumulative and distribution plots may aid visual assessment, but the numerical data supplied do not support stronger conclusions about temporal trends or influential studies.

From a practice perspective, the evidence supplied here does not support recommending prophylactic infant skin care solely to reduce eczema incidence by ages 1–3 years. This conclusion should be limited to preventive efficacy for eczema and should not be generalized to treatment of existing xerosis or eczema, comfort, skin integrity, infection, adverse skin reactions, caregiver burden, or cost, none of which were evaluated in the provided outcome set.

Future trials should use clearly specified formulations and regimens, document adherence and control-group contamination, prespecify validated eczema definitions, report event counts by randomized arm, and retain participants for standardized follow-up time points. Individual participant data could help determine whether effects vary by genetic or familial risk, skin-barrier phenotype, age at initiation, and adherence. Complete reporting of adverse events is also necessary because a preventive strategy applied to otherwise healthy infants must have a favorable safety profile.

## Limitations

1. **Small quantitative evidence base:** Only three studies contributed to the meta-analysis. Estimates of the pooled effect, heterogeneity, and publication bias are therefore unstable.
2. **Incomplete use of the identified evidence:** Six studies were represented in the pipeline, but only three supplied analyzable effect sizes. Reasons the other three did not contribute were not provided, creating uncertainty about potential availability or selection bias.
3. **Limited study descriptors:** Intervention composition, comparator care, participant risk, adherence, outcome definition, follow-up age, and setting were unavailable, preventing assessment of clinical comparability.
4. **No study-level risk-of-bias assessment:** Randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, missing data, selective reporting, and other bias domains could not be evaluated from the supplied data.
5. **Substantial heterogeneity:** I² was 68.01% and τ² was 0.085264. An average random-effects estimate may obscure differences among interventions or populations.
6. **Imprecision:** The 95% CI of 0.684–1.569 crossed the null and included potentially important effects in either direction.
7. **No defensible moderator analysis:** The small number of studies and absence of covariate data precluded investigation of heterogeneity.
8. **Publication-bias analyses were underpowered:** Egger’s test is not reliable for excluding small-study effects with k=3, and trim-and-fill may be distorted by heterogeneity.
9. **Scale mismatch in supplied outputs:** The principal synthesis used native RRs, whereas the study-level records and trim-and-fill output included standardized values without corresponding native study-level RRs. This prevented independent reconstruction and limits direct comparison across outputs.
10. **Review-process information was unavailable:** Search methods, screening decisions, protocol registration, and the PRISMA flow were not supplied; completeness of study identification cannot therefore be independently judged.

## Conclusion

In three studies involving 1,521 participants, prophylactic skin-care interventions during infancy did not produce a statistically significant reduction in eczema by 1–3 years of age. The REML random-effects pooled **Risk Ratio was 1.0362202513958838** (95% CI **0.684–1.569**; p=**0.8665371418734427**) and favored neither arm according to the supplied classification. Heterogeneity was substantial (**I²=68.01%; τ²=0.085264**), and publication-bias risk was considered moderate. The evidence is therefore insufficient to establish preventive benefit and remains compatible with clinically important effects in either direction. Better-characterized, adequately powered, and transparently reported trials are needed to identify whether particular formulations, regimens, or infant subgroups experience benefit or harm.
