# Iron-Fortified Condiments and Seasonings and Serum/Plasma Ferritin Concentration in Children and Adults: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Condiments and seasonings used daily in small, consistent amounts — soy sauce, fish sauce, curry powder and salt — have been proposed as efficient vehicles for iron fortification in populations with a high burden of iron deficiency. Serum or plasma ferritin is the most widely used indicator of body iron stores, and quantifying the magnitude of change achievable through condiment fortification is central to programme design.

**Objective.** To estimate the effect of iron-fortified condiments or seasonings, compared with the same condiment without added iron, on end-of-intervention serum or plasma ferritin concentration (µg/L) in children and adults.

**Methods.** Randomised controlled trials comparing an iron-fortified condiment/seasoning (alone, or with other micronutrients added equally to both arms) with the identical unfortified or placebo condiment were eligible. Ferritin was extracted as mean, SD and n per arm and harmonised to µg/L. Six records entered data extraction; five contributed analysable ferritin contrasts (n = 1,520 participants). Effects were pooled on the native mean-difference scale using a random-effects model with REML estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was 13.85 µg/L (95% CI 4.398 to 23.292; p = 0.0041; k = 5; n = 1,520), favouring the iron-fortified condiment arm. Heterogeneity was very high (Q = 57.067, p < 0.001; I² = 96.22%; τ² = 101.903, τ ≈ 10.1 µg/L), and the approximate 95% prediction interval spanned zero. Standardised study-level effects ranged from g = 0.442 (95% CI 0.241 to 0.643) to g = 1.415 (95% CI 1.254 to 1.576), all positive. Egger's test showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept 1.1388, p = 0.6646) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0); publication bias risk was rated low.

**Conclusions.** Iron-fortified condiments produce a statistically significant and, on average, substantial increase in ferritin relative to unfortified condiments, but the magnitude is extremely heterogeneous across trials and the pooled point estimate should not be read as a transportable expected effect for any single programme.

## 1. Introduction

Iron deficiency remains the most prevalent single-nutrient deficiency worldwide and the leading cause of anaemia, with the heaviest burden falling on young children, adolescent girls and women of reproductive age in low- and middle-income settings. Dietary diversification and supplementation programmes face well-documented barriers of cost, adherence and supply, which has sustained interest in food fortification as a population-level strategy that requires no behaviour change from the consumer.

Condiments and seasonings are an unusually attractive fortification vehicle. Soy sauce, fish sauce, curry powder and table salt are consumed almost universally within the cultures in which they are staples; they are consumed daily, in small and remarkably stable quantities; they are typically produced in a small number of centralised facilities, which simplifies quality control and regulatory oversight; and the amount consumed per person per day is largely independent of household income, so the vehicle reaches the poorest households as reliably as the wealthiest. The principal technical challenge is organoleptic: soluble iron compounds catalyse lipid oxidation and produce colour and flavour changes, which has driven the development of encapsulated and chelated iron forms (most prominently NaFeEDTA, ferrous sulphate with encapsulation, and ferric pyrophosphate) specifically for condiment matrices.

Serum or plasma ferritin is the most widely used biomarker of body iron stores and the outcome of choice for efficacy trials of iron interventions, because it responds to repletion of storage iron before haemoglobin does and is not confounded by the many non-iron determinants of haemoglobin concentration. It is, however, an acute-phase reactant, which complicates interpretation in populations with a high burden of infection and inflammation.

Previous syntheses of iron fortification have tended to pool across all food vehicles — flour, rice, milk, biscuits, condiments — which conflates vehicles with very different iron doses, bioavailability enhancers and consumption patterns. The present review focuses narrowly on condiment and seasoning vehicles, with the comparator fixed as the *same* condiment without added iron, so that the contrast isolates the effect of the added iron rather than of the vehicle or of co-administered micronutrients.

**Review question.** What is the effect of iron-fortified condiments or seasonings, compared with the same condiment without added iron, on serum or plasma ferritin concentration (µg/L) in children and adults?

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

| Element | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Children and/or adults, any age, any iron status at baseline |
| **Intervention** | An iron-fortified condiment or seasoning (e.g., fortified soy sauce, fish sauce, curry powder, salt), alone or with other micronutrients provided **equally to both arms** |
| **Comparator** | The *same* condiment or seasoning without added iron (unfortified or placebo condiment) |
| **Outcome** | Serum or plasma ferritin concentration at the end of the intervention (last reported follow-up), in µg/L |
| **Design** | Randomised controlled trials, including individually randomised and cluster-randomised (village, school, household, family) designs |

Trials in which the control arm received a different vehicle, no vehicle at all, iron tablets, or a nutrition-education-only intervention were not eligible as the comparator of record; where such arms existed within an otherwise eligible multi-arm trial, they were ignored and only the fortified-condiment and unfortified-condiment arms were carried forward.

### 2.2 Information sources and search

The search was conducted across the bibliographic corpus available to the review pipeline, targeting records describing randomised trials of iron-fortified condiments or seasonings reporting ferritin outcomes. Search concepts combined (i) vehicle terms (soy sauce, fish sauce, curry powder, salt, condiment, seasoning, double-fortified salt), (ii) intervention terms (iron, ferrous, ferric, NaFeEDTA, fortification, fortified), (iii) outcome terms (ferritin, iron status, iron stores, anaemia) and (iv) a randomised-trial design filter. Reference lists of retrieved records and relevant reviews were screened for additional candidates.

### 2.3 Study selection

Records were screened on title and abstract and then at full text against the eligibility criteria above. Reviews, commentaries, non-randomised evaluations and duplicate reports of the same trial were removed. Where multiple reports of a single trial were identified, only one report was retained to avoid double-counting participants.

### 2.4 Data extraction

For each eligible comparison the following were extracted: trial identifier, country/setting, population and age range, condiment vehicle, iron compound and daily iron dose, co-fortificants, randomisation unit, intervention duration, and the ferritin outcome as **mean, SD and n per arm**.

Pre-specified harmonisation rules were applied:

- **Units.** All values were converted to µg/L (= ng/mL; pmol/L × 0.445).
- **Change scores.** Where only change-from-baseline was reported, the mean change and SD of change were used for **both** arms, and the use of change scores was recorded. End values and change scores were never mixed across arms within a comparison.
- **Dispersion.** Where standard errors or SEMs were reported, SD was recovered as SD = SE × √n, after checking table footnotes to determine whether "±" denoted SD or SE.
- **Skewed/log-scale reporting.** Where ferritin was reported only as a geometric mean with confidence interval or on a log scale, arithmetic mean and SD were used if available; otherwise the geometric mean was recorded and the departure noted.
- **Cluster-randomised trials.** Both arms' sample sizes were divided by the design effect 1 + (average cluster size − 1) × ICC using the ICC reported by the trial. Where no ICC was reported, this was noted and the trial's own cluster-adjusted analysis was used if available.
- **Multi-arm trials.** Only the fortified-condiment and unfortified-condiment arms were included; multiple fortified-condiment arms were combined where present.

### 2.5 Effect measures and synthesis

The pre-specified and user-requested effect measure was the **Mean Difference (MD)** in ferritin (µg/L) between the iron-fortified condiment arm and the unfortified condiment control arm. **Native-scale pooling on the MD scale was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`; effect-measure source: explicit user request; no effect-measure conflict was detected). Standardised effect sizes (Hedges' *g*) were computed in parallel for each comparison to allow cross-study comparison of magnitude on a common, scale-free metric, and to support the small-study-effect diagnostics.

A positive mean difference indicates **higher serum/plasma ferritin in the iron-fortified condiment group** than in the unfortified condiment control group — i.e., better iron stores, favouring fortification.

Effects were pooled using a random-effects model with **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of the between-study variance τ². Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q, the I² statistic and τ² (with τ expressed in the native µg/L metric). An approximate 95% prediction interval was derived from the pooled estimate, τ² and the standard error of the pooled effect using a t-distribution with k − 1 degrees of freedom.

### 2.6 Small-study effects

Funnel plot asymmetry was assessed by Egger's regression test, and the Duval & Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure was applied to estimate the number of potentially missing studies (k₀) and the correspondingly adjusted effect. Both procedures are acknowledged to be underpowered with small k.

## 3. Search and Screening

The screening workflow identified **six studies** that passed full-text eligibility assessment and entered data extraction. Of these, **five** yielded an extractable ferritin contrast with mean, SD and n for both the fortified-condiment and unfortified-condiment arms and were carried into the quantitative synthesis; the sixth did not provide ferritin data in a form that could be converted to the required mean/SD/n per arm under the pre-specified harmonisation rules (for example, reporting restricted to a geometric mean without usable dispersion, or to a between-arm test statistic only) and therefore contributes to the narrative synthesis only.

The analysed evidence base therefore comprises **k = 5 effect sizes from 5 trials, with n = 1,520 participants** contributing to the pooled estimate.

**PRISMA flow (as realised in this review).**

| Stage | n |
|---|---|
| Studies passing full-text eligibility and entering extraction | 6 |
| Studies excluded at extraction for non-extractable ferritin outcome | 1 |
| Studies contributing effect sizes to the meta-analysis | 5 |
| Participants in the pooled analysis | 1,520 |

No eligible trial was excluded for reasons of language or publication date.

## 4. Included Studies

Five trials contributed the quantitative evidence. Each compared an iron-fortified condiment or seasoning against the identical condiment without added iron, with serum or plasma ferritin at the final follow-up as the extracted outcome. Study-level sample sizes contributing to the analysis ranged from **91 to 741 participants**, a greater than eight-fold spread that is reflected directly in the inverse-variance weighting of the pooled model.

|---|---|---|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | 91 | 0.732 | 0.307 to 1.157 | Favours fortified condiment |
| study_2_R2 | 741 | 1.415 | 1.254 to 1.576 | Favours fortified condiment |
| study_4_R4 | 136 | 1.069 | 0.709 to 1.429 | Favours fortified condiment |
| study_5_R5 | 163 | 0.763 | 0.445 to 1.081 | Favours fortified condiment |
| study_6_R6 | 389 | 0.442 | 0.241 to 0.643 | Favours fortified condiment |
| **Total** | **1,520** | — | — | — |

Two features of Table 1 are worth stating explicitly. First, **all five contrasts point in the same direction**: every 95% confidence interval for the standardised effect excludes zero and lies entirely above it. There is no trial in this evidence base in which the unfortified condiment arm achieved higher end-of-intervention ferritin. Second, the *magnitude* of the standardised effect varies more than three-fold across trials, from 0.442 to 1.415, and the confidence intervals of the largest (study_2_R2) and smallest (study_6_R6) effects do not overlap. This non-overlap of the two most precisely estimated contrasts is the proximate statistical source of the very high heterogeneity reported in Section 7.

Within the contrast labels, the numbering gap (studies 1, 2, 4, 5, 6) reflects the study excluded at the extraction stage for a non-extractable ferritin outcome, as described in Section 3.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Each contrast was expressed on the native mean-difference scale in µg/L, the pre-specified metric, with Hedges' *g* computed in parallel as a scale-free check. The standardised effects are shown in Table 1 and visualised in the forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) and in the effect-size distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`).

Interpreted against conventional benchmarks, the standardised effects span the range from **small-to-moderate** (g = 0.442) through **moderate-to-large** (g = 0.732, 0.763), **large** (g = 1.069) to **very large** (g = 1.415). The unweighted mean of the five standardised effects is approximately g = 0.88, which is consistent with the trim-and-fill reference value of 0.8907 reported in Section 8 — i.e., the standardised evidence base is centred on a large positive effect even before any weighting or bias adjustment.

Because the analysis was conducted and reported on the **native MD scale**, the headline statistic in Section 6 is expressed in µg/L of ferritin and is directly interpretable against clinical thresholds (for example, the conventional 15 µg/L cut-off for depleted iron stores in adults and the 12 µg/L cut-off commonly applied in children under five). Readers should note that the standardised *g* values in Table 1 and the native MD in Section 6 are two different metrics applied to the same data and are not numerically interchangeable; the relationship between them depends on each trial's within-group ferritin SD, which varies substantially across these populations.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Results

A single pre-specified outcome group was analysed — end-of-intervention serum/plasma ferritin concentration in µg/L — so no separation into multiple outcome or timepoint groups was required, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data for pooling.

**Table 2. Random-effects (REML) pooled result.**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Mean Difference (MD)**, µg/L |
| Native-scale pooling used | Yes |
| Pooled MD | **13.845 µg/L** |
| 95% confidence interval | **4.398 to 23.292 µg/L** |
| p-value | **0.0041** |
| Studies (k) | 5 |
| Participants (n) | 1,520 |
| Between-study variance τ² | 101.903 (τ ≈ 10.09 µg/L) |
| I² | 96.22% |
| Cochran's Q (df = 4) | 57.067 (p < 0.001) |
| Estimation method | REML |
| **Favours** | **Iron-fortified condiment/seasoning (fortified soy sauce, fish sauce, curry powder, salt), alone or with other micronutrients added equally to both arms** |

The pooled **Mean Difference (MD)** is **13.845 µg/L (95% CI 4.398 to 23.292; p = 0.0041)**. Per the model's direction field, this result **favours the iron-fortified condiment/seasoning arm**: participants receiving the iron-fortified condiment ended the intervention with higher serum/plasma ferritin — that is, better iron stores — than those receiving the same condiment without added iron. The effect measure was explicitly requested and confirmed, and pooling was performed on the native MD scale, so this is a genuine native-scale mean difference in µg/L and not a standardised fallback.

The clinical interpretation of a ~14 µg/L mean increment is not trivial. It is of the same order as the entire diagnostic threshold for depleted iron stores (15 µg/L in adults, 12 µg/L in children under five). In a population whose mean ferritin sits just below the deficiency cut-off, an increment of this size would be sufficient, on average, to move the distribution across the threshold — which is consistent with the consistently positive direction of every individual trial in Table 1.

However, the confidence interval is wide, spanning from a modest 4.4 µg/L to a very large 23.3 µg/L. The lower bound alone would represent a worthwhile but much less dramatic programme effect. Furthermore, given τ² = 101.903, the approximate **95% prediction interval is roughly −17 to +45 µg/L**, which includes zero. The pooled estimate therefore establishes that, averaged over these trials, iron-fortified condiments raise ferritin — but it does *not* support a confident prediction that any particular new trial or programme, in a new setting with a different vehicle, iron compound, dose and baseline iron status, will produce an increase of approximately 14 µg/L.

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows how the pooled estimate evolved as studies were accumulated; the stability of the direction, alongside the drift in magnitude as the larger trials enter, reinforces the reading that the *sign* of the effect is robust while the *size* is not well pinned down.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity in this evidence base is **very high and statistically unambiguous**:

- **Cochran's Q = 57.067** on 4 degrees of freedom, **p < 0.001** — the hypothesis of a common underlying effect is decisively rejected.
- **I² = 96.22%** — approximately 96% of the observed variability in effect estimates reflects genuine between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. This is at the extreme upper end of the conventional "considerable heterogeneity" band (≥75%).
- **τ² = 101.903**, i.e. a between-study standard deviation of τ ≈ **10.09 µg/L**. In native units, the typical trial-to-trial dispersion of the true effect is roughly three-quarters of the magnitude of the pooled effect itself.

The heterogeneity summary figure (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`) displays these diagnostics graphically.

Crucially, this is **heterogeneity of magnitude, not of direction**. All five effects are positive and all five confidence intervals exclude the null. The dispersion arises because some trials achieved a modest repletion of iron stores while others achieved a very large one, not because some trials found harm. For a decision-maker, this distinction matters: the qualitative conclusion ("fortifying the condiment with iron improves iron stores") is robust across every included trial, while the quantitative conclusion ("by about 14 µg/L") is not transportable.

Plausible sources of this dispersion, all of which are well established in the fortification literature, include:

1. **Iron compound and bioavailability.** NaFeEDTA, encapsulated ferrous sulphate and ferric pyrophosphate differ severalfold in absorption from a given matrix, particularly in diets high in phytate and polyphenols.
2. **Daily iron dose delivered.** This is a product of the fortificant concentration and the habitual consumption of the condiment, which differs markedly between, say, fish sauce and curry powder.
3. **Baseline iron status.** Ferritin responses to iron are strongly regression-dependent on baseline stores: iron-depleted populations absorb more and gain more, so trials recruiting severely deficient participants will show larger increments.
4. **Intervention duration.** Storage iron accrues progressively, so trials with longer follow-up accumulate larger between-arm differences.
5. **Inflammation burden.** Ferritin is an acute-phase reactant; populations with high infection prevalence show elevated and noisier ferritin values, and trials differ in whether they adjusted for CRP/AGP.
6. **Age group and physiological demand.** Growing children and menstruating women have markedly different iron requirements and therefore different repletion trajectories.
7. **Methodological harmonisation.** The analysis set includes contrasts that may have been derived from change scores rather than end values, from SE-to-SD conversions, or from cluster-adjusted sample sizes, each of which affects the estimated variance and hence the study's weight.

Given k = 5, the τ² estimate is itself imprecise, and the REML point estimate of between-study variance should be treated as an order-of-magnitude indication rather than a precise quantity.

---

## 8. Moderator Analyses

With only **five** contributing contrasts and I² = 96.22%, formal moderator analysis is severely constrained. Conventional guidance recommends at least ten studies per covariate for meta-regression; with k = 5, any single-covariate meta-regression would have essentially no power to distinguish a real moderator from noise, and any apparently significant coefficient would carry a high false-positive probability. **No formal meta-regression or subgroup analysis was therefore conducted, and none should be inferred from this report.**

An exploratory bubble plot relating effect size to study precision/size was generated (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) and is presented for descriptive purposes only. Visual inspection shows no monotonic relationship between sample size and effect magnitude: the largest trial (n = 741) produced the *largest* standardised effect (g = 1.415) while the second-largest (n = 389) produced the *smallest* (g = 0.442), and the smallest trial (n = 91) sits in the middle of the range (g = 0.732). This pattern is the opposite of the classic small-study-effect signature, in which small trials show inflated effects, and it is consistent with the formal bias diagnostics reported in Section 9.

The substantive moderators most likely to explain the heterogeneity — iron compound, delivered daily iron dose, condiment vehicle, baseline ferritin, intervention duration, age group and inflammation adjustment — are precisely those that cannot be tested with five studies. Identifying them is the principal priority for future research in this area, and would require either a substantially expanded trial base or an individual-participant-data synthesis.

## 9. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

**Table 3. Small-study-effect diagnostics.**

| Test | Statistic | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 1.1388 | Small, near-null asymmetry |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.6646** | No significant funnel asymmetry |
| Trim-and-fill, studies imputed (k₀) | **0** | No studies imputed |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted effect (standardised scale) | 0.8907 | Pooled estimate unchanged by adjustment |
| Overall risk rating | **Low** | — |

Egger's regression test shows **no significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = 1.1388, p = 0.6646)**, and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed **no missing studies (k₀ = 0)**, leaving the adjusted estimate equal to the unadjusted one (the reported adjusted value of 0.8907 is expressed on the standardised scale and corresponds to the unchanged, unadjusted pooling). Together these results provide no indication of small-study effects or publication bias, consistent with the overall **"low risk"** rating assigned by the pipeline.

This conclusion must nonetheless be heavily qualified. With **k = 5**, Egger's test has very low statistical power — it is generally considered uninformative below ten studies — and trim-and-fill performs poorly, often imputing zero studies, when between-study heterogeneity is as extreme as it is here (I² = 96.22%). A non-significant asymmetry test under these conditions is **the absence of evidence of bias, not evidence of the absence of bias**. Selective publication or selective outcome reporting of null condiment-fortification trials cannot be excluded on the strength of these diagnostics. The uniformly positive and uniformly significant direction of all five included contrasts (Table 1) is itself a pattern that would be expected both from a genuinely efficacious intervention *and* from a literature in which null results were under-reported, and the available tests cannot distinguish between those two explanations.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis of five randomised comparisons in 1,520 children and adults found that iron-fortified condiments and seasonings increased end-of-intervention serum or plasma ferritin by a pooled Mean Difference of **13.845 µg/L (95% CI 4.398 to 23.292; p = 0.0041)** relative to the identical condiment without added iron, a result that **favours the iron-fortified condiment arm**. Because the comparator in every included trial was the *same* vehicle minus the iron, and because any co-administered micronutrients were required to be delivered equally to both arms, this contrast isolates the effect of the added iron rather than of the vehicle, of the delivery platform, or of general micronutrient supplementation.

Three findings stand out.

**First, the direction of effect is completely consistent.** All five contrasts favour fortification, and all five standardised confidence intervals exclude the null (Table 1; forest plot at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`). For a biomarker as sensitive to population context as ferritin, this unanimity across different vehicles, settings and age groups is a strong qualitative signal that condiment fortification delivers absorbable iron.

**Second, the magnitude is both large and highly uncertain.** A ~14 µg/L average increment is comparable to the entire diagnostic threshold for iron depletion, which suggests that well-designed condiment fortification can plausibly shift a substantial fraction of a deficient population above the cut-off. But τ ≈ 10.1 µg/L and a prediction interval spanning roughly −17 to +45 µg/L mean that the *expected* effect in a new setting is not well determined by this evidence base. Programme planners should treat 13.8 µg/L as the average of a very wide distribution of achievable effects, not as a design target.

**Third, the biological plausibility of the heterogeneity is high, which is somewhat reassuring.** The dispersion is not mysterious: iron compound, delivered dose, duration, baseline deficiency and inflammation burden are all known, strong modifiers of ferritin response, and the included trials necessarily differ on all of them. This makes it more likely that the heterogeneity reflects real variation in intervention delivery and population responsiveness than that it reflects methodological artefact — although with k = 5 this remains an interpretation rather than a demonstrated conclusion.

These results are broadly concordant with the wider iron-fortification literature, which has repeatedly shown that NaFeEDTA-fortified soy and fish sauces and double-fortified salt improve iron status in deficient populations, and with the observation that ferritin responds more strongly and more reliably than haemoglobin in such trials. The present synthesis adds value by restricting the comparator to the matched unfortified condiment, thereby removing vehicle and co-micronutrient confounding, and by reporting on the clinically interpretable native µg/L scale rather than only in standardised units.

**Implications for practice.** The evidence supports iron fortification of centrally produced condiments as an efficacious means of improving iron stores where condiment consumption is habitual and iron deficiency is prevalent. Programmes should nonetheless specify the iron compound and the delivered daily iron dose explicitly, monitor the biomarker response locally rather than assuming the pooled effect, and interpret ferritin alongside inflammation markers.

**Implications for research.** The dominant need is not for more trials demonstrating that condiment fortification works, but for trials and syntheses capable of explaining *why the effect varies so much*. Priorities are: head-to-head comparisons of iron compounds within the same vehicle and population; dose–response designs; consistent reporting of delivered daily iron intake from the condiment; universal CRP/AGP adjustment of ferritin; full reporting of ICCs in cluster-randomised designs; and reporting of arithmetic means with SDs alongside geometric means so that trials are not lost from quantitative synthesis.

## 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No formal validation flags were returned by the analysis pipeline for this review. For transparency, the following data-handling points nonetheless warrant plain statement:

- **Six studies entered extraction but only five contributed effect sizes.** One study that satisfied the eligibility criteria could not be included in the pooled analysis because its ferritin outcome was not reported in a form convertible to mean, SD and n per arm under the pre-specified rules. Its direction of effect therefore does not contribute to, and cannot be checked against, the pooled estimate.
- **Mixed reporting formats were harmonised.** Where trials reported SE/SEM rather than SD, change scores rather than end values, or clustered designs, the pre-specified conversions (SD = SE × √n; change scores applied to both arms; sample sizes divided by the design effect) were applied. These conversions introduce approximation into the study-level variances and hence into the inverse-variance weights.
- **Effect measure.** The Mean Difference was explicitly requested, was confirmed, and **native-scale pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`). The pooled 13.845 µg/L is therefore a true native-scale mean difference, not a standardised fallback. The Hedges' *g* values reported in Table 1 are a parallel, supplementary metric; the trim-and-fill value of 0.8907 is also on the standardised scale and should not be confused with the native MD.
- **No outcome or timepoint subgrouping was required.** A single outcome group (end-of-intervention ferritin, µg/L) was analysed, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data to pool.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small number of studies (k = 5).** This limits the precision of τ², renders meta-regression and subgroup analysis infeasible, and leaves the publication-bias diagnostics severely underpowered.
2. **Extreme heterogeneity (I² = 96.22%).** The pooled mean difference summarises a distribution of true effects so wide that its prediction interval includes zero. The pooled point estimate should not be used as a planning value for any individual programme.
3. **Unexplained heterogeneity.** The moderators most likely to account for the dispersion — iron compound, delivered dose, vehicle, duration, baseline ferritin, inflammation — could not be formally examined.
4. **Ferritin as an acute-phase reactant.** In populations with high infection burden, ferritin can be elevated independently of iron stores. Trials differed in whether inflammation adjustment was applied, and this review could not standardise that adjustment.
5. **One eligible study excluded from pooling.** The loss of a trial for reporting-format reasons introduces a form of availability bias that is independent of, and not detectable by, the funnel-based diagnostics.
6. **Approximation in data harmonisation.** SE-to-SD conversion, use of change scores, geometric-mean substitution where unavoidable, and design-effect adjustment (including cases where no ICC was reported) each add uncertainty not reflected in the nominal confidence intervals.
7. **Risk of bias in included trials not formally tabulated here.** Blinding of a fortified versus unfortified condiment is feasible but imperfect, given possible colour and flavour differences from iron compounds; differential attrition and laboratory-assay differences across trials are further potential sources of bias.
8. **Generalisability.** Findings apply to settings where the relevant condiment is habitually consumed in stable amounts and where iron deficiency is prevalent; the effect in iron-replete populations is likely to be substantially smaller, since ferritin responses attenuate as stores fill.

---

## 13. Conclusion

Across five randomised trials in 1,520 children and adults, iron-fortified condiments and seasonings increased end-of-intervention serum or plasma ferritin by a pooled **Mean Difference of 13.845 µg/L (95% CI 4.398 to 23.292; p = 0.0041)** compared with the same condiment without added iron, a result that **favours the iron-fortified condiment arm**. Every individual trial pointed in the same direction, and no evidence of publication bias was detected (Egger's p = 0.6646; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0), though these tests are weak with five studies. Heterogeneity was, however, extreme (I² = 96.22%; τ² = 101.903; Q = 57.067, p < 0.001), and the prediction interval spans zero.

The defensible conclusion is therefore **qualitative and robust, quantitative and fragile**: iron fortification of condiments reliably improves iron stores relative to the unfortified vehicle, and is a credible population-level strategy where condiment consumption is habitual and deficiency prevalent — but the size of the benefit varies several-fold across settings, and the pooled 13.8 µg/L figure should be read as the centre of a wide distribution rather than as an expected effect for any new programme. Resolving which of iron compound, delivered dose, duration and baseline iron status drives that variation is the key outstanding question.
