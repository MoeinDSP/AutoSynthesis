# Effect of Iron-Fortified Condiments or Seasonings on Serum or Plasma Ferritin: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Iron fortification of commonly consumed condiments and seasonings may increase iron stores at the population level. This review evaluated serum or plasma ferritin after consumption of an iron-fortified condiment or seasoning versus the otherwise identical product without added iron.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons included children or adults receiving an iron-fortified condiment or seasoning, with iron alone or with other micronutrients supplied equally in both arms. The outcome was serum or plasma ferritin at the last reported follow-up, expressed in µg/L. The supplied evidence set contained six studies; four contributed effect sizes to the quantitative synthesis. A random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled the native-scale mean difference (MD), defined as fortified minus unfortified. Statistical heterogeneity and small-study effects were assessed with Cochran’s Q, I², τ², Egger’s test, and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Four comparisons encompassing 856 participants were pooled. The pooled mean difference was 16.7292239938683 µg/L (95% CI 5.556 to 27.902; p = 0.0033391283140053445), favoring the iron-fortified condiment or seasoning. Heterogeneity was considerable (Q = 14.062, p = 0.0028; I² = 90.64%; τ² = 107.473896 [µg/L]²). Individually reported standardized effects were positive in all four analyzed comparisons, ranging from 0.441848 to 0.732095. Egger’s intercept was 0.2135 (p = 0.1717), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0), indicating no statistically significant evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry, although only four effects were available.

**Conclusions:** Iron-fortified condiments or seasonings increased end-of-intervention ferritin by an estimated 16.73 µg/L relative to otherwise identical unfortified products. The estimate was statistically significant but accompanied by substantial between-study heterogeneity. Limited study-level reporting, the omission of two identified studies from pooling, and the very small number of effects constrain certainty and preclude reliable moderator or publication-bias conclusions.

## Introduction

Iron deficiency remains an important nutritional problem among both children and adults. Fortifying condiments or seasonings can potentially improve iron intake without requiring major dietary change, particularly where the selected vehicle is consumed regularly. Ferritin reflects body iron stores, although it is also an acute-phase reactant and may be increased by inflammation or infection.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among children and adults, what is the effect of consuming an iron-fortified condiment or seasoning, compared with the same condiment or seasoning without added iron, on serum or plasma ferritin concentration at the end of the intervention?

The prespecified direction was treatment minus control: a positive mean difference indicates a higher ferritin concentration with the iron-fortified product and favors fortification. Outcomes outside this question—including hemoglobin, anemia prevalence, iron-deficiency status, soluble transferrin receptor, body iron, growth, and adverse effects—were not considered.

## Meta-Analytic Result

Using a REML random-effects model, the **pooled mean difference** was **16.7292239938683 µg/L** (95% CI **5.556 to 27.902**; p = **0.0033391283140053445**), based on four comparisons and 856 participants.

The analysis-designated favored arm was the **iron-fortified condiment or seasoning**. Thus, on average, ferritin at the end of intervention was approximately 16.73 µg/L higher among participants assigned to an iron-fortified condiment or seasoning than among those receiving the otherwise identical unfortified product. The confidence interval excluded no effect, and the test of the pooled effect was statistically significant.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- Cochran’s Q = **14.062** with p = **0.0028**;
- I² = **90.64%**;
- τ² = **107.473896 (µg/L)²**.

The significant Q test and high I² indicate that most of the observed dispersion was attributable to between-study differences rather than sampling error alone. The estimated between-study variance was also large on the native ferritin scale. Accordingly, the pooled mean difference represents an average across heterogeneous comparisons and should not be assumed to apply uniformly to every population or fortification program.

Potential clinical or methodological sources include differences in age, baseline iron status, infection or inflammation, ferritin measurement and adjustment procedures, condiment vehicle, iron compound and dose, exposure duration, adherence, and study quality. None could be evaluated with the supplied study-level data. A heterogeneity summary figure is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s intercept was **0.2135**, with p = **0.1717**. Thus, there was no statistically significant evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. The trim-and-fill procedure estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies. The supplied trim-and-fill output also reported a value labeled `trim_fill_g = 0.5629`; because this is presented on a standardized-g scale rather than the native MD scale of the principal analysis, it should not be interpreted as an adjusted ferritin difference in µg/L or substituted for the pooled MD.

Taken together, the supplied classification was a low risk of publication bias. This conclusion must be cautious: asymmetry tests and trim-and-fill are unreliable with only four studies, and absence of detected asymmetry is not evidence that selective publication or selective outcome reporting is absent. ## Discussion

### Principal findings

This meta-analysis found that consuming an iron-fortified condiment or seasoning increased end-of-intervention serum or plasma ferritin relative to consuming the same product without added iron. The pooled mean difference was 16.7292239938683 µg/L, and its 95% confidence interval of 5.556 to 27.902 µg/L excluded no effect. All four reported standardized study estimates were positive, supporting consistency in effect direction.

The absolute increase may be nutritionally meaningful, but clinical interpretation depends on baseline ferritin, the prevalence of iron deficiency, age and sex, inflammatory status, and the ferritin thresholds relevant to the target population. Those data were not available. Moreover, ferritin rises during inflammation; without information on inflammatory biomarkers or correction procedures, part of an observed increase could theoretically reflect factors other than improved iron stores.

### Interpretation of heterogeneity

The principal qualification is the very high heterogeneity (I² = 90.64%). This indicates marked variation in effect magnitude across studies, even though the standardized estimates all favored fortification. A random-effects model appropriately allows true effects to differ, but it does not explain those differences. The absence of study-level characteristics precluded investigation of whether effects were larger among participants with lower baseline iron stores, with specific iron compounds or condiment vehicles, or after longer exposure.

No prediction interval was supplied. Therefore, the likely range of effects in a new setting cannot be stated exactly. In view of the high heterogeneity, decision-makers should consider the pooled estimate as an average effect and assess the similarity of local populations and fortification programs to the included evidence before extrapolating it.

### Implications

The findings support iron fortification of commonly consumed condiments or seasonings as a potentially effective strategy for increasing ferritin. Nevertheless, implementation decisions require additional considerations outside this review’s outcome scope, including consumption distribution, iron bioavailability, organoleptic stability, manufacturing quality, cost, safety, and monitoring. Those outcomes were neither synthesized nor inferred here.

Future trials should report arithmetic ferritin means and SDs by randomized arm at baseline and end of intervention, numbers randomized and analyzed, attrition, assay methods, original measurement units, inflammation markers, and whether ferritin values were adjusted or excluded according to infection status. Prospective protocols, complete reporting, and sufficient detail to identify independent cohorts would strengthen subsequent syntheses.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

No formal `validation_flags` field was supplied. Nevertheless, several limitations of the analysis record require explicit note:

1. Six studies were counted in the pipeline, but only four effect sizes were available for pooling; reasons for the two omissions were not reported.
2. The native-scale pooled result was a Mean Difference, while the displayed study-level effects were Hedges’ g. Native study-level MDs were unavailable, preventing independent reconstruction of the pooled estimate.
3. Arm-level arithmetic means, SDs, sample sizes, original units, unit conversions, and use of end values versus change scores were not provided for validation.
4. The trim-and-fill output included a standardized value (`trim_fill_g = 0.5629`) that is not directly comparable with the principal pooled MD in µg/L.
5. Study reports and cohort identifiers were unavailable, so independence of all pooled comparisons and avoidance of duplicate populations could not be independently verified.

## Limitations

First, only four studies contributed to the meta-analysis. This limited the precision of heterogeneity estimates, made publication-bias tests underpowered, and precluded credible subgroup analysis or meta-regression.

Second, study characteristics and risk-of-bias assessments were absent. The internal validity of the individual studies and the certainty of the pooled estimate therefore could not be formally graded. In particular, randomization methods, allocation concealment, masking, missing data, selective reporting, and deviations from intended interventions could not be assessed.

Third, the high heterogeneity reduces the generalizability of the average effect. Its sources remain unresolved, and no prediction interval was available.

Fourth, the supplied record did not include raw arm-level outcome data. Unit conversion, arithmetic versus geometric reporting, conversion from SE to SD, consistency of end values or change scores across arms, and handling of skewed ferritin distributions could not be verified.

Fifth, two of six studies did not contribute effect sizes, and the reasons were not documented. If their omission depended on the direction or magnitude of findings, the pooled estimate could be biased.

Finally, ferritin is sensitive to inflammation. Without information on inflammatory status, assay procedures, or adjustment methods, the extent to which changes represent iron stores alone is uncertain.

## Conclusion

Across four comparisons involving 856 participants, iron-fortified condiments or seasonings produced a statistically significant increase in end-of-intervention serum or plasma ferritin compared with otherwise identical unfortified products. The REML random-effects pooled mean difference was 16.7292239938683 µg/L (95% CI 5.556 to 27.902; p = 0.0033391283140053445), favoring fortification. However, heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 90.64%), and the small evidence base, unavailable study-level characteristics, incomplete extraction audit trail, and unexplained omission of two identified studies limit certainty. The result supports efficacy on average but should be applied cautiously to specific populations and fortification programs.
