# Remotely Delivered Cognitive Behavioural Therapy for Functional Disability in Adults With Chronic Non-Headache Pain: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

## Abstract

## Abstract

**Background:** Remotely delivered cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) may improve functioning while reducing barriers to psychological treatment for adults with chronic pain.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of internet-, telephone-, app-, or video-delivered CBT—including acceptance and commitment therapy and exposure-based variants with a cognitive-behavioural basis—on functional disability or physical functioning at the first assessment after treatment, compared with treatment as usual, usual care, waiting list, or usual care plus waiting list.

**Methods:** The supplied review dataset contained 14 studies, of which 11 contributed one eligible post-treatment effect size each. Disability-specific measures were preferred over general physical-function subscales. Effects were expressed as Hedges' *g* under the published treatment-minus-control convention, for which *g* < 0 denotes less disability or better physical functioning and favors remote CBT. A random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation was used.

**Results:** Eleven comparisons involving 1,398 participants were pooled. The pooled Hedges' *g* was −0.29684020230133307 (95% CI [−0.532, −0.062], *p* = 0.013256785675496405), favoring remotely delivered CBT. Heterogeneity was substantial: *Q* = 43.872, *I*² = 78.45%, and τ² = 0.120657; the heterogeneity-test probability was recorded as 0.0 (i.e., *p* < 0.001). Individual effects ranged from −0.902799 to 0.252682. Egger's intercept was 0.4411 (*p* = 0.167), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (*k*₀ = 0); the trim-and-fill estimate therefore remained −0.2968. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as low.

**Conclusions:** At the first post-treatment assessment, remotely delivered CBT produced a statistically significant improvement in disability or physical functioning relative to eligible control conditions, with an effect of modest standardized magnitude. Substantial between-study heterogeneity limits the certainty with which this average can be generalized to a particular pain population, intervention format, or disability instrument. The absence of detected funnel-plot asymmetry does not exclude reporting or publication bias.

## Introduction

Chronic non-headache pain commonly restricts mobility, work, self-care, social participation, and other aspects of daily functioning. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) seeks to reduce pain-related disability by changing maladaptive cognitions and behaviours, increasing adaptive coping, and supporting graded engagement in meaningful activity. Internet, telephone, mobile-app, and video delivery may improve treatment access for people who face geographical, mobility, scheduling, or service-capacity barriers. Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT) and exposure-based interventions can also be considered part of this treatment family when they have an explicit cognitive-behavioural basis.

This review addressed the following question: among adults with chronic non-headache pain, what is the effect of remotely delivered CBT, compared with treatment as usual, usual care, waiting list, or usual care plus waiting list, on functional disability or physical functioning at the first assessment after treatment ends? The analysis was deliberately restricted to one functional outcome per trial at the initial post-treatment assessment. It did not pool later follow-up findings or outcomes such as pain intensity, pain improvement, general quality of life, mood, anxiety, or adverse events.

## Search/Screening

The supplied pipeline identified 14 studies. Eleven studies contributed eligible and analysable effect sizes to the quantitative synthesis; consequently, three identified studies did not contribute to the pooled estimate. No study-level reasons for the absence of those three effect sizes were included in the supplied results, so they cannot be classified more specifically without risking unsupported inference.

Eligibility was defined by the following review criteria:

- **Population:** adults with chronic non-headache pain.
- **Intervention:** remotely delivered CBT through the internet, telephone, an app, or video, including cognitive-behaviourally based ACT or exposure variants.
- **Comparator:** treatment as usual/usual care, waiting list, or usual care plus waiting list.
- **Outcome:** functional disability or physical functioning at the first assessment after treatment ended, typically 3–24 weeks after randomisation.
- **Outcome hierarchy:** a disability-specific measure—such as the Roland–Morris Disability Questionnaire, Pain Disability Index, Oswestry Disability Index, or WHO Disability Assessment Schedule—was preferred. A general physical-function subscale, such as one from the SF-36 or SF-12, was eligible only if no disability-specific measure was available.
- **Multiplicity rule:** one eligible functional outcome was selected per trial.

Search dates, databases, full search strategies, deduplication counts, numbers screened at title/abstract and full-text stages, exclusion reasons, language restrictions, protocol registration, reviewer procedures, and a conventional PRISMA flow diagram were not supplied. Accordingly, those details cannot be reconstructed or reported reliably.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model estimated with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled the 11 eligible Hedges' *g* values. The pooled Hedges' *g* was **−0.29684020230133307** (95% CI **[−0.532, −0.062]**; *p* = **0.013256785675496405**; *k* = **11**; total *n* = **1,398**). According to the prespecified direction and the supplied `favors` field, this result favors remotely delivered cognitive behavioural therapy.

The estimate indicates that, on average, the remote-CBT groups had approximately 0.30 standard deviations less disability or better physical functioning than control groups at the first assessment after treatment. Because standardized effects combine instruments with potentially different constructs and scaling properties, this estimate should be interpreted as a cross-measure standardized contrast rather than as a change in points on any single disability scale.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- Cochran's *Q* = **43.872** with 10 degrees of freedom;
- heterogeneity-test *p* recorded as **0.0**, conventionally reported as *p* < 0.001;
- *I*² = **78.45%**;
- τ² = **0.120657** on the Hedges' *g* scale.

The *I*² value indicates that a large proportion of the observed dispersion was attributable to between-study differences rather than sampling error alone. The τ² estimate likewise indicates meaningful dispersion in underlying effects. This heterogeneity makes the pooled effect an average across differing study circumstances rather than a single effect expected to apply uniformly.

Plausible sources include variation in chronic pain diagnosis, baseline disability, remote delivery channel, intervention content, therapist guidance, dose and duration, comparator intensity, disability instrument, and post-treatment timing. These explanations remain hypotheses because the necessary study-level covariates were not supplied. A heterogeneity summary is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## Publication Bias

Egger's regression yielded an intercept of **0.4411** with *p* = **0.167**. This test did not provide statistically significant evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **zero** missing studies (*k*₀ = **0**), leaving the trim-and-fill pooled estimate unchanged at **−0.2968**. The supplied assessment consequently classified publication-bias risk as **low**.

This finding should not be read as proof that bias was absent. With only 11 comparisons, tests of asymmetry have limited power, and substantial heterogeneity can complicate their interpretation. Selective outcome reporting also cannot be assessed from Egger's test or trim-and-fill alone.

## Discussion

This synthesis found that remotely delivered CBT improved functional disability or physical functioning at the first post-treatment assessment compared with usual-care or waiting-list controls. The pooled Hedges' *g* of −0.29684020230133307 was statistically distinguishable from no effect and corresponds to a modest average standardized benefit. The direction of benefit is clinically relevant because the review prioritized disability and functioning rather than symptom intensity alone.

The result supports remote cognitive-behavioural delivery as a potentially useful means of improving day-to-day functioning in adults with chronic non-headache pain. Remote formats may reduce access barriers, but this meta-analysis does not establish which technology, amount of therapist support, or cognitive-behavioural variant is most effective. It also does not show whether the post-treatment improvement persists, because later follow-up assessments were intentionally excluded from the target outcome.

The substantial heterogeneity is central to interpretation. Some trials reported moderate-to-large benefits, whereas others were near null or had point estimates favoring control. Differences in intervention intensity, comparator type, populations, measurement instruments, and timing could all contribute. Because moderator data were unavailable, the pooled estimate should not be assumed to represent every implementation context.

The analysis focused narrowly on the prespecified functional endpoint. It therefore should not be generalized to pain severity, emotional distress, quality of life as a whole, safety, treatment adherence, health-service use, or cost-effectiveness. Nor does statistical significance alone establish that the average benefit exceeded a clinically important threshold on each underlying instrument.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

No formal `validation_flags` object was included in the supplied context. Nevertheless, several data constraints are explicit in the results:

1. The pipeline contained 14 studies, but only 11 effect sizes were available for pooling; reasons for non-contribution by the other three studies were not supplied.
2. Study records were anonymized, preventing linkage to complete bibliographic citations and detailed trial characteristics.
3. Arm-level post-treatment means, standard deviations, and sample sizes were not supplied, so the standardized effects could not be independently recalculated.
4. No risk-of-bias assessments, outcome-instrument names, exact assessment times, or moderator variables were provided.
5. The heterogeneity-test probability was stored as 0.0; because a probability is not literally zero, it is reported inferentially as *p* < 0.001.

## Limitations

First, the search and screening methods were incompletely documented in the supplied material. Databases, dates, complete search strategies, reviewer procedures, and exclusion reasons were unavailable, limiting appraisal of review completeness and reproducibility.

Second, three of the 14 pipeline studies did not contribute to the synthesis, but the reasons were not reported. Their omission could matter if data availability was associated with the magnitude or direction of results.

Third, risk-of-bias judgments were not provided. The pooled estimate therefore cannot be qualified according to randomization, allocation concealment, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, selective reporting, or deviations from intended treatment. Blinding participants to psychological treatment is generally difficult, making outcome assessment and attrition especially important.

Fourth, substantial heterogeneity (*I*² = 78.45%) reduces confidence in applying the average effect to any specific clinical setting. No adequately documented subgroup analysis or meta-regression was available to explain that heterogeneity.

Fifth, Hedges' *g* facilitates pooling across different scales but is less directly interpretable than a mean difference on a familiar disability instrument. No minimally important difference or back-translation was supplied, so clinical importance cannot be established directly.

Sixth, only the initial post-treatment endpoint was included. This matches the research question but leaves durability unknown. The synthesis also does not address adverse events or other benefits and harms.

Finally, publication-bias analyses involved only 11 effects. Egger's test and trim-and-fill found no indication of missing-study effects, but both approaches have limited sensitivity and rely on assumptions that may be challenged by heterogeneous interventions and populations.

## Conclusion

Among adults with chronic non-headache pain, remotely delivered CBT was associated with better physical functioning or less functional disability than treatment as usual, usual care, waiting list, or usual care plus waiting list at the first post-treatment assessment. The REML random-effects pooled Hedges' *g* was −0.29684020230133307 (95% CI [−0.532, −0.062]; *p* = 0.013256785675496405), favoring remote CBT. The average effect was modest, and heterogeneity was substantial (*I*² = 78.45%; τ² = 0.120657). No clear publication-bias signal was detected, but the small number of studies and missing methodological detail require caution. Better-reported trials and individual-study data are needed to determine which remote formats work best, for whom, and whether functional gains persist.
