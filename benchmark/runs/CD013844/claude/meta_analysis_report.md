# Indicated Suicide-Prevention Interventions Delivered in Education Settings and Postintervention Suicidal Ideation in Young People Aged ≤25: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Young people who already report suicidal ideation or a history of self-harm constitute a high-risk group for whom "indicated" prevention is recommended. Schools, colleges and universities are a natural platform for reaching them, but the magnitude of benefit of indicated interventions delivered in these settings on suicidal ideation at the immediate end of treatment has not been quantified precisely.

**Objective.** To estimate the pooled effect of indicated suicide-prevention interventions, delivered in education settings to young people up to age 25 showing early signs or symptoms of suicide risk, versus control conditions, on suicidal ideation measured at postintervention only.

**Methods.** A systematic search and structured screening pipeline identified 10 eligible study records, of which 8 provided extractable postintervention suicidal-ideation data suitable for quantitative synthesis (N = 511 participants). Outcomes were measured with validated self-report or interview instruments (e.g. SIQ/SIQ-JR, BSS/BSSI, C-SSRS ideation items, PANSI, SBQ ideation items, or ideation subscales/items of broader instruments). Each indicated-intervention arm of a multi-arm trial contributed one row against the shared control group. Effect sizes were expressed as Standardized Mean Differences (Hedges' g-corrected SMD) and pooled with a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was assessed by Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** The pooled effect was an SMD of −0.335 (95% CI −0.539 to −0.132; p = 0.0013; k = 8; N = 511). In the raw treatment-minus-control sign convention used here, lower ideation scores indicate better outcome, so this estimate **favours the indicated intervention** over the control condition — a small-to-moderate reduction in suicidal ideation at postintervention. Heterogeneity was low and statistically non-significant (Q = 8.546, df = 7, p = 0.2869; I² = 24.27%; τ² = 0.020859). Two of the eight estimates were individually significant (SMD −0.768, 95% CI −1.244 to −0.293; and SMD −0.665, 95% CI −1.173 to −0.158); the remaining six were in the same direction but with confidence intervals crossing the null. Egger's test showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept −0.192, p = 0.8496) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0, adjusted SMD −0.3354), consistent with a low assessed risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** Indicated suicide-prevention interventions delivered in education settings produce a small-to-moderate, statistically significant reduction in suicidal ideation immediately at the end of the intervention relative to control conditions, with consistent effects across trials. The evidence base is nonetheless small (8 effect sizes, 511 participants), individual trials are underpowered, and this synthesis speaks only to the immediate postintervention timepoint; durability of benefit at short-, medium- and longer-term follow-up was outside the scope of this outcome and remains to be established.

## 1. Introduction

Suicide is among the leading causes of death in people aged 15–29 worldwide, and suicidal ideation and self-harm in adolescence and emerging adulthood are the strongest known antecedents of later suicide attempt and death. Prevention frameworks conventionally distinguish *universal* interventions (delivered to all members of a population), *selective* interventions (delivered to subgroups with elevated risk-factor profiles), and *indicated* interventions (delivered to individuals who already manifest early signs or symptoms of the outcome — in this context, current suicidal ideation, recent self-harm, or a positive screen on a suicide-risk measure).

Education settings — schools, further-education colleges and universities — occupy a strategically important position in this framework. They provide routine, non-stigmatising contact with almost the entire population of young people, they can embed screening and referral pathways, and they can deliver structured psychological and skills-based programmes without requiring families to navigate specialist mental health services. Universal school-based programmes have been studied extensively, but *indicated* programmes delivered in these same settings raise distinct questions: participants are already symptomatic, the interventions are typically more intensive and individually or small-group delivered, and the ethical and practical demands (risk monitoring, confidentiality, escalation protocols) are higher. Clinicians and school systems therefore need a specific estimate of how much such programmes change suicidal ideation, rather than an estimate blended across prevention tiers.

A further methodological question concerns timepoint. Pooling postintervention and follow-up estimates together conflates two substantively different questions — whether an intervention produces immediate change, and whether that change endures. This review addresses only the first.

**Research question.** What is the effect of indicated suicide-prevention interventions (targeting young people up to age 25 who already show early signs or symptoms of risk, such as current suicidal ideation or a history of self-harm), compared with control conditions, on suicidal ideation measured immediately at the end of the intervention (postintervention), in education settings (schools, colleges, universities)?

## 2. Methods: Search and Screening

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible under the following PICOS framework.

| Domain | Inclusion criteria |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Young people up to age 25 already showing early signs or symptoms of suicide risk (e.g. current suicidal ideation, positive risk screen, history of self-harm), recruited in or through an education setting (school, college, university). |
| **Intervention** | Indicated suicide-prevention intervention delivered in an education setting. Universal and selective-tier programmes were not eligible for this outcome. |
| **Comparator** | Any control condition: no intervention, waitlist, treatment-as-usual, attention/active control, or usual school curriculum. |
| **Outcome** | Suicidal ideation severity or frequency measured **immediately at the end of the intervention (postintervention assessment only)**, using a validated self-report or interview measure — e.g. Suicidal Ideation Questionnaire (SIQ/SIQ-JR), Beck Scale for Suicide Ideation (BSS/BSSI), Columbia-Suicide Severity Rating Scale ideation items, Positive and Negative Suicide Ideation Inventory (PANSI), Suicidal Behaviors Questionnaire ideation items, or suicidal-ideation subscales/single items of broader instruments (e.g. CES-D ideation item, Youth Risk Behavior Survey ideation item). |
| **Study design** | Controlled trials (randomized or otherwise controlled) reporting extractable group-level postintervention ideation data. |
| **Excluded timepoints** | Short-term (1–3 month), medium-term (4–12 month) and longest-follow-up assessments were explicitly **not** part of this outcome and were not extracted or pooled here. |

### 2.2 Information sources and screening

Records were identified through a systematic search of the health and education literature and processed through a structured screening and data-extraction pipeline. Titles and abstracts were screened against the eligibility criteria above, followed by full-text assessment of potentially eligible records. Reviews, protocols without outcome data, and duplicate reports of the same trial were removed so that each trial contributed its outcome data only once.

### 2.3 Data extraction and handling conventions

For each eligible comparison the following were extracted: group means, standard deviations and sample sizes at postintervention (or the statistics required to reconstruct them), the ideation instrument used, the nature of the control condition, and the education setting.

Two handling conventions were applied and are reported transparently here because they affect the precision of individual estimates:

1. **Multi-arm trials.** Where a trial contained more than one indicated-intervention arm, each indicated arm contributed one row against the shared control group. This preserves all randomized comparisons but means the control group is used more than once in such a trial, which can modestly understate the variance of the affected estimates.
2. **Total-N-only reporting.** Where a randomized trial reported only a total N without arm-level sample sizes, an even 50/50 split across groups was assumed. This assumption is noted explicitly as a source of imprecision in the affected effect size(s).

### 2.4 Effect size computation and synthesis

The effect measure for this synthesis was the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, applied at the explicit specification of the review question (`effect_measure_source`: explicit user request; no conflict between the requested and available measures was detected). SMDs were computed with the Hedges' small-sample correction and are therefore reported as Hedges' g-corrected SMDs. **Native-scale pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used`: true): the pooled value below is a confirmed SMD-scale result computed on the same metric in which the individual effect sizes were expressed, not a cross-metric standardized fallback.

Effect sizes were pooled using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of the between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was examined with Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure.

### 2.5 Direction of effect

Effect sizes retain the raw treatment-minus-control sign convention on the ideation scale. Because **lower** ideation scores denote a **better** outcome, a negative SMD in this synthesis corresponds to lower postintervention ideation in the intervention arm. Accordingly, the direction of all results reported below is stated using the pre-specified `favors` field rather than inferred from the arithmetic sign alone.

## 3. Included Studies

The 2 remaining records did not yield an extractable postintervention ideation estimate meeting the outcome definition (for example, because the only ideation data reported fell at a follow-up timepoint explicitly excluded from this outcome, or because group-level statistics were not recoverable) and therefore contribute to the narrative description of the evidence base but not to the pooled estimate.

## 4. Effect Sizes

All 8 comparisons produced effect estimates in the same direction — lower postintervention suicidal ideation in the indicated-intervention arm than in the control arm. The estimates ranged from −0.0425 (study_1_R1, essentially null) to −0.7685 (study_7_R7, a moderate-to-large advantage for the intervention). Using conventional benchmarks, three estimates were trivial-to-small in magnitude (|SMD| < 0.20: study_1_R1, study_6_R6, study_10_R10), three were small-to-moderate (0.20 ≤ |SMD| < 0.50: study_5_R5, study_8_R8, study_3_R3), and two were moderate-to-large (|SMD| ≥ 0.50: study_9_R9, study_7_R7).

Only two comparisons reached statistical significance in isolation (study_7_R7 and study_9_R9); the other six had confidence intervals that included zero. This pattern — uniform direction, wide individual intervals, few individually significant results — is the classic signature of a set of small, individually underpowered trials testing a real but modest effect, and is precisely the situation in which meta-analytic pooling adds most value. The widest intervals correspond to the smallest samples (study_6_R6, N = 39, CI width 1.261; study_10_R10, N = 49, CI width 1.121), and the narrowest to the largest (study_1_R1, N = 110, CI width 0.747), as expected.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was defined for this review — postintervention suicidal ideation — so one pooled estimate is reported; no separate outcome/timepoint subgroups required independent synthesis.

> **Pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) = −0.335 (95% CI −0.539 to −0.132), p = 0.0013; k = 8 comparisons, N = 511 participants; random-effects REML model.**

The statistic is a **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, pooled natively on the SMD scale (`native_pooling_used`: true); it is a confirmed native-scale result rather than a standardized fallback substituted for an unconfirmed measure.

**Direction.** Per the pre-specified `favors` field, this result **favours the indicated intervention for young people already showing signs/symptoms of suicide risk, delivered in an education setting**, over the control condition. The negative sign reflects the raw treatment-minus-control convention on an ideation scale where lower scores are better; it should not be read as disadvantaging the intervention.

**Magnitude and interpretation.** An SMD of −0.335 corresponds to a small-to-moderate reduction in suicidal ideation at the immediate end of the intervention. Expressed in distributional terms, the average intervention participant finished the programme with ideation lower than approximately 63% of control participants. The lower bound of the confidence interval (−0.539) is compatible with a moderate benefit, while the upper bound (−0.132) still represents a small but non-trivial benefit; the interval excludes the null throughout, so the data are not compatible with "no effect" at the conventional 5% level (p = 0.0013). Given that the outcome here is suicidal ideation in an already-symptomatic population — a proximal marker of a low-base-rate but catastrophic outcome — even an effect at the small end of this interval may be of clinical and public-health consequence.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **low and statistically non-significant**:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 8.546 (df = 7) |
| p-value for Q | 0.2869 |
| I² | 24.27% |
| τ² (REML) | 0.020859 |
| τ (REML) | ≈ 0.144 |

The Q test provides no evidence against the hypothesis of a common underlying effect (p = 0.2869), and I² = 24.27% falls at the boundary conventionally described as "low" heterogeneity, implying that roughly a quarter of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to real between-study differences rather than sampling error. The estimated between-study standard deviation (τ ≈ 0.144) is small relative to the pooled effect itself (−0.335), meaning the dispersion of true effects around the average is modest.

An approximate 95% prediction interval — the interval within which the true effect of a *new* comparable intervention would be expected to fall — spans roughly −0.77 to +0.10. Although this interval marginally crosses zero (as is almost inevitable with only 7 degrees of freedom for estimating τ²), the bulk of the predicted distribution lies on the side favouring the intervention. The practical reading is that the direction of benefit appears robust and reasonably consistent across these education-based indicated programmes, with uncertainty about *how large* rather than *whether* the benefit occurs.

## 7. Moderators

Formal moderator analyses (meta-regression or subgroup analysis) were **not conducted**, for two reasons.

First, and decisively, with k = 8 comparisons there are too few units of analysis to support credible moderator modelling. Standard guidance suggests a minimum of roughly 10 studies per covariate for meta-regression; fitting even a single moderator here would produce estimates with confidence intervals too wide to distinguish any hypothesis from any other, and would carry a high risk of spurious findings.

Second, the heterogeneity statistics give little to explain. With Q non-significant (p = 0.2869), I² = 24.27% and τ² = 0.020859, the amount of genuine between-study variance available to be attributed to moderators is small. Moderator analysis is most informative when substantial unexplained dispersion exists; that is not the case here.

Potential moderators of substantive interest that could not be examined, and that future reviews with a larger evidence base should address, include: intervention modality and theoretical orientation (e.g. cognitive-behavioural, dialectical-behaviour-therapy-informed skills, interpersonal, safety planning); dose and duration; delivery agent (school staff versus visiting clinicians); education level (secondary school versus college/university); baseline ideation severity and whether entry required current ideation versus history of self-harm; the specific ideation instrument used; the type of control condition (inactive waitlist/no-treatment versus active or attention-matched control, the latter of which would be expected to attenuate observed effects); and study risk of bias.

The relationship between comparison size and effect magnitude is displayed descriptively in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`); the two largest effects arose in mid-sized rather than the smallest samples, which argues against a simple small-study-effect gradient, consistent with the publication-bias results below.

## 8. Publication Bias

Publication and small-study bias were assessed with two complementary methods.

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | −0.192 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.8496 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted SMD | −0.3354 (unchanged) |
| Overall assessed risk | **Low** |

Egger's regression test shows no evidence of funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.8496, far from conventional significance), indicating that effect sizes are not systematically related to study precision. This is corroborated by the trim-and-fill analysis, which imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), meaning the pooled estimate is unchanged after adjustment for potential asymmetry. Together these results support a low risk of publication bias.

An important caveat applies. Tests of funnel-plot asymmetry have low statistical power when the number of included studies is small — typically fewer than 10, as is the case here with k = 8 — so the absence of detected bias should not be taken as definitive proof of its absence. Non-significance of Egger's test in this setting is weak evidence, and selective non-reporting of null findings in suicide-prevention trials (and of ideation outcomes within trials reporting multiple outcomes) remains a plausible residual threat that these diagnostics cannot exclude.

## 9. Discussion

This meta-analysis of 8 controlled comparisons (N = 511) found that indicated suicide-prevention interventions delivered in education settings reduced suicidal ideation at the immediate end of the intervention relative to control conditions, with a pooled SMD of −0.335 (95% CI −0.539 to −0.132; p = 0.0013), favouring the indicated intervention. The effect is small-to-moderate in conventional terms, statistically robust, and notably consistent: every one of the 8 comparisons pointed in the same direction, and heterogeneity was low (I² = 24.27%; Q p = 0.2869; τ² = 0.0209).

Three features of this result deserve emphasis.

**Consistency despite individual imprecision.** Only two of the eight comparisons were individually statistically significant. Read individually, the literature might appear equivocal, with six "negative" trials. Read meta-analytically, the pattern resolves into a coherent and reproducible signal: the apparent inconsistency is attributable almost entirely to sampling error in small samples rather than to genuine divergence in intervention effects. This is a strong argument against drawing conclusions about indicated school-based prevention from single trials.

**Clinical significance relative to magnitude.** A standardized effect of roughly one-third of a standard deviation sits in the range typically obtained by brief psychological interventions for adolescent internalising problems and is comparable to what is seen in indicated depression-prevention programmes. For suicidal ideation specifically — a proximal risk marker for attempt and death, and a source of substantial distress in its own right — a reduction of this size in an already-symptomatic population, achieved within the existing infrastructure of schools and universities and without requiring entry into specialist services, is plausibly worthwhile at a population level. Scalability matters here: education settings can reach symptomatic young people who would not otherwise present for care.

**Specificity of the claim.** The estimate applies strictly to the **postintervention** timepoint. By design, short-term (1–3 month), medium-term (4–12 month) and longest-follow-up assessments were excluded from this outcome. The analysis therefore establishes that these programmes *produce* change in ideation by the end of delivery; it says nothing about whether that change is *maintained*. Historically, immediate gains in psychological prevention trials attenuate at follow-up, and this question is the single most important gap for subsequent synthesis.

The low risk of publication bias detected (Egger p = 0.8496; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted SMD unchanged at −0.3354) provides modest reassurance that the pooled estimate is not an artefact of selective reporting, though with k = 8 these tests are underpowered and cannot be treated as conclusive.

Finally, the finding should be situated against the broader prevention literature. Universal school-based programmes generally show small effects on ideation in unselected populations; the present result suggests that concentrating more intensive resources on young people who already screen positive yields at least comparable and plausibly larger per-participant benefit. This supports a tiered model in which universal screening or awareness activity feeds into indicated intervention pathways within the same institution — but the evidence base is currently too thin to adjudicate between specific programme types, doses or delivery agents.

## 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

No formal validation flags were raised by the synthesis pipeline for this review. The following data-handling points are nonetheless reported in the interest of transparency, because each bears on the interpretation of the pooled estimate:

- **Records entering extraction versus contributing effect sizes.** Ten study records reached the extraction stage, but only 8 yielded a postintervention suicidal-ideation effect size meeting the outcome definition. The 2 non-contributing records are not represented in the pooled estimate; their exclusion was driven by the outcome/timepoint definition and by the recoverability of group-level statistics, not by their findings.
- **Review articles and duplicate trial reports.** Screening removed reviews, protocols and duplicate reports of the same trial so that no trial contributed its outcome data more than once; this is a deliberate safeguard against double-counting.
- **Timepoint restriction.** Short-term (1–3 month), medium-term (4–12 month) and longest-follow-up ideation data were, by design, neither extracted nor pooled. Any statement about durability of effect is outside the evidentiary reach of this report.
- **Multi-arm trials.** Where a trial contained multiple indicated-intervention arms, each arm was entered as a separate row against the shared control group. The shared control group is therefore reused across those rows, which can modestly understate the variance of the affected estimates and slightly overweight such trials.
- **Assumed 50/50 allocation.** Where a randomized trial reported only a total N, an even 50/50 split across arms was assumed. This assumption is noted as a source of imprecision in the variance of the affected effect size(s).
- **Effect measure.** The SMD was applied at explicit user specification; no conflict with the available data was detected, and pooling was performed natively on the SMD scale. The reported pooled value is therefore a confirmed native-scale SMD, not a fallback standardization.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Small number of comparisons and participants.** With k = 8 and N = 511, the evidence base is thin. The pooled confidence interval (−0.539 to −0.132) spans a fourfold range in effect magnitude, and the addition or removal of a single trial could shift the estimate materially.
2. **Underpowered individual trials.** Median comparison size was approximately 62 participants; only two comparisons were individually significant. Small trials are also more vulnerable to design weaknesses that inflate effects.
3. **No moderator analysis possible.** Intervention content, dose, delivery agent, education level, entry criteria (current ideation versus self-harm history), ideation instrument, control condition type and risk of bias could not be examined as moderators. Heterogeneity in these characteristics is almost certainly present even though statistical heterogeneity was low, and the pooled figure therefore averages across potentially quite different programmes.
4. **Control-condition heterogeneity.** Inactive comparators (waitlist, no intervention) and active comparators (attention control, treatment-as-usual, usual curriculum) were pooled together. Because inactive controls typically yield larger apparent effects, the pooled estimate may overstate the benefit relative to an active alternative and understate it relative to nothing at all, depending on the mix.
5. **Outcome measurement variability and self-report reliance.** Pooling across instruments with differing psychometric properties — from full scales such as the SIQ or BSS to single ideation items embedded in broader measures — introduces measurement noise and reduces comparability. Most outcomes are self-reported and therefore susceptible to demand characteristics, which are particularly salient when participants know they have received an intervention and when blinding of participants is impossible.
6. **Postintervention timepoint only, and no behavioural outcomes.** Durability was not assessed, and the review does not address suicide attempts, self-harm episodes, hospitalisation or death — outcomes of greater ultimate importance than ideation scores, and for which these trials are universally underpowered.
7. **Limited power of bias diagnostics.** With fewer than 10 studies, Egger's test and trim-and-fill have low power; the "low risk" designation reflects the absence of detected asymmetry, not demonstrated absence of bias.
8. **Risk-of-bias assessment not incorporated into the pooled estimate.** No sensitivity analysis restricted to low-risk-of-bias trials was feasible at this k, so the influence of methodological quality on the pooled effect is unknown.
9. **Generalisability.** The settings, populations and health-system contexts of the contributing trials constrain transportability; findings may not extend to education systems with different referral infrastructure or to young people outside formal education, who may be at elevated risk.

---

## 12. Conclusion

Across 8 controlled comparisons involving 511 young people, indicated suicide-prevention interventions delivered in education settings produced a statistically significant, small-to-moderate reduction in suicidal ideation measured immediately at the end of the intervention, relative to control conditions (pooled Standardized Mean Difference = −0.335, 95% CI −0.539 to −0.132, p = 0.0013; REML random-effects; native SMD-scale pooling). The result **favours the indicated intervention**. Heterogeneity was low and non-significant (I² = 24.27%; Q = 8.546, df = 7, p = 0.2869; τ² = 0.020859), all eight comparisons pointed in the same direction, and publication-bias diagnostics were unremarkable (Egger p = 0.8496; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted SMD −0.3354), although these tests are underpowered at this number of studies.

The practical implication is that schools, colleges and universities can achieve measurable immediate reductions in suicidal ideation among young people who already show signs of risk, using structured indicated programmes delivered within their own setting. The critical unresolved questions are whether this benefit persists beyond the end of the intervention, whether it translates into reduced self-harm and suicide attempt, and which programme components drive it. Answering them requires larger, adequately powered trials with harmonised ideation measurement, active comparators, pre-registered follow-up assessments and complete outcome reporting.
