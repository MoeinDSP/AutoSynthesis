# Indicated Interventions for Suicidal Ideation in Young People in Education Settings: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Immediate Postintervention Effects

## Abstract

## Abstract

**Background:** Young people in education settings who already exhibit suicidal ideation, self-harm, or related warning signs are an important target for indicated suicide-prevention interventions.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of indicated interventions, compared with control conditions, on suicidal ideation measured immediately at intervention completion among young people aged up to 25 years in education settings.

**Methods:** The supplied evidence pipeline contained 10 studies and six immediate-postintervention effect sizes suitable for quantitative synthesis. To prevent statistical dependence, the prespecified outcome-selection hierarchy was a validated dedicated suicidal-ideation scale, followed by the ideation subscale of a validated suicide-risk scale, and then a single-item measure. A random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation was used. The supplied statistic was labelled Hedges' g; however, its original effect measure was unresolved and native-scale pooling was not used, so it must be treated as a standardized fallback rather than a confirmed native-scale result. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's test and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Six effect sizes representing 402 participants were pooled. The pooled Hedges' g was -0.35267667500426897 (95% CI [-0.607, -0.098], p = 0.006557572515697006; k = 6). According to the supplied direction-of-effect classification, this result favored indicated interventions. Heterogeneity was modest to moderate (Q = 7.678, p = 0.1749; I² = 37.2%; τ² = 0.037176). Egger's intercept was -0.0991 (p = 0.7723), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀ = 0), leaving the adjusted estimate at -0.3527. The supplied publication-bias classification was low risk.

**Conclusions:** The available synthesis indicates a statistically significant, small-to-moderate immediate postintervention advantage for indicated interventions. Interpretation should nevertheless be cautious because only six effects were pooled, study-level clinical and methodological details were unavailable, the original effect measure could not be confirmed, and the reported negative sign conflicts with the prespecified statement that positive Hedges' g denotes greater improvement under intervention. The intervention-favoring interpretation therefore follows the supplied `favors` field and requires verification against the original outcome coding.

# Introduction

Suicidal ideation and self-harm among young people are major public-health concerns. Education settings may provide opportunities to identify symptomatic or already-affected young people and deliver indicated interventions to those with current suicidal ideation, a history of self-harm, or related evidence of elevated suicide risk. Unlike universal interventions delivered to all students or selective interventions aimed at broad risk groups, indicated interventions target individuals already showing relevant signs or symptoms.

This review addressed the following question: among young people aged up to 25 years in education settings who already show early signs or symptoms of suicide risk, what is the effect of indicated interventions, compared with a control condition, on suicidal ideation measured immediately at the end of the intervention?

The treatment of interest was any indicated intervention for symptomatic or already-affected young people at risk of suicide in an education setting. Eligible comparators included usual care, waitlist, attention control, minimal intervention, or no intervention. The outcome was quantitative suicidal ideation measured at immediate intervention completion. Assessments at 1–3 months, 4–12 months, or the longest follow-up were outside the scope of this analysis.

# Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood estimation pooled six effects from 402 participants. The pooled **Hedges' g** was **-0.35267667500426897** (95% CI **[-0.607, -0.098]**; p = **0.006557572515697006**; k = **6**). The supplied `favors` field classifies this result as favoring **indicated interventions for symptomatic or already-affected young people at risk of suicide in education settings**.

The confidence interval excluded the null, and the model test was statistically significant. In standardized-effect terms, the magnitude is conventionally compatible with a small-to-moderate effect. Such benchmarks are only descriptive, however, and should not replace clinical interpretation based on the suicidal-ideation instruments, baseline severity, and absolute change—information that was not supplied.

Because the original effect measure was unresolved and native pooling was not used, the pooled value should be described as a standardized fallback labelled Hedges' g rather than as a confirmed native-scale estimate. # Heterogeneity

Cochran's heterogeneity statistic was **Q = 7.678** with **p = 0.1749**. The estimated proportion of variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity was **I² = 37.2%**, and the estimated between-study variance was **τ² = 0.037176**.

These results suggest modest-to-moderate inconsistency across effects. The Q test did not reject homogeneity at the conventional 0.05 threshold, but its power is limited with only six studies. Accordingly, the non-significant Q result should not be treated as evidence that intervention effects are identical across settings or intervention types. # Publication Bias

Egger's regression intercept was **-0.0991**, with **p = 0.7723**. This test did not detect funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies and consequently left the pooled estimate unchanged at **-0.3527**. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **low**.

These findings provide no statistical evidence of publication bias in the available data, but they do not establish its absence. Both Egger's test and trim-and-fill have limited reliability when only six studies are available, and asymmetry can arise from causes other than publication bias. The low-risk classification should therefore be interpreted as provisional.

# Discussion

This synthesis found that indicated interventions for young people already exhibiting suicide-risk signs or symptoms in education settings were associated with a statistically significant immediate postintervention advantage over control conditions. Across six effects and 402 participants, the pooled Hedges' g was -0.35267667500426897 (95% CI [-0.607, -0.098]), and the supplied direction classification favored the indicated-intervention arm. Between-study inconsistency was present but not extreme (I² = 37.2%; τ² = 0.037176).

The result is relevant to a focused clinical and educational question: whether targeted intervention can reduce suicidal ideation by the point at which treatment ends. The analysis deliberately does not address whether any benefit persists at 1–3 months, 4–12 months, or longer follow-up. It also does not establish effects on suicide attempts, self-harm, service use, acceptability, or adverse events. Immediate reductions in ideation may be valuable, but durability and effects on behavioral outcomes remain separate questions.

At the individual-study level, effects varied in magnitude, and four of six confidence intervals included the null. Two studies showed confidence intervals excluding the null. The random-effects result therefore reflects a weighted synthesis rather than uniform statistically significant findings across all studies. Differences in populations, intervention content, control intensity, or measurement could plausibly contribute to the observed heterogeneity, although these explanations could not be tested.

The publication-bias analyses were reassuring in that Egger's test was non-significant and trim-and-fill imputed no studies. Nevertheless, the evidence base was too small for these methods to exclude selective dissemination reliably. Similarly, the statistically significant pooled result does not by itself demonstrate that the change was clinically important, because no instrument-specific thresholds, absolute score differences, response rates, or minimal important differences were available.

A major interpretive issue concerns effect coding. The protocol-level direction statement specifies that positive Hedges' g indicates greater improvement under intervention, yet the reported pooled estimate is negative while the analysis metadata says that it favors intervention. The substantive direction in this report follows the supplied `favors` field, as required, but this discrepancy should be reconciled by checking the original group means, score direction, and effect-size calculation before the estimate is used for clinical guidance or publication.

# Limitations

1. **Incomplete search and screening information:** Databases, search dates, search strategies, deduplication procedures, full-text exclusions, and a PRISMA flow were not supplied, limiting reproducibility and preventing appraisal of search completeness.
2. **Limited study-level description:** Participant, setting, intervention, comparator, and outcome-measure characteristics were unavailable, precluding assessment of clinical comparability and generalizability.
3. **Unexplained attrition from the evidence pipeline:** Ten studies were present in the pipeline, but only six contributed to the pooled immediate-postintervention result. Study-specific reasons for exclusion from the meta-analysis were not reported.
4. **Unconfirmed effect measure:** Although labelled Hedges' g, the original measure was unresolved and native pooling was not used. The estimate is therefore a standardized fallback, not a confirmed native-scale result.
5. **Direction-coding inconsistency:** Negative estimates were classified as favoring intervention even though the prespecified interpretation stated that positive Hedges' g represented intervention benefit. This requires source-data verification.
6. **Small evidence base:** Only six effects and 402 participants were pooled. Estimates of heterogeneity and publication bias are imprecise with so few studies.
7. **No risk-of-bias assessment supplied:** Randomization, allocation concealment, masking, attrition, selective reporting, and other bias domains could not be evaluated.
8. **No moderator or sensitivity analyses:** The robustness of the result to study quality, intervention type, comparator type, outcome measure, influential cases, or alternative variance estimators was not reported.
9. **Immediate outcomes only:** The analysis does not indicate whether reductions in suicidal ideation were sustained after intervention completion.
10. **Clinical significance uncertain:** Standardized effects cannot be directly translated into absolute symptom change or meaningful improvement without instrument-specific information.

# Conclusion

Based on six effects involving 402 participants, indicated interventions in education settings produced a statistically significant immediate postintervention advantage in suicidal ideation relative to control conditions. The random-effects pooled Hedges' g was -0.35267667500426897 (95% CI [-0.607, -0.098], p = 0.006557572515697006), with the supplied direction field favoring indicated interventions. Heterogeneity was modest to moderate (I² = 37.2%), and the available tests did not identify publication bias.

The finding supports the potential short-term value of targeted intervention for young people already showing suicide-risk symptoms, but confidence is constrained by the small evidence base, missing study-level and risk-of-bias information, lack of longer-term inference, use of an unconfirmed standardized fallback, and unresolved sign coding. Verification of the effect direction and a fully documented systematic-review process are necessary before drawing definitive practice or policy conclusions.
