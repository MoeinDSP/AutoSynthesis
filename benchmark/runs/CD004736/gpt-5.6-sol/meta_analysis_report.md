# Iron-Containing Supplementation During Pregnancy and Maternal Iron-Deficiency Anaemia at Term: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Maternal iron-deficiency anaemia at term is clinically important, but its response to antenatal iron-containing supplementation must be evaluated using an outcome definition that combines low haemoglobin with laboratory evidence of iron deficiency.

**Objective:** To assess, among pregnant women, the effect of any iron-containing supplement versus the same supplement without iron, placebo, or no treatment on maternal iron-deficiency anaemia at term, defined as haemoglobin <110 g/L plus at least one additional laboratory indicator of iron deficiency, assessed at 37 weeks’ gestation or later.

**Methods:** Six studies were represented in the supplied evidence set, but only two studies contributed effect estimates to the quantitative synthesis. A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled risk ratios (RRs) on their native scale. The two contributing studies included 1,999 participants in total. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ².

**Results:** The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.4393167626963164 (95% CI 0.288–0.670; p=0.00013140399729587667; k=2; n=1,999). Heterogeneity was low but imprecisely estimated (Q=1.38, p=0.2402; I²=27.52%; τ²=0.041976). Although the pooled estimate differed statistically from the null value of RR=1, the supplied outcome-coding metadata classified the result as favoring neither arm because the direction of benefit could not be confirmed. It should therefore be interpreted as evidence of a between-group difference, not as confirmed evidence that either treatment or control reduced iron-deficiency anaemia. Publication bias and moderator effects could not be evaluated meaningfully with only two studies.

**Conclusions:** Two studies provided a statistically significant pooled RR for maternal iron-deficiency anaemia at term, with low observed heterogeneity. However, incomplete outcome-direction information, absent arm-level event counts, and the exclusion of four of six studies from quantitative synthesis prevent a definitive conclusion about whether iron-containing supplementation reduced the outcome.

## Introduction

Iron deficiency and anaemia are common concerns during pregnancy and may persist until delivery. Because anaemia has multiple causes, a clinically specific assessment of maternal iron-deficiency anaemia requires both a low haemoglobin concentration and corroborating laboratory evidence of iron deficiency. This review therefore focused on a strict binary outcome: haemoglobin <110 g/L together with at least one additional laboratory indicator of iron deficiency, measured at 37 weeks’ gestation or later.

The review question was: among pregnant women, what is the effect of any iron-containing supplement, compared with the same supplement without iron, placebo, or no treatment, on maternal iron-deficiency anaemia at term?

The prespecified direction of clinical benefit was a reduction in maternal iron-deficiency anaemia with iron-containing supplementation. Nevertheless, direction was not inferred from the numerical sign or magnitude of the supplied estimates; it was interpreted according to the provided outcome-coding metadata.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model estimated using restricted maximum likelihood produced a **pooled Risk Ratio (RR) of 0.4393167626963164** (95% CI 0.288–0.670; p=0.00013140399729587667; k=2; n=1,999).

The confidence interval excluded the null value of RR=1, and the associated p-value indicated a statistically significant between-group difference. However, the supplied `favors` classification was **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).”** The outcome-direction coding was therefore not sufficiently confirmed to state that the result favored iron-containing supplementation or the control condition. In accordance with that classification, the estimate should be interpreted as evidence of a difference between groups rather than as confirmed evidence of reduced iron-deficiency anaemia in either arm.

## Heterogeneity

Observed heterogeneity was low:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 1.38
- **Q-test p-value:** 0.2402
- **I²:** 27.52%
- **τ²:** 0.041976

With k=2, Cochran’s Q has 1 degree of freedom. The non-significant Q test does not establish homogeneity because this test has very low power when only two studies are included. Similarly, I² and τ² are unstable with such a small evidence base. The heterogeneity results should therefore be viewed as descriptive rather than definitive.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This random-effects meta-analysis of two studies and 1,999 participants yielded a pooled RR of 0.4393167626963164 (95% CI 0.288–0.670; p=0.00013140399729587667). Statistical heterogeneity was low by conventional descriptive thresholds (I²=27.52%; τ²=0.041976), and Cochran’s Q was 1.38 (p=0.2402).

Despite the estimate’s statistical separation from RR=1, the direction of clinical benefit cannot be asserted from the supplied data. The provided coding explicitly classified the result as favoring neither arm because it was not confirmed which event was represented in the ratio and how the arms were oriented. It would therefore be inappropriate to conclude solely from the RR being below 1 that antenatal iron supplementation reduced maternal iron-deficiency anaemia at term.

### Clinical interpretation

The target endpoint is clinically specific because it requires haemoglobin <110 g/L plus an additional biochemical indicator of iron deficiency. This is preferable to haemoglobin alone for distinguishing iron-deficiency anaemia from anaemia due to other causes. However, no study-level event counts, laboratory-marker definitions, or diagnostic thresholds beyond the review-level definition were available. It is consequently uncertain whether both contributing studies operationalized the endpoint identically.

If the event and arm coding were verified as iron-deficiency anaemia in the iron-supplement arm relative to control, the magnitude of the reported RR would represent a potentially important relative difference. That conditional interpretation cannot be adopted as the review conclusion because the supplied direction metadata does not confirm it. Absolute effects and numbers needed to treat also cannot be calculated without a control-group event rate.

### Robustness and certainty

The confidence interval was statistically precise relative to the null, driven in part by the total sample size of 1,999. Nevertheless, statistical precision does not resolve uncertainty arising from outcome coding, missing arm-level data, incomplete study inclusion, potential clinical heterogeneity, and unavailable risk-of-bias assessments.

No formal certainty-of-evidence rating could be completed because information required for domains such as study limitations, indirectness, inconsistency, imprecision in absolute terms, and publication bias was incomplete. At minimum, confidence is constrained by the presence of only two pooled studies, the inability to verify the direction of effect, and the absence of data explaining why four represented studies were not synthesized.

No formal `validation_flags` object was included in the supplied context. However, the available fields reveal several issues requiring explicit caution:

- Study-level values were labelled as Hedges’ g, while the pooled analysis was explicitly a native-scale RR analysis. These statistics are not interchangeable, and the study-level values cannot be used to reproduce the pooled RR.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations:

1. **Only two studies were pooled.** Heterogeneity estimates, random-effects variance, and the pooled result are correspondingly sensitive to each study.
2. **Four represented studies did not enter the synthesis.** Their eligibility status and reasons for non-contribution were not supplied, creating possible availability or reporting bias.
3. **No arm-level binary data were available.** Event counts and denominators could not be verified, and absolute risk differences could not be estimated.
4. **Direction of effect was unresolved.** The pooled RR differed from 1, but the metadata did not permit assignment of benefit to iron-containing supplementation or control.
5. **Study-level and pooled effect labels were discordant.** Standardized study-level fields were supplied alongside a native RR meta-analysis, precluding transparent reconstruction.
6. **Clinical and methodological characteristics were missing.** Intervention regimens, comparators, baseline iron status, adherence, outcome-marker definitions, and attrition could not be compared.
7. **Risk of bias was not reported.** Internal validity and the potential influence of randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, missing data, or selective reporting could not be assessed.
8. **Publication bias could not be tested.** With k=2, asymmetry tests and related methods are uninformative.
9. **No moderator analysis was feasible.** Potential sources of clinical heterogeneity remain unexplored.
10. **Search and screening methods were unavailable.** The completeness and reproducibility of study identification cannot be established.

## Conclusion

Across two studies involving 1,999 participants, antenatal iron-containing supplementation versus a non-iron or untreated control was associated with a pooled Risk Ratio of 0.4393167626963164 (95% CI 0.288–0.670; p=0.00013140399729587667) for the analyzed term outcome. Observed heterogeneity was low (I²=27.52%; τ²=0.041976; Q=1.38, p=0.2402), but all heterogeneity measures were based on only two studies.

The supplied coding did not establish which arm the pooled result favored. Therefore, this synthesis demonstrates a statistically significant between-group difference but does **not** establish from the available information that iron-containing supplementation reduced maternal iron-deficiency anaemia at term. Verification of arm orientation, event coding, study-level event counts, and the reasons four studies did not contribute is required before a definitive clinical conclusion can be drawn.
