# Antenatal Magnesium Sulphate for Fetal Neuroprotection and Cerebral Palsy at 18–24 Months: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Antenatal magnesium sulphate is administered to women at risk of preterm birth to reduce neurological morbidity in their children. This review evaluated cerebral palsy at each trial’s first reported follow-up from 18 months through approximately 2 years’ corrected age.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons were antenatal magnesium sulphate administered specifically for fetal neuroprotection versus placebo. The intended outcome was cerebral palsy diagnosed in children, using each trial’s definition and outcome-specific child denominator. Multiple magnesium dose-regimen arms were to be combined within trials. The supplied evidence set contained six studies, of which four contributed effect estimates to the quantitative synthesis. A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled risk ratios (RRs). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were evaluated with Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Four studies comprising 3,131 children contributed to the meta-analysis. The pooled risk ratio was 0.818701947800097 (95% CI 0.587–1.142; p=0.23888933750558375). The supplied result classified the finding as favoring neither arm because the confidence interval included the null. Heterogeneity was negligible (Q=2.789, p=0.4254; I²=0.0%; τ²=0.000001). Egger’s intercept was −0.4666 (p=0.001), and trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study; the supplied publication-bias assessment rated the risk as high. Because only four studies were pooled, these asymmetry analyses are intrinsically unstable.

**Conclusions:** The available aggregate analysis did not establish a statistically clear reduction in cerebral palsy with antenatal magnesium sulphate compared with placebo at approximately 18–24 months. Although the point estimate was below 1, the confidence interval included both potentially important benefit and possible harm. Interpretation is constrained by possible small-study effects and, critically, by the absence of trial-level event counts, outcome denominators, diagnostic definitions, assessor details, follow-up timing, and bibliographic records in the supplied data.

## Introduction

Preterm birth is associated with an increased risk of cerebral palsy and other long-term neurodevelopmental impairment. Antenatal magnesium sulphate administered shortly before anticipated preterm birth has been investigated as a fetal neuroprotective intervention. Because death competes with ascertainment of cerebral palsy and because neurodevelopmental definitions and follow-up denominators may differ among trials, a focused synthesis should distinguish cerebral palsy among children with relevant outcome data from composites such as death or cerebral palsy.

The review question was: among women at risk of preterm birth, what is the effect of antenatal magnesium sulphate administered specifically for fetal neuroprotection, compared with placebo, on cerebral palsy in their children at the first reported follow-up from 18 months through approximately 2 years’ corrected age?

The prespecified unit of analysis was the child, including children from multiple births. The intended effect measure was the risk ratio (RR), with cerebral palsy as the event. An RR below 1 therefore corresponds numerically to fewer cerebral-palsy diagnoses after magnesium sulphate, although conclusions about which arm is favored must also account for statistical uncertainty.

## Search/Screening

### Eligibility criteria

The intended eligibility criteria were:

- **Population:** women at risk of preterm birth and their children;
- **Intervention:** antenatal magnesium sulphate administered specifically for fetal neuroprotection, with multiple eligible magnesium dose-regimen arms combined within a trial;
- **Comparator:** placebo;
- **Outcome:** cerebral palsy in children at the first reported follow-up from 18 months through approximately 2 years’ corrected age;
- **Outcome definition:** each trial’s own cerebral-palsy diagnosis, such as abnormal tone with loss of motor function or an overall neurological diagnosis made by a pediatric assessor;
- **Denominator hierarchy:** children with cerebral-palsy data when reported; otherwise surviving children assessed, all live births with data, or children randomized only when no more appropriate denominator was available;
- **Excluded outcomes:** school-age follow-up, death alone, death or cerebral palsy, substantial gross motor dysfunction, blindness, deafness, maternal outcomes, and adverse events.

### Search and selection information available

The supplied pipeline summary identified six studies and four quantitative effect sizes. Four studies entered the meta-analysis; the two-study difference between the evidence-set count and the pooled-study count was not explained. ## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects meta-analysis using restricted maximum likelihood included four studies and 3,131 children. The **pooled Risk Ratio (RR)** was **0.818701947800097** with a **95% confidence interval of 0.587 to 1.142** and **p=0.23888933750558375**.

The supplied favorability field classified this result as **favoring neither arm**. This is consistent with the absence of a statistically clear difference: the 95% confidence interval crosses the null value of RR=1. Although the point estimate is numerically below 1, the interval includes both a potentially meaningful reduction and a possible increase in cerebral-palsy risk. The evidence therefore does not establish superiority of magnesium sulphate or placebo for this outcome at the specified early follow-up.

No absolute effect can be estimated because the control-group event rate and arm-specific event counts were not provided. Accordingly, numbers needed to treat or harm were not calculated.

A cumulative-analysis figure was generated at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`. Its sequence and numerical incremental estimates were not supplied in the structured results, so no claims are made about when or whether cumulative statistical significance was reached.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was negligible in the fitted model:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 2.789;
- **Q-test p value:** 0.4254;
- **I²:** 0.0%;
- **τ²:** 0.000001;
- **Number of pooled studies:** 4.

The Q test did not reject homogeneity, and I² was 0.0%. The extremely small estimated between-study variance indicates that the model detected essentially no dispersion beyond sampling error. Nevertheless, with only four studies, both Q and I² have limited ability to identify genuine clinical or methodological heterogeneity. Moreover, statistical homogeneity does not demonstrate equivalence of cerebral-palsy definitions, assessors, follow-up procedures, populations, or magnesium regimens.

## Moderators

## Publication Bias

The supplied small-study-effect diagnostics were:

- **Egger’s intercept:** −0.4666;
- **Egger’s-test p value:** 0.001;
- **Trim-and-fill estimated missing studies:** 1;
- **Supplied trim-and-fill diagnostic estimate:** 0.28;
- **Overall publication-bias rating:** high risk.

The statistically significant Egger’s result indicates marked funnel-plot asymmetry in the supplied analysis and may reflect publication bias, selective reporting, small-study effects, chance, or clinical and methodological differences correlated with study size. Trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study. The supplied value of 0.28 is reported only as a diagnostic estimate on the pipeline’s unspecified trim-and-fill scale; it is **not** presented as a corrected pooled RR because no native RR label or confidence interval accompanied it.

These findings warrant caution, but they are not definitive evidence of publication bias. Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill are generally unreliable with very few studies, and only four studies were pooled here. The small study with n=59 and its comparatively extreme supplied standardized value may strongly influence asymmetry diagnostics. The high-risk classification is retained exactly as supplied, while recognizing this substantial imprecision.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

In four studies totaling 3,131 children, antenatal magnesium sulphate given for fetal neuroprotection was not associated with a statistically clear difference in cerebral palsy compared with placebo at the first reported follow-up from 18 months through approximately 2 years’ corrected age. The pooled RR was 0.818701947800097 (95% CI 0.587–1.142; p=0.23888933750558375). The supplied interpretation favored neither arm.

The model estimated negligible statistical heterogeneity (I²=0.0%; τ²=0.000001), but the evidence base was small. In addition, Egger’s test suggested pronounced asymmetry (intercept −0.4666; p=0.001), trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study, and the supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as high. Thus, the pooled estimate should not be interpreted in isolation.

### Clinical interpretation

The point estimate is compatible with fewer cerebral-palsy diagnoses under magnesium sulphate, but the confidence interval does not exclude no effect or a possible increase. Because arm-specific event counts and a representative control risk were absent, the result cannot be translated into an absolute number of cerebral-palsy cases prevented or added per 1,000 children.

The outcome in this review is cerebral palsy alone rather than death or a composite of death or cerebral palsy. That distinction is important because treatment could affect survival and thereby alter the population in whom cerebral palsy can be assessed. The supplied aggregate result does not permit competing-risk analyses or evaluation of differential survival and follow-up.

### Certainty and applicability

A formal GRADE assessment was not possible because trial-level risk-of-bias information, directness details, outcome definitions, attrition, and event counts were missing. Precision is limited because the confidence interval spans appreciable benefit and possible harm. Potential small-study effects further reduce confidence. Applicability to particular gestational ages, causes of threatened preterm birth, dosing protocols, or health-care settings cannot be established from the supplied data.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` object was supplied, several internal reporting constraints are evident in the dataset:

- The pipeline reports six studies but only four quantitative effect sizes; no reasons are given for the two studies not represented in the pooled estimate.
- Trial names and bibliographic records are absent, preventing verification of duplicate publications or overlapping cohorts.
- Cerebral-palsy event counts, arm-level totals, diagnosis definitions, assessor details, and exact corrected ages are absent.
- The study-level standardized values are on a different scale from the confirmed native RR meta-analysis and must not be treated as individual RRs.
- The trim-and-fill estimate of 0.28 lacks a confirmed native RR label and therefore cannot be interpreted as an adjusted pooled risk ratio.
- A bubble plot was generated without a reported moderator definition or meta-regression result.

These issues do not change the reported pooled statistics, but they materially limit reproducibility and clinical interpretation.

## Limitations

First, the search and screening process cannot be reproduced because databases, dates, queries, screening counts, and full-text exclusion reasons were not provided. The synthesis cannot therefore establish that all eligible trials or reports were identified.

Second, the trial-level event counts and denominators required for a transparent binary-outcome meta-analysis were unavailable. It was not possible to confirm whether denominators represented children with cerebral-palsy data, surviving children assessed, live births with data, or randomized children. Percentage-to-count conversions and handling of missing outcome data could not be audited.

Third, cerebral-palsy definitions, outcome assessors, masking, and exact follow-up ages were not reported in the supplied dataset. Clinical consistency with the requested 18-month to approximately 2-year corrected-age outcome remains unverified.

Fourth, six studies were reported in the pipeline but only four entered the pooled analysis, without an explanation for the discrepancy. Selective availability of eligible outcome data cannot be excluded.

Fifth, only four studies were pooled. Estimates of heterogeneity are imprecise, moderator analyses are not credible at this sample size, and tests for funnel-plot asymmetry and trim-and-fill adjustments are unstable.

Sixth, no risk-of-bias assessment or certainty-of-evidence evaluation was supplied. The effects of randomization procedures, allocation concealment, masking, attrition, selective reporting, and other biases therefore remain unknown.

Finally, the supplied study-level standardized values cannot be reconciled with individual native-scale RRs from the available information. The pooled RR should be regarded as the authoritative quantitative result, but independent numerical replication is not possible without arm-level data.

## Conclusion

Across four studies and 3,131 children, the pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** for cerebral palsy after antenatal magnesium sulphate administered for fetal neuroprotection versus placebo was **0.818701947800097** (95% CI **0.587–1.142**; p=**0.23888933750558375**). The supplied analysis classified the finding as favoring neither arm. Statistical heterogeneity was negligible, but possible small-study effects and publication bias were rated as high risk.

The analysis therefore does not provide statistically conclusive evidence that antenatal magnesium sulphate reduces cerebral palsy at approximately 18–24 months, while also not excluding a clinically important benefit. Firm interpretation requires the original trial reports, arm-specific event counts and outcome denominators, cerebral-palsy definitions, assessor information, exact follow-up timing, and a transparent account of why two of the six represented studies did not enter the meta-analysis.
