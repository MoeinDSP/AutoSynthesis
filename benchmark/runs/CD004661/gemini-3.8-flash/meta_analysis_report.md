# Antenatal Magnesium Sulphate and Cerebral Palsy in Children Up to 2 Years' Corrected Age: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Preterm birth is a leading cause of neurodevelopmental impairment, including cerebral palsy. Antenatal administration of magnesium sulphate has been investigated as a neuroprotective strategy for fetuses at risk of preterm delivery.

Objectives: To evaluate the effect of antenatal magnesium sulphate compared with placebo or no treatment on the incidence of cerebral palsy in children up to 2 years' corrected age.

Methods: A systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis was conducted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. Trials evaluating antenatal magnesium sulphate versus placebo or no treatment in women at risk of preterm birth reporting cerebral palsy up to 2 years' corrected age were identified. Effect measures were analyzed according to reported metrics, stratifying by outcome measure groups.

Results: From 6 identified studies, 5 individual effect sizes were evaluated. For the primary pooled analysis of studies reporting or harmonized to Risk Ratio (RR) with sufficient data (k = 4 trials, n = 4,693 children), the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.75 (95% CI [0.550, 1.011], p = 0.0590). In terms of directional categorization, the result favors neither arm (tie or undetermined/ratio measure where favourability depends on threshold/event counting), remaining borderline statistically non-significant at the conventional 5% alpha level. Heterogeneity across trials was virtually absent (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.000, Cochran's Q = 1.915, p = 0.5903). A distinct subgrouping for trials with reported Odds Ratios (k = 1, n = 680) possessed insufficient data to pool independently, exhibiting an individual effect size of -0.272 (95% CI [-0.423, -0.121]). Assessment of publication bias demonstrated low risk of small-study effects (Egger's intercept = -0.26, p = 0.5137; Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill k₀ = 0).

Conclusions: Antenatal magnesium sulphate administration showed a directionally favorable trend toward reduced cerebral palsy risk in offspring up to 2 years corrected age, but the pooled Risk Ratio (RR 0.75, 95% CI [0.550, 1.011]) was not statistically significant (p = 0.0590) and favors neither arm based on strict confidence bounds. Further standardization of outcome definitions and continuous long-term follow-up are warranted.

# Antenatal Magnesium Sulphate for Neuroprotection Against Cerebral Palsy in Preterm Offspring: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Introduction

Cerebral palsy encompasses a group of permanent disorders of movement and posture development causing activity limitation, attributed to non-progressive disturbances occurring in the developing fetal or infant brain. Preterm birth is the single most prominent antecedent risk factor for cerebral palsy, with infants born prior to 32 to 34 weeks of gestation experiencing disproportionately elevated rates of intraventricular hemorrhage, periventricular leukomalacia, and subsequent motor impairments.

Over the past three decades, antenatal administration of magnesium sulphate ($MgSO_4$) to mothers threatening imminent preterm delivery has emerged as an intervention with putative neuroprotective properties. Proposed mechanisms include stabilization of neuronal cellular membranes, reduction of excitotoxic calcium influx through N-methyl-D-aspartate (NMDA) receptor inhibition, preservation of cerebral blood flow autoregulation, and suppression of pro-inflammatory cytokine cascades during perinatal hypoxia-ischemia.

Despite multiple randomized controlled trials, controversy and heterogeneity in clinical trial definitions, inclusion gestational ages, dosing regimens, and primary composite endpoints have persisted. Some trials evaluated magnesium sulphate specifically for fetal neuroprotection, whereas earlier investigations investigated it as a tocolytic agent or for pre-eclampsia seizure prophylaxis with pediatric neurodevelopment recorded as a secondary outcome. The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to determine the quantitative effect of antenatal magnesium sulphate compared with placebo or no treatment on the incidence of cerebral palsy in children assessed up to 2 years' corrected age.

---

## Search and Screening Strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across international biomedical databases (including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials [CENTRAL]) from inception through recent updates. The search strategy incorporated Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms reflecting:
1. *Population*: Pregnant women at risk of preterm delivery (e.g., "preterm birth", "premature infant", "preterm labor", "premature rupture of membranes").
2. *Intervention*: Antenatal magnesium sulphate (e.g., "magnesium sulfate", "MgSO4").
3. *Comparator*: Placebo (saline solution) or no treatment.
4. *Outcome*: Cerebral palsy, pediatric motor dysfunction, neurodevelopmental disability, assessed up to 2 years' corrected age.

The selection process was conducted according to PRISMA guidelines. Dual independent screening of titles, abstracts, and full texts was performed. Eligible investigations were required to be randomized controlled trials comparing antenatal magnesium sulphate versus placebo or no treatment and reporting verified neurological assessments in children up to 24 months corrected age. From the pipeline processing, 6 studies were retrieved and reviewed in detail, yielding 5 distinct study-level effect sizes suitable for analytical extraction.

## Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Small-study effects and potential reporting biases were formally investigated:
* **Egger's Linear Regression Test**: Intercept = $-0.26$, standard error = $0.38$, $p = 0.5137$.
* **Duval and Tweedie Trim-and-Fill Procedure**: Imputed missing studies ($k_0$) = 0. The adjusted effect size remained identical to the unadjusted estimate ($\text{adjusted } g = -0.1625$).

Egger's test revealed no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry ($p = 0.5137$), and the trim-and-fill method imputed zero missing studies ($k_0 = 0$). Consequently, the risk of publication bias is low, indicating that the pooled results are unlikely to be substantially influenced by selective reporting or small-study effects.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis investigated the neuroprotective efficacy of antenatal magnesium sulphate compared with placebo or no treatment for reducing cerebral palsy in children evaluated up to 2 years' corrected age. 

In the primary pooled analysis of $k = 4$ randomized controlled trials encompassing 4,693 pediatric evaluations, the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was $0.75$ (95% CI $[0.550, 1.011]$, $p = 0.0590$). Although the point estimate indicates a 25% relative reduction in the incidence of cerebral palsy among infants exposed to antenatal magnesium sulphate, the upper limit of the 95% confidence interval marginally encompasses $1.011$. Therefore, the result favors neither arm under formal categorical interpretation.

These findings mirror international guideline syntheses (such as the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists [ACOG], the Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists [RCOG], and the World Health Organization [WHO]). While clinical practice guidelines widely recommend antenatal magnesium sulphate prior to 30 to 32 weeks of gestation based on composite outcomes of "death or cerebral palsy" and secondary reductions in moderate-to-severe motor deficits, isolated endpoints of cerebral palsy alone frequently encounter sample-size constraints due to low baseline incidence rates (approximately 4% to 7% in extreme preterms). 

Importantly, between-study heterogeneity in our analysis was nil ($I^2 = 0.0\%$, $\tau^2 = 0.000$, $p = 0.5903$). This complete absence of observed heterogeneity reinforces that differences in dosing strategies (e.g., 4 g bolus alone versus 4 g bolus plus maintenance infusion) and obstetric indications across the major trials (e.g., Australasian Collaborative Trial of Magnesium Sulphate [ACTOMgSO4], PREMAG, and the BEAM trial) did not introduce conflicting directions of effect. Furthermore, formal testing via Egger's regression ($p = 0.5137$) and trim-and-fill analysis demonstrated low risk of publication bias.

---

## Limitations

Several methodological and clinical limitations must be considered:
1. **Statistical Power for Rare Binary Events**: Cerebral palsy is a relatively uncommon outcome even among very preterm cohorts. With an event rate hovering around 4–6%, individual clinical trials and meta-analytic syntheses require massive sample sizes (often exceeding 5,000–6,000 children) to demonstrate statistical significance for isolated motor deficits independent of competing mortality.
2. **Competing Risk of Perinatal Mortality**: Because magnesium sulphate does not significantly increase or decrease overall pediatric mortality, differential survival of extremely fragile micro-preterm infants could dilute the observed neuroprotective effect against cerebral palsy.
3. **Metric Harmonization Discrepancy**: One identified trial ($n = 680$) reported an Odds Ratio rather than a Risk Ratio and could not be pooled natively within the 4-study RR model.
4. **Follow-Up Duration**: Neurodevelopmental outcomes in this synthesis were restricted to assessments up to 2 years' corrected age. Although neurological examinations at 18 to 24 months are standardized, milder forms of cerebral palsy, executive dysfunction, and subtle motor impairments may not be accurately diagnosed until school age (5 to 8 years).

---

## Conclusion

Antenatal magnesium sulphate administration to women at risk of preterm birth demonstrated a clinically meaningful point-estimate reduction in the risk of cerebral palsy in offspring evaluated up to 2 years' corrected age (pooled Risk Ratio = $0.75$). However, because the 95% confidence interval spans unity ($[0.550, 1.011]$, $p = 0.0590$), the primary pooled effect strictly favors neither arm. Heterogeneity across trials was virtually nonexistent ($I^2 = 0.0\%$). These findings support continued clinical implementation within evidence-based guidelines while emphasizing the need for extended neurodevelopmental follow-up into middle childhood to capture the full spectrum of long-term motor and cognitive function.
