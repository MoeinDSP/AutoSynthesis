# Antenatal Magnesium Sulphate for Fetal Neuroprotection and Cerebral Palsy at 18 Months to 2 Years' Corrected Age: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Placebo-Controlled Randomised Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Antenatal magnesium sulphate given to women at risk of preterm birth has been proposed as a fetal neuroprotective agent. The most clinically consequential question is whether it reduces the number of children diagnosed with cerebral palsy at early childhood follow-up.

**Objective.** To determine whether antenatal magnesium sulphate administered for fetal neuroprotection, compared with placebo, reduces the number of children diagnosed with cerebral palsy at the first follow-up reported between 18 months and 2 years' corrected age.

**Methods.** We systematically identified randomised placebo-controlled trials of antenatal magnesium sulphate given for fetal neuroprotection in women at risk of preterm birth. The outcome was the number of children diagnosed with cerebral palsy (each trial's own diagnostic definition) out of each trial's own denominator for that outcome (children with cerebral palsy data preferred; otherwise surviving children assessed, live births with data, or number randomised). Children, not mothers or pregnancies, were the unit of analysis; where a trial used more than one magnesium dose regimen, arms were combined into a single magnesium group and the placebo group was used once. School-age follow-up was not used. Risk ratios (RR) were pooled on the native risk-ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Six records were taken forward from screening, of which five randomised comparisons contributed usable cerebral-palsy data at 18 months to 2 years' corrected age (k = 5; 5,075 children). The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.707 (95% CI 0.552 to 0.906; p = 0.0061), computed with native risk-ratio pooling (native_pooling_used = true), i.e. the effect measure is a confirmed native-scale risk ratio rather than a standardized fallback. The automated direction field for this analysis returned "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"; under the review's pre-specified convention — that an RR below 1 denotes fewer children with cerebral palsy after magnesium sulphate — the point estimate and confidence interval lie entirely below 1. Heterogeneity was absent (Q = 4.459, df = 4, p = 0.3474; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.000001), despite one small trial (n = 59) whose standardized effect pointed in the opposite direction to the other four. Egger's regression indicated marked funnel asymmetry (intercept −0.4976, p = 0.0002, overall small-study-effect risk rated "high"), whereas trim-and-fill imputed only one hypothetical missing study (k₀ = 1) with little change to the adjusted estimate.

**Conclusions.** Pooled evidence from five placebo-controlled randomised trials in 5,075 children gives a statistically significant pooled risk ratio of 0.707 for cerebral palsy at 18 months to 2 years' corrected age following antenatal magnesium sulphate, with no detectable between-study heterogeneity. Because the automated direction classifier did not assign the result to an arm, and because Egger's test signals possible small-study effects, the estimate should be read as a consistent but cautiously interpreted reduction of roughly 29% in relative terms, plausibly an upper bound on benefit, and should be confirmed against trial-level absolute event data and longer-term follow-up.

## 1. Introduction

Cerebral palsy is the commonest cause of severe physical disability in childhood, and preterm birth is its single strongest antecedent. The inverse gradient between gestational age at birth and the prevalence of cerebral palsy has motivated a long search for interventions that can be given to the mother before delivery and that act on the fetal brain. Magnesium sulphate — long used in obstetrics for eclampsia prophylaxis and, in some settings, for tocolysis — became a leading candidate after observational work reported lower rates of cerebral palsy among very low birthweight infants exposed in utero. Biologically plausible mechanisms include blockade of the N-methyl-D-aspartate receptor and attenuation of excitotoxic calcium influx, stabilisation of cerebral blood flow and membrane potential, and reduction of oxidative and inflammatory injury in the developing white matter.

Several randomised placebo-controlled trials were subsequently mounted, with differing primary aims (neuroprotection, tocolysis, prevention of preeclampsia-related morbidity), differing gestational-age entry criteria, and differing magnesium regimens (loading dose alone versus loading plus maintenance, and in some trials more than one dose arm). Their individual results ranged from a clear reduction in cerebral palsy to no effect, and one small trial reported a result in the opposite direction. The question therefore remains a quantitative-synthesis question rather than a single-trial question.

This review addresses one precisely specified comparison: in women at risk of preterm birth, does antenatal magnesium sulphate given for fetal neuroprotection, compared with placebo, reduce the number of children diagnosed with cerebral palsy at the first follow-up reported between 18 months and 2 years' corrected age? Restricting to this early-childhood window — and explicitly excluding school-age follow-up — keeps the outcome construct homogeneous (a toddler-age neurological diagnosis of abnormal tone with loss of motor function, or an equivalent overall neurological diagnosis by a paediatric assessor) and avoids mixing it with later, differently-ascertained measures of motor function.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Women at risk of preterm birth (threatened or planned preterm delivery, preterm prelabour rupture of membranes, or other indications for imminent preterm birth), and their children.

**Intervention.** Antenatal magnesium sulphate administered with fetal neuroprotection as an aim of the trial. Where a trial randomised participants to more than one magnesium dose regimen, all magnesium arms were combined into a single magnesium group.

**Comparator.** Placebo. The placebo group was used once in the analysis, irrespective of the number of magnesium arms it was compared against, so that no control child was counted twice.

**Outcome.** The number of children diagnosed with cerebral palsy, using each trial's own diagnostic definition (for example, abnormalities of tone with loss of motor function, or an overall neurological diagnosis made by a paediatric assessor), out of the number of children assessed or counted in that arm at the first follow-up reported between 18 months and 2 years' corrected age. The denominator was the paper's own denominator for this outcome, in the following order of preference: children with data on cerebral palsy; otherwise surviving children assessed; otherwise live births with data; otherwise the number randomised, with the basis stated.

**Unit of analysis.** Children, not mothers or pregnancies. Multiple births contributed each child to the denominator where the trial reported the outcome per child.

**Design.** Randomised controlled trials with a placebo comparator.

**Exclusions.** Narrative and systematic reviews, duplicate records, trials without a placebo arm, trials reporting only school-age (or later) follow-up, and secondary reports of an already-included trial where inclusion would double-count the same randomised children.

### 2.2 Screening outcome

Six records were carried forward from screening as potentially eligible trial reports. Five of these six provided a usable cerebral-palsy count and denominator within the 18-month to 2-year corrected-age window and were included in the quantitative synthesis; the sixth did not yield an extractable effect size for this specific outcome and timepoint (k = 5 effect sizes from 6 retained study records, per the pipeline summary). This single discrepancy between the number of retained study records (6) and the number of pooled effect sizes (5) is the only screening-to-analysis attrition in the dataset and is reported here rather than being silently absorbed into the pooled k.

No separate `meta_analysis_groups` structure was produced for this dataset: all five contributing comparisons address the same outcome construct (clinician-diagnosed cerebral palsy) at the same timepoint window (first follow-up between 18 months and 2 years' corrected age), and are therefore reported as a single synthesis with a single pooled estimate, a single heterogeneity assessment, and a single small-study-effects assessment.

Two features of this evidence base are important for interpretation. First, the sample sizes are extremely unequal: the three largest comparisons (study_5_R5, study_2_R2, study_3_R3) contribute 4,336 of the 5,075 children (85.4%), while the smallest (study_4_R4) contributes 59 children (1.2%). Because the trials differed in their primary objectives, gestational-age windows and magnesium dose regimens, and because the cerebral palsy diagnosis was made according to each trial's own protocol, some clinical and methodological diversity is present by construction. ## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure and scale

The pre-specified and analysed effect measure for this synthesis is the **Risk Ratio (RR)** (`measure_label`: "Risk Ratio (RR)"; `effect_measure`: RR; `computation_scale`: RR; `effect_measure_source`: explicit user request, with no recorded conflict). Critically, **native pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used` = true): the pooled result is a genuine, confirmed native-scale risk ratio computed on the risk-ratio scale, **not** a standardized fallback converted from another metric. It should be named "pooled risk ratio" throughout and must not be described as a Hedges' *g*.

A note on the per-study column in Section 3 is necessary to avoid confusion. The individual study entries in the analytic dataset are carried as standardized mean-difference-type values (Hedges' *g*) with their own confidence intervals; these are useful for ranking the trials by direction and relative magnitude and for the small-study-effects diagnostics, but they are *not* the quantities that were pooled. The synthesis itself was performed on the native risk-ratio scale. Readers should therefore read the per-study *g* values as directional/relative indicators and the pooled value as a risk ratio; the two are on different scales and are not numerically interchangeable.

### 4.2 Direction convention

Under the review's pre-specified convention, a risk ratio **below 1** means that fewer children developed cerebral palsy after antenatal magnesium sulphate than after placebo — that is, a result favouring magnesium sulphate. A negative Hedges' *g* likewise indicates fewer cerebral palsy diagnoses in the magnesium arm.

Separately, the analysis carries an automated `favors` field, which for this synthesis returned: **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**. In accordance with reporting policy, the arm favoured by the result is stated using this field, which declines to assign the result to either arm because, for a ratio measure, favourability depends on which event the numerator counted. We therefore report both facts side by side without substituting our own inference: the automated direction classifier assigned the result to *neither* arm, while the point estimate and its entire confidence interval lie below 1, which under the stated convention for this outcome (cerebral palsy diagnoses counted as the event) corresponds to fewer affected children in the magnesium group.

The pooled risk ratio is **0.707 (95% CI 0.552 to 0.906; p = 0.0061)**. In relative terms this corresponds to a 29.3% lower risk of a cerebral palsy diagnosis at 18 months to 2 years' corrected age in the magnesium group than in the placebo group, with a confidence interval compatible with reductions as large as 44.8% and as small as 9.4%. The interval excludes 1.0 and the two-sided p-value of 0.0061 indicates that the pooled estimate is statistically distinguishable from no effect at conventional thresholds.

Two cautions attach to the clinical reading of this number. First, as noted, the automated direction field did not assign the result to an arm; we therefore state the relative-risk reduction descriptively under the review's event-coding convention rather than asserting an arm-level winner on the classifier's behalf. Second, a risk ratio alone does not convey absolute benefit. Cerebral palsy at this age is an uncommon outcome in trial populations, so a 29% relative reduction translates into a small absolute difference and a large number needed to treat; the absolute risk difference and number needed to treat should be read off the trials' own event counts rather than inferred from the pooled ratio, and are not reported here because the analytic dataset supplied for this synthesis carries pooled ratios and standardized per-study effects rather than arm-level event counts.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was negligible by every available index:

- **Cochran's Q** = 4.459 on 4 degrees of freedom, p = 0.3474 — no statistical evidence that the observed dispersion of effects exceeds what sampling error alone would produce.
- **I²** = 0.0% — none of the observed variability in effects is attributable to true between-study differences rather than chance.
- **τ²** = 0.000001 — the estimated between-study variance is effectively zero, so the random-effects and fixed-effect solutions coincide for practical purposes and the weighting is driven almost entirely by within-study precision.

This is a strong consistency result and it survives the inclusion of the discordant 59-child comparison (study_4_R4). The explanation is that study_4_R4's effect, although large in magnitude, is estimated with very wide uncertainty (g CI 0.549 to 1.644) and carries minimal weight; Q weights each deviation by its precision, so an imprecise outlier contributes little to the heterogeneity statistic. The practical implication is that the pooled risk ratio of 0.707 is not an average across qualitatively different effects — the four substantial comparisons are mutually compatible — but the absence of measured heterogeneity should not be over-read as evidence that the clinical populations, dose regimens and diagnostic protocols were equivalent. With k = 5, both Q and I² have low power to detect moderate heterogeneity, and τ² estimated from five studies is itself imprecise. A graphical summary of the heterogeneity diagnostics is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 7. Moderators

No moderator (meta-regression or subgroup) analysis was pre-specified or is supported by the available data, and none was fitted as a confirmatory analysis. Three reasons apply.

First, with k = 5 comparisons, meta-regression has essentially no power: standard guidance requires on the order of ten studies per covariate, and fitting even a single moderator here would consume one of four residual degrees of freedom while producing an uninterpretably wide coefficient.

Second, I² = 0.0% and τ² ≈ 0 mean there is no detected between-study variance for a moderator to explain. A covariate can only account for heterogeneity that exists in the estimate; here there is none to partition.

Third, the trial-level covariates that would be clinically most interesting — gestational age at randomisation, total magnesium dose and whether a maintenance infusion was given, time from administration to birth, singleton versus multiple pregnancy, and the precise diagnostic criteria used by the paediatric assessor — are not available as structured variables in the analytic dataset supplied for this synthesis, so they cannot be tested even exploratorily.

A bubble chart relating effect size to study size is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`. It should be read descriptively only: the single visible pattern is that the one comparison with a positive (away-from-magnesium) effect is also by far the smallest, which is a small-study-effects observation (Section 8) rather than a moderator finding. Any statement that effect size varies systematically with gestational age, dose or population would be unsupported by these data.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | −0.4976 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.0002 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 1 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (as reported by the routine) | 0.1221 |
| Overall small-study-effects risk rating | **High** |

Egger's regression provides strong statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept −0.4976, p = 0.0002), consistent with the "high" overall risk rating. Taken at face value, this means the pooled estimate may be inflated by small-study effects or selective reporting, and the magnitude of benefit implied by RR = 0.707 may be optimistic.

The two diagnostics are, however, discordant. Trim-and-fill imputed only **one** hypothetical missing study (k₀ = 1), implying that the asymmetry-adjusted estimate would differ little from the unadjusted one. (The adjusted value returned by the routine, 0.1221, is expressed on the routine's internal standardized/log working scale and must not be misread as a risk ratio of 0.12.) Such discordance between Egger's test and trim-and-fill is common and has well-understood causes: trim-and-fill performs poorly in small sets and under asymmetry that is driven by one extreme point rather than by a smooth suppression gradient, and Egger's test with k = 5 is itself fragile and known to produce small p-values when a single low-precision study sits far from the pooled effect — which is exactly the structure here (study_4_R4, n = 59, g = +1.097).

It is also important to recognise that funnel asymmetry is not synonymous with publication bias. Asymmetry can arise from genuine small-study differences — lower methodological quality, different populations, different dose regimens, or outcome ascertainment that is less rigorous in small single-centre trials — rather than from suppression of negative results. In the present dataset the asymmetry runs in the *unusual* direction for a publication-bias story: the small trial reports a result *unfavourable* to the intervention while the large trials report favourable ones, which is the opposite of the pattern expected if small unfavourable studies were being suppressed.

The appropriate conclusion is the cautious one carried in the analysis interpretation: the pooled estimate should be treated as a probable **upper bound** on the true benefit, and robustness should be gauged with sensitivity analyses — PET-PEESE, selection models, or restriction to the larger and lower-risk-of-bias trials — before the magnitude (as opposed to the direction) of the pooled risk ratio is relied upon. With only five comparisons, all such diagnostics are underpowered and none of them can confirm or exclude bias.

## 9. Discussion

Across five placebo-controlled randomised comparisons involving 5,075 children, antenatal magnesium sulphate given with fetal neuroprotective intent was associated with a pooled risk ratio for cerebral palsy at 18 months to 2 years' corrected age of 0.707 (95% CI 0.552 to 0.906; p = 0.0061), with no detectable between-study heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.000001; Q = 4.459, p = 0.3474). The result is a confirmed native-scale risk ratio, not a standardized approximation.

Three features give this synthesis more interpretive weight than its small k might suggest. First, the consistency is real: four of the five comparisons, representing 98.8% of the included children, point in the same direction, and the only dissenting comparison is a 59-child study whose confidence interval is so wide that it is compatible with a broad range of truths. Second, the heterogeneity statistics are uniformly null, so the pooled estimate behaves as a summary of one common effect rather than as an average over disparate effects. Third, the confidence interval excludes the null across its whole range, so the inference does not rest on a borderline p-value.

Set against this are three substantive cautions. The first is the direction classification: the automated `favors` field returned "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)", and we have reported that verbatim rather than overriding it. For a ratio measure the favoured arm depends on whether the numerator counted cerebral palsy cases or cerebral-palsy-free children; under this review's explicit convention the event counted was a cerebral palsy diagnosis, so RR < 1 denotes fewer affected children after magnesium, but the classifier's refusal to assign an arm is a genuine limitation of the automated output and readers should verify the event coding against the source trials before translating 0.707 into a clinical recommendation.

The second caution is the small-study-effects signal. Egger's p = 0.0002 with a "high" risk rating is a strong flag, even though trim-and-fill implies negligible adjustment and even though the direction of the asymmetry is atypical for classic publication bias. The honest reading is that the magnitude of benefit is less secure than its direction.

The third caution concerns the outcome itself. A clinician-made diagnosis of cerebral palsy at 18 months to 2 years' corrected age is an early diagnosis. Early tone abnormalities can resolve, and milder impairments can emerge later, so toddler-age ascertainment is neither perfectly sensitive nor perfectly specific for the eventual diagnosis. This review deliberately excluded school-age follow-up to keep the outcome construct homogeneous, but that restriction means the present result speaks to early-childhood diagnosis only and cannot be extrapolated to durable motor outcome or to functional severity. Nor does the pooled risk ratio address mortality, the competing risk that most complicates neonatal neuroprotection trials, or maternal adverse effects of magnesium administration.

Finally, the relative scale can mislead about clinical scale. Cerebral palsy is uncommon even in preterm cohorts, so a 29% relative reduction corresponds to a modest absolute reduction and a substantial number needed to treat. Decision-makers should combine the relative estimate reported here with their own population's baseline risk and with the trials' arm-level event counts, which are not part of the analytic dataset underlying this report.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No structured `validation_flags` were returned for this analysis. For transparency, the following data-handling points are nevertheless stated plainly, as they bear directly on how the numbers above should be read:

- **Record-to-effect-size attrition.** Six study records were retained through screening but only five contributed a usable effect size for cerebral palsy at 18 months to 2 years' corrected age (n_studies = 6, n_effect_sizes = 5). One retained record therefore did not yield extractable data for this outcome and timepoint and contributes nothing to the pooled estimate.
- **Mixed scales within the dataset.** The pooled result is a native risk ratio, whereas the per-study entries in the dataset are carried as standardized (Hedges' *g*) values. These are not on a common scale and have not been merged; the per-study values are used only for direction, relative magnitude and the asymmetry diagnostics.
- **Unassigned direction.** The automated `favors` field returned "neither", i.e. the analysis did not assign the result to the magnesium or the placebo arm. This is reported as returned and has not been overwritten with an inferred direction.
- **Trim-and-fill adjusted value scale.** The trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (0.1221) is reported on the routine's internal working scale and is not a risk ratio; it is included only to document that the adjustment implied by k₀ = 1 is minimal.
- **No subgroup or moderator data.** Gestational age, dose regimen, singleton/multiple status and diagnostic criteria are not available as structured covariates, so no moderator analysis was possible and none should be inferred from the bubble chart.
- **Double-counting safeguards.** By protocol, multiple magnesium dose arms within a trial were combined into one magnesium group with the placebo group used once, and children rather than mothers or pregnancies were counted, so no participant contributes twice to the pooled denominator of 5,075.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Few comparisons.** With k = 5, every secondary statistic in this report is fragile. τ² is imprecisely estimated, Q and I² have low power to detect moderate true heterogeneity, Egger's regression is unreliable, and trim-and-fill is near-uninformative. The pooled point estimate is the most stable quantity here; everything around it is weakly determined.
2. **Dominance by large trials.** Three comparisons supply 85.4% of the children and, through precision weighting, nearly all of the information in the pooled estimate. The result is effectively a summary of those trials; the two smaller comparisons neither confirm nor contradict it with meaningful force.
3. **Unresolved asymmetry signal.** Egger's test and trim-and-fill disagree. We cannot determine from these data whether the asymmetry reflects selective reporting, genuine differences between small and large trials, or an artefact of one extreme low-precision point.
4. **Unassigned effect direction.** The automated direction field did not nominate a favoured arm, so the clinical direction rests on the review's stated event-coding convention rather than on a confirmed classification.
5. **Outcome definition heterogeneity.** Each trial applied its own diagnostic criteria for cerebral palsy, and denominators followed each paper's own convention (children with cerebral palsy data, surviving children assessed, live births with data, or number randomised). Differential loss to follow-up between arms, if present, is not quantifiable from the available data and could bias the pooled ratio in either direction.
6. **Early timepoint only.** By design, only the first follow-up between 18 months and 2 years' corrected age was used, and school-age follow-up was excluded. Conclusions about persistence of effect, functional severity, or later-emerging impairment cannot be drawn.
7. **No absolute-risk or competing-outcome data.** Arm-level event counts, mortality, and maternal adverse effects are outside the analytic dataset; the risk ratio alone cannot support a benefit–harm judgement.
8. **No formal risk-of-bias assessment.** Trial-level domain ratings (allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessors, completeness of follow-up) were not available in the supplied data, so the certainty of the body of evidence could not be graded formally (e.g. by GRADE), and the "high" small-study-effects rating is the only bias-related signal reported.

## 12. Conclusion

In women at risk of preterm birth, antenatal magnesium sulphate given for fetal neuroprotection was associated with a pooled risk ratio for cerebral palsy of **0.707 (95% CI 0.552 to 0.906; p = 0.0061)** at the first follow-up between 18 months and 2 years' corrected age, pooled across **5 randomised placebo-controlled comparisons in 5,075 children** using native risk-ratio pooling under a REML random-effects model. Between-study heterogeneity was absent (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.000001; Q = 4.459, p = 0.3474), indicating that the contributing trials are mutually consistent.

The automated direction field for this analysis returned "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"; under the review's explicit convention — cerebral palsy diagnoses counted as the event, so that RR below 1 means fewer affected children after magnesium — the entire confidence interval lies below 1, corresponding to an approximately 29% lower relative risk. Evidence of funnel asymmetry (Egger's p = 0.0002, risk rated high), only partly offset by a minimal trim-and-fill adjustment (k₀ = 1), means the magnitude should be regarded as a probable upper bound rather than a precise estimate.

Overall, the direction and consistency of the evidence support antenatal magnesium sulphate as neuroprotective against early-childhood cerebral palsy diagnosis, while the small number of trials, the dominance of a few large studies, the unassigned direction classification and the asymmetry signal mean the size of that benefit remains uncertain. Future work should report absolute event counts and risk differences alongside ratios, pre-register and report longer-term motor outcomes to complement toddler-age diagnosis, and — given that only five comparisons address this question — prioritise individual participant data synthesis over further aggregate pooling, since IPD would permit the dose, gestational-age and timing analyses that the present evidence base cannot support.
