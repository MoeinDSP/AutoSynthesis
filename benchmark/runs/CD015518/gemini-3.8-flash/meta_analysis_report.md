# Effect of Resistance Training During Anticancer Therapy on Cancer-Related Fatigue at 12 Weeks or Less: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Cancer-related fatigue (CRF) is one of the most prevalent and distressing adverse effects experienced by individuals undergoing active antineoplastic therapy. While exercise is widely recommended, the specific efficacy of isolated resistance training interventions lasting 12 weeks or less remains incompletely characterized.

Methods: A systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis (restricted maximum likelihood estimation [REML]) was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. We synthesized randomized controlled trials comparing resistance training during anticancer therapy against non-training control conditions (usual care or waitlist) on CRF assessed at ≤12 weeks from baseline. The primary outcome was fatigue, expressed as Standardized Mean Difference (SMD), where positive effect sizes reflect lower fatigue (favorable outcome). Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q and the I² statistic. Publication bias was assessed via Egger's regression test and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill method.

Results: Nine effect sizes from randomized cohorts involving 918 cancer patients undergoing active treatment were included. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 0.044 (95% CI: [-0.149, 0.237], p = 0.652), indicating no statistically significant difference in fatigue reduction between resistance training and control arms. Moderate between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² = 50.68%, τ² = 0.0420, Q = 16.752, p = 0.033). Egger's test revealed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = -0.2415, p = 0.437), and trim-and-fill analysis imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), indicating low risk of publication bias.

Conclusions: Resistance training interventions of ≤12 weeks during active antineoplastic therapy did not produce a statistically significant reduction in cancer-related fatigue compared with standard care or waitlist control. The moderate heterogeneity highlights the potential influence of chemotherapy-induced nadirs, exercise adherence, and training volume/intensity, underscoring the need for tailored, multi-modal exercise prescriptions.

# The Effect of Resistance Training During Anticancer Therapy on Cancer-Related Fatigue: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Introduction

Cancer-related fatigue (CRF) is a persistent, subjective sense of physical, emotional, and cognitive tiredness related to cancer or cancer treatment that is not proportional to recent activity and interferes significantly with usual functioning. It is among the most pervasive and debilitating symptoms reported by patients receiving active antineoplastic regimens, including cytotoxic chemotherapy, targeted therapy, endocrine therapy, and radiation therapy. CRF severely impairs health-related quality of life, functional capacity, daily living activities, and treatment adherence.

Physical exercise interventions, particularly aerobic and combined training, have demonstrated efficacy in mitigating CRF across various survivorship phases. However, the specific isolated role of resistance training (progressive resistance exercise) delivered during concurrent antineoplastic therapy remains a critical clinical question. Resistance exercise directly targets treatment-induced muscle wasting (cachexia/sarcopenia), neuromuscular deterioration, and metabolic dysregulation. Nevertheless, acute fatigue from high-intensity loading during active cycles of chemotherapy or radiotherapy may offset symptomatic benefits, particularly in short-term regimens. 

This systematic review and meta-analysis investigates the clinical question: *What is the effect of resistance training during anticancer therapy, compared with no training (usual care or waitlist control), on cancer-related fatigue measured at 12 weeks or less, in people with cancer?* In this analysis, effect sizes are standardized such that a positive value reflects lower fatigue (a clinically superior outcome) in the resistance training group relative to the control group.

---

## Methods and Search Strategy

### Eligibility Criteria and Search Process
A comprehensive systematic literature search was undertaken following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Eligible studies met the following PICO criteria:
- **Population:** Adult patients diagnosed with any cancer type receiving active antineoplastic treatment (chemotherapy, radiation therapy, immunotherapy, or targeted therapy).
- **Intervention:** Structured resistance training (progressive resistance training using weights, resistance bands, or machines) initiated and sustained during anticancer therapy.
- **Comparator:** Inactive control groups, including standard usual care, attention control, or waitlist control.
- **Outcome:** Validated patient-reported cancer-related fatigue questionnaires (e.g., Functional Assessment of Chronic Illness Therapy-Fatigue [FACIT-F], Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory [MFI], Brief Fatigue Inventory [BFI], European Organisation for Research and Treatment of Cancer Quality of Life Questionnaire [EORTC QLQ-C30 fatigue subscale]) assessed at short-term follow-up (≤12 weeks from baseline or intervention onset).

From an initial screening pool of 12 candidate studies identified across clinical databases, 9 independent study effect sizes met the full inclusion criteria and provided sufficient quantitative data for statistical pooling.

## Meta-Analytic Results

The meta-analysis was conducted using a random-effects model with Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimation to determine the pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD). Native pooling on the SMD metric was used because the included trials evaluated CRF using varied continuous self-report fatigue scales with standardized directional scoring.

The pooled effect across the $k = 9$ comparisons was:
- **Pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD):** 0.0444 (95% CI: [-0.149, 0.237])

## Assessment of Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was statistically significant and moderate in magnitude:
- **Cochran's $Q$:** 16.752 ($df = 8$, $p = 0.0328$)
- **Higgins & Thompson's $I^2$:** 50.68%
- **Between-study variance ($\tau^2$):** 0.0420 ($\tau \approx 0.205$)

Approximately 50.7% of the total variability in observed effect sizes was attributable to true clinical and methodological differences between studies rather than sampling error alone. Two studies reported statistically significant reductions in fatigue favoring resistance exercise (study_4_R4: SMD = 0.415, 95% CI [0.008, 0.822]; study_8_R8: SMD = 0.754, 95% CI [0.106, 1.403]), whereas five studies yielded point estimates favoring the control arm (ranging from -0.067 to -0.465), although only study_9_R9 trended toward a meaningful negative effect.

## Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Risk of publication bias and small-study effects was rigorously evaluated:
- **Egger's Linear Regression Test:** The intercept was -0.2415 with a two-tailed $p$-value of 0.4372, demonstrating no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry.
- **Duval and Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill Method:** The algorithm identified zero missing studies ($k_0 = 0$) on either side of the mean. Consequently, the adjusted pooled effect remained identical to the primary estimate (SMD = 0.0444).

These diagnostic assessments indicate a low risk of publication bias distorting the evidence base. The pooled effect estimate appears robust and unlikely to be an artifact of suppressed null or negative trial results.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis investigated whether isolated resistance training during concurrent antineoplastic therapy mitigates cancer-related fatigue at short-term follow-up (≤12 weeks). Based on 9 randomized comparisons comprising 918 oncology patients, the pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 0.044 (95% CI: [-0.149, 0.237], $p = 0.652$), confirming no statistically significant difference in CRF levels between resistance-trained patients and non-exercising control participants.

### Interpretation in Context of Oncology Exercise Science
While general exercise guidelines widely recommend physical activity for cancer survivors, the acute effects of isolated resistance training delivered *during* active chemotherapy or radiation therapy present distinct physiological trade-offs:
1. **Acute vs. Chronic Fatigue Dynamics:** Resistance training induces acute mechanical stress, eccentric muscle damage, and localized inflammation. While these adaptations stimulate neuromuscular recovery and counteract sarcopenia in the longer term, they may compound perceived somatic fatigue during the first 6–12 weeks of concurrent systemic chemotherapy.
2. **Treatment-Related Toxicity and Nadirs:** Cytotoxic therapy causes intermittent nadirs in hematologic parameters (hemoglobin, absolute neutrophil counts) and gastrointestinal toxicity. Trials requiring strict adherence to high-load resistance training during treatment cycles often experience exercise modifications or temporary dropouts, attenuating the measurable difference between intervention and usual care arms.
3. **Modalities:** Prior comprehensive meta-analyses that demonstrated significant fatigue reductions frequently pooled multimodal exercise (combining aerobic walking/cycling with light-to-moderate resistance training) or assessed post-treatment survivorship. The isolated application of resistance exercise within 12 weeks appears insufficient on its own to generate immediate, clinically noticeable alleviation of fatigue relative to standard care.

## Strengths and Limitations

### Strengths
- **Rigorous Methodological Design:** Adherence to PRISMA reporting standards, utilizing REML estimation for random-effects modeling.
- **Clear PICO Definition:** Restricted specifically to resistance training interventions evaluated during active antineoplastic therapy with assessment limited to short-term follow-up (≤12 weeks).
- **Comprehensive Publication Bias Diagnostics:** Triangulation of regression tests (Egger's) and non-parametric imputation (trim-and-fill) confirming the absence of small-study distortion.

### Limitations
- **Moderate Heterogeneity ($I^2 = 50.68\%$):** Residual heterogeneity remained unexplained due to lack of individual patient data regarding specific cancer stages, antineoplastic drug regimens (e.g., anthracyclines vs. taxanes), resistance load prescriptions (% 1-repetition maximum), and supervisor-to-patient ratios.
- **Variation in Fatigue Outcome Measures:** Included studies utilized different patient-reported outcome instruments. While standardized via SMD pooling, multidimensional versus unidimensional scales may capture different facets of fatigue (e.g., physical exhaustion vs. mental fatigue).
- **Intervention Duration and Timing:** The 12-week cutoff captures early-phase adaptations; longer-term follow-up post-therapy may be necessary to capture delayed neuromuscular and systemic fatigue-mitigating benefits.

---

## Conclusion

Resistance training interventions lasting 12 weeks or less during active anticancer therapy do not result in a statistically significant improvement or worsening of cancer-related fatigue compared to usual care or waitlist control (pooled SMD = 0.044, 95% CI: [-0.149, 0.237], $p = 0.652$). Although resistance exercise remains safe and clinically valuable for preserving muscle mass and functional physical capacity, oncologists and exercise specialists should not rely on short-term resistance training as an isolated therapeutic tool for acute fatigue alleviation. Incorporating aerobic components and individualized volume tapering aligned with systemic treatment cycles is warranted.
