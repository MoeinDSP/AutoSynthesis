# Resistance Training During Anticancer Therapy for Cancer-Related Fatigue at 12 Weeks or Less: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Cancer-related fatigue is a common adverse consequence of cancer and anticancer therapy. This review evaluated resistance training during anticancer therapy, compared with no training, for fatigue assessed at 12 weeks or less.

**Methods:** Eligible participants were people with cancer receiving anticancer therapy. The intervention was resistance training delivered during therapy; comparators were usual care, wait-list, or attention control without structured exercise. Eligible outcomes were validated self-report, clinician-rated, or performance-linked measures of cancer-related fatigue assessed no later than 12 weeks. To prevent double-counting, the prespecified outcome hierarchy was the eligible time point closest to 12 weeks, followed by the study-designated primary fatigue instrument. Standardized Mean Differences (SMDs) were pooled using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The supplied pipeline identified 12 studies, of which six effect sizes (total N=518) were available for quantitative synthesis.

**Results:** Across six effect sizes, the pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was -0.39430061280582684 (95% CI -1.059 to 0.270; p=0.2448084664194768), designated by the supplied analysis as favoring resistance training during anticancer therapy. The confidence interval included the null. Heterogeneity was considerable (Q=79.291, df=5, recorded p=0.0; I²=92.05%; τ²=0.625182). Egger’s intercept was -0.2037 (p=0.9004), but trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study and changed the adjusted pooled estimate to -0.1314; publication-bias risk was therefore classified as moderate.

**Conclusions:** The point estimate was designated as favoring resistance training, but the pooled result was not statistically significant and was highly heterogeneous. Evidence is insufficient to establish a consistent short-term reduction in cancer-related fatigue. Interpretation is further limited by the small meta-analysis, incomplete study-level reporting, and unavailable risk-of-bias information.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) combined six effect sizes (k=6; total N=518). The **pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** was **-0.39430061280582684** (95% CI **-1.059 to 0.270**; p=**0.2448084664194768**). According to the supplied `favors` classification, the point estimate favored resistance training during anticancer therapy. However, the 95% confidence interval crossed the null, and the pooled effect was not statistically significant.

Accordingly, the synthesis does not provide conclusive evidence that resistance training reduces cancer-related fatigue within 12 weeks relative to no training. The wide interval is compatible with materially different average effects, and the very high between-study inconsistency further limits interpretation of the pooled mean.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was considerable:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 79.291 with 5 degrees of freedom;
- **Q-test p-value:** recorded as 0.0, indicating a value below the reporting precision of the analysis rather than a literal probability of zero;
- **I²:** 92.05%;
- **τ²:** 0.625182.

An I² of 92.05% indicates that most observed dispersion was attributed to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error under the model. The large τ² similarly indicates substantial variation in underlying standardized effects. The presence of effects on both sides of the null is consistent with this high heterogeneity. An overall mean should therefore not be interpreted as a uniform treatment effect across cancer populations, treatment settings, or resistance-training programs.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **-0.2037** with **p=0.9004**. Thus, there was no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects by this test. Nevertheless, only six effects were available, so Egger’s test had limited power.

Trim-and-fill estimated **one** potentially missing study (`k0=1`) and produced an adjusted pooled estimate of **-0.1314**. This attenuation relative to the primary pooled SMD shows that the result is sensitive to the assumptions of the trim-and-fill procedure. Trim-and-fill is not proof that a study is missing and can perform poorly when genuine heterogeneity is substantial. Considering the non-significant Egger test, the imputed study, the small number of effects, and I²=92.05%, the supplied analysis appropriately classified publication-bias risk as **moderate**. Publication bias and other selective-reporting processes cannot be excluded.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized six short-term fatigue effects involving 518 participants receiving anticancer therapy. The REML random-effects point estimate was designated as favoring resistance training, but its confidence interval included no effect and the p-value was 0.2448084664194768. The evidence therefore does not establish a statistically reliable average benefit of resistance training over no training for cancer-related fatigue measured at 12 weeks or less.

The most prominent feature of the evidence was substantial heterogeneity. Individual standardized effects ranged from -1.651434 to 0.514701, and I² reached 92.05%. Potential explanations may include differences in cancer diagnosis, treatment phase, fatigue instrument, baseline fatigue burden, resistance-training prescription, intervention adherence, control condition, and assessment timing. These explanations remain hypotheses because the necessary study characteristics were not supplied and no supported moderator analysis was available.

The findings have two practical implications. First, the absence of a statistically significant pooled effect should not be interpreted as proof that resistance training is ineffective. The confidence interval and heterogeneity indicate appreciable uncertainty, and some studies reported relatively large effects. Second, the average point estimate should not be generalized to every patient or program because effects were highly inconsistent. Decisions about resistance training during anticancer treatment should also consider safety, patient preferences, physical function, treatment-related contraindications, and feasibility—outcomes not evaluated in this fatigue-focused analysis.

Future trials should prespecify a primary fatigue instrument and assessment time, report group-level means and dispersion measures for all relevant time points, document adherence and intervention dose, and report adverse events. Future syntheses should examine whether cancer type, concurrent treatment, baseline fatigue severity, training intensity, and exact assessment timing explain heterogeneity. Larger evidence bases will also be needed for credible small-study-effect tests and adequately powered meta-regression.

## Limitations

This review has several important limitations. First, the evidence base was small: only six effects were pooled. Precision, assessment of funnel asymmetry, and exploration of heterogeneity were therefore limited. Second, heterogeneity was extreme (I²=92.05%; τ²=0.625182), making the pooled average an incomplete description of the evidence.

Third, the supplied results lacked a reproducible search strategy and complete PRISMA screening counts, so search completeness and selection processes cannot be independently assessed. Fourth, bibliographic and clinical study characteristics were unavailable, precluding detailed evaluation of applicability and clinical sources of heterogeneity. Fifth, no study-level risk-of-bias assessments or certainty-of-evidence rating were provided. The pooled estimate may therefore be affected by randomization problems, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcomes, outcome-measurement limitations, or selective reporting, but these domains could not be evaluated.

Sixth, the absence of instrument and time-point details prevented independent verification of outcome harmonization and the prespecified selection hierarchy. Finally, trim-and-fill suggested one potentially missing study, while Egger’s test was underpowered; selective dissemination remains plausible despite the non-significant asymmetry test.

## Conclusion

Among people with cancer receiving anticancer therapy, six effect sizes with 518 participants yielded a pooled **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) of -0.39430061280582684** (95% CI -1.059 to 0.270; p=0.2448084664194768), designated as favoring resistance training over no training for cancer-related fatigue assessed at 12 weeks or less. The result was not statistically significant, and heterogeneity was very high (I²=92.05%). The current evidence therefore does not demonstrate a consistent short-term fatigue benefit. Better-reported trials and larger, clinically stratified syntheses are required to determine which patients and resistance-training programs, if any, produce meaningful reductions in fatigue.
