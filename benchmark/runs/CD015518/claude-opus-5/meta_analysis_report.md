# Resistance Training During Anticancer Therapy and Short-Term Cancer-Related Fatigue: A PRISMA-Compliant Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Outcomes at ≤12 Weeks

## Abstract

**Background.** Cancer-related fatigue (CRF) is the most prevalent and disabling symptom reported during anticancer therapy. Resistance (strength) training is widely recommended as a supportive-care intervention, but whether it reduces fatigue within the first weeks of treatment — the window in which treatment-related fatigue typically accumulates — remains uncertain.

**Objective.** To determine whether resistance training delivered during anticancer therapy, compared with no training (usual care, waitlist, or non-exercise control), reduces cancer-related fatigue measured with any validated self-report instrument at a post-intervention or follow-up assessment occurring 12 weeks or less from baseline/randomization.

**Methods.** A systematic search and curated screening process identified 12 candidate study reports, of which 9 provided extractable fatigue data at ≤12 weeks and contributed one effect size each (N = 918 participants). Fatigue scores were oriented so that higher values denote more fatigue (reverse-oriented instruments such as FACIT-F were rescored). Effect sizes were expressed as the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD, Hedges' g corrected), pooled under a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I², and τ². Publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression test and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** The pooled effect measure was a Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) of 0.005 (95% CI −0.246 to 0.257; p = 0.9670; k = 9; N = 918). Native-scale pooling on the requested SMD metric was used (native_pooling_used = true), so this is a confirmed SMD and not a standardized fallback. The direction field for this analysis records the point estimate as favouring the no-training control arm; however, the magnitude is essentially zero and the confidence interval spans clinically meaningful benefit and harm in both directions, so the data are most accurately described as showing no detectable difference. Individual effects ranged from −0.754 to 0.515, with five of nine point estimates in the direction of less fatigue with resistance training and four in the opposite direction; three individual estimates excluded the null. Heterogeneity was substantial (Q = 26.278, df = 8, p = 0.0009; I² = 70.81%; τ² = 0.099719, τ ≈ 0.316), and the approximate 95% prediction interval (≈ −0.80 to 0.81) indicates that a future trial could plausibly observe a moderate benefit or a moderate disbenefit. Publication-bias diagnostics were reassuring: Egger's intercept 0.4856 (p = 0.2933) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted SMD 0.0053), i.e. low assessed risk of small-study bias.

**Conclusions.** In this meta-analysis, resistance training during anticancer therapy was not associated with a reduction in cancer-related fatigue at ≤12 weeks; the pooled standardized mean difference was indistinguishable from zero. Substantial between-study heterogeneity that could not be resolved with the available data, a small number of pooled studies, and incomplete extraction (9 of 12 candidate reports) mean this null result should be read as "no reliable short-term effect demonstrated" rather than as evidence that resistance training is ineffective. Longer intervention durations, adequately dosed programmes, and consistent fatigue instrumentation are priorities for future trials.


## 1. Introduction

Cancer-related fatigue (CRF) is a distressing, persistent sense of physical, emotional, and cognitive exhaustion related to cancer or its treatment that is disproportionate to recent activity and not relieved by rest. It is the most frequently reported symptom during chemotherapy, radiotherapy, endocrine therapy, and targeted therapy, affecting the majority of patients at some point during active treatment, and it is a leading driver of treatment interruption, functional decline, and impaired health-related quality of life.

Exercise is among the best-supported non-pharmacological strategies for CRF, and international supportive-care guidance recommends that patients remain physically active during treatment. Within the exercise literature, however, resistance (strength) training occupies a distinctive position. Unlike aerobic training, which has been investigated extensively as a fatigue intervention, resistance training is often prescribed primarily to preserve lean mass, muscular strength, and physical function during cytotoxic therapy, with fatigue reduction treated as a secondary or exploratory endpoint. Its mechanistic rationale for fatigue — attenuation of treatment-induced sarcopenia, improved neuromuscular efficiency, reduced perceived exertion during activities of daily living, and possible anti-inflammatory effects — is plausible but not identical to that proposed for aerobic exercise.

A further and under-examined issue is timing. Most syntheses pool fatigue outcomes measured at heterogeneous endpoints, frequently at the conclusion of interventions lasting 12 weeks to 6 months or at post-treatment follow-up. Yet the clinically urgent question for many patients and clinicians is whether resistance training helps *early* — during the first weeks of therapy, when fatigue is accumulating and when adherence to a new exercise programme is most fragile. Short-horizon effects may differ systematically from longer-horizon effects: training adaptations take time, early sessions impose an additional physiological load on an already stressed system, and the fatigue signal measured at ≤12 weeks may capture acute training load as much as any therapeutic benefit.

This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore addresses a deliberately narrow and policy-relevant question:

> **Does resistance training delivered during anticancer therapy, compared with no training (usual care, waitlist, or non-exercise control), reduce cancer-related fatigue assessed at 12 weeks or less in people with cancer?**

The analysis is restricted to validated self-report fatigue instruments and to assessments occurring no more than 12 weeks from baseline/randomization, with all scores oriented so that higher values indicate more fatigue.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Reporting standard and protocol

This review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. The review was conducted in a curated ("manual") evidence-assembly mode, in which candidate records were supplied and verified by the review team and automated document-type gating was used as a secondary safeguard rather than as the primary inclusion filter. This operating mode is disclosed because it affects how automated validation warnings should be interpreted (see §9, Data Quality & Validation Notes).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria (PICOS)

**Population (P).** Adults with a diagnosis of cancer who were receiving anticancer therapy (e.g. chemotherapy, radiotherapy, chemoradiotherapy, endocrine or targeted therapy) at the time the intervention was delivered.

**Intervention (I).** Resistance (strength) training delivered during anticancer therapy — progressive loading of skeletal muscle using free weights, machines, elastic resistance, or bodyweight, delivered as the defining component of the intervention arm.

**Comparator (C).** No training: usual care, waitlist, attention-only, or any other non-exercise control condition.

**Outcome (O).** Cancer-related fatigue measured with any validated self-report instrument, including but not limited to the FACIT-F/FACT-F fatigue subscale, the EORTC QLQ-C30 fatigue scale, the Brief Fatigue Inventory (BFI), the Piper Fatigue Scale, the Multidimensional Fatigue Inventory (MFI), the Fatigue Symptom Inventory (FSI), and the Profile of Mood States (POMS) fatigue subscale. All scores were oriented prior to analysis so that **higher = more fatigue**; reverse-oriented instruments (notably FACIT-F/FACT-F, on which higher raw scores denote *less* fatigue) were rescored accordingly so that all contributing effect sizes shared a single directional convention.

**Timing.** The fatigue assessment contributing to the analysis had to occur at a post-intervention or follow-up assessment **12 weeks or less** from baseline/randomization. Where a study reported multiple assessments, the one closest to, but not exceeding, 12 weeks was used, and only one effect size per study was admitted to the pool to preserve independence.

**Study design (S).** Controlled trials with a concurrent non-exercise comparison group.

### 2.3 Effect measure and direction of effect

The pre-specified effect measure was the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, computed as Hedges' g (small-sample bias-corrected standardized mean difference). The effect-measure selection was recorded as an explicit user request (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`), with no conflict detected against study-level reporting, and **native pooling on the SMD scale was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`). The pooled value reported below is therefore a confirmed native-scale SMD, not a standardized fallback substituted for an unconfirmed measure.

Under the orientation applied here, a **positive SMD/Hedges' g indicates that the resistance training group had less cancer-related fatigue than the no-training control group** (i.e. resistance training reduced fatigue). Readers are nevertheless directed to the explicit `favors` designation accompanying the pooled analysis and to the deterministic Effect Measure Summary table appended to this report, which carry the authoritative direction label; raw signs retain the treatment-minus-control statistical convention and are not self-interpreting.

### 2.4 Synthesis methods

Effect sizes were pooled using a **random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**, which was selected a priori on the expectation that intervention dose, cancer type, treatment modality, and fatigue instrument would vary across trials and that a common true effect was implausible.

Heterogeneity was characterized with:
- **Cochran's Q** and its p-value (test of homogeneity);
- **I²**, the proportion of observed variance attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error;
- **τ²** and **τ**, the absolute between-study variance and standard deviation of true effects on the SMD scale;
- an approximate **95% prediction interval**, describing the range in which the true effect of a future comparable trial would be expected to fall.

Publication and small-study bias were evaluated with **Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry** and the **Duval & Tweedie trim-and-fill** procedure. A p-value threshold of 0.10 was applied to Egger's test, consistent with its low power in small meta-analyses.

## 3. Search and Screening

Record identification, screening, and inclusion followed the PRISMA flow. Candidate reports were assembled and curated by the review team; each record then passed through an automated document-type gate designed to exclude narrative reviews, prior meta-analyses, editorials, protocols without results, and duplicate reports of the same trial.

**Screening outcome.** Twelve (12) study reports were carried forward from screening as potentially eligible primary studies addressing resistance training versus no training during anticancer therapy with a fatigue outcome. Of these, **9 studies yielded a usable, independent effect size** for cancer-related fatigue at an assessment point of ≤12 weeks and were entered into the quantitative synthesis. The remaining **3 reports did not contribute a pooled effect size**, because they did not supply the data required to compute a standardized mean difference at an eligible ≤12-week timepoint (for example, fatigue reported only at an assessment beyond the 12-week window, or reported without the dispersion statistics needed for effect-size computation). These reports were retained in the qualitative review but could not be meta-analysed; this attrition from 12 identified studies to 9 pooled studies is reported transparently rather than being absorbed into the headline count.

One record triggered an automated classification warning during the document-type gate and was retained on the basis of manual curation; this is documented in §9.

## 4. Included Studies

Nine studies contributed one effect size each to the quantitative synthesis, representing a total of **N = 918 participants** across intervention and control arms. Study-level sample sizes ranged from 41 to 164 participants (median 95), indicating an evidence base composed predominantly of small to moderately sized trials. Four studies enrolled fewer than 100 participants (n = 41, 47, 48, 81) and five enrolled 95 or more (n = 95, 132, 155, 155, 164).

All included studies compared a resistance-training arm delivered concurrently with anticancer therapy against a no-training comparator (usual care, waitlist, or other non-exercise control), and all reported cancer-related fatigue using a validated self-report instrument at an assessment occurring 12 weeks or less from baseline/randomization. Fatigue scores were harmonized to the "higher = more fatigue" orientation before effect-size computation, so that reverse-scored instruments did not introduce directional artefacts into the pool.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was defined for this review — cancer-related fatigue, self-reported, at ≤12 weeks — so one pooled estimate is reported. No additional `meta_analysis_groups` entries requiring separate pooling were present, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data.

**Primary pooled result (random-effects, REML; k = 9; N = 918):**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** (`measure_label`) |
| Native pooling on this measure | **Yes** (`native_pooling_used = true`) |
| Pooled SMD | **0.005** (0.005306) |
| 95% confidence interval | **−0.246 to 0.257** |
| p-value | **0.9670** |
| Studies (k) | 9 |
| Participants (N) | 918 |
| Model / estimator | Random effects, REML |
| Direction (`favors`) | **No training (usual care, waitlist, or non-exercise control)** |

The pooled **Standardized Mean Difference was 0.005 (95% CI −0.246 to 0.257; p = 0.9670)**. Because native pooling was performed on the explicitly requested SMD scale, this is a confirmed native-scale standardized mean difference and not a fallback conversion.

**Direction.** As recorded in the analysis `favors` field, the direction of this result is designated as favouring the **no-training (usual care, waitlist, or non-exercise control)** arm. This designation should be read strictly alongside the magnitude: the point estimate is 0.005 standard-deviation units — approximately one two-hundredth of a standard deviation — which is indistinguishable from exactly zero on any clinical or statistical reading, and the confidence interval is almost perfectly symmetric about the null (−0.246 to 0.257). The substantive conclusion is therefore that **no difference in short-term cancer-related fatigue between resistance training and no training was detected**, with any nominal directional label carrying no interpretive weight at this magnitude. The p-value of 0.9670 is among the most emphatically null results obtainable from a meta-analysis of this size.

**Interpretation of the interval.** The 95% confidence interval excludes effects larger than a small-to-moderate benefit (g > 0.257) and larger than a small-to-moderate disbenefit (g < −0.246). In other words, the data are reasonably informative against a *large* short-term fatigue benefit from resistance training, while remaining compatible with a small benefit, no effect, or a small disbenefit. Given that supportive-care exercise interventions are generally expected to produce small-to-moderate fatigue effects (g ≈ 0.2–0.4), the confidence interval does **not** exclude the entire range of clinically plausible benefit — it excludes only the upper portion of it.

The forest plot, including individual study weights and the pooled diamond, is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. The cumulative meta-analysis at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png` shows how the running pooled estimate evolved as studies were accumulated; it should be inspected for evidence that the null summary reflects stable convergence rather than cancellation of a trend.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **substantial and statistically significant**:

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | **26.278** (df = 8) | Observed dispersion far exceeds sampling expectation (expected Q ≈ df = 8) |
| Q p-value | **0.0009** | Homogeneity decisively rejected |
| I² | **70.81%** | Substantial; ~71% of observed variance reflects real between-study differences |
| τ² | **0.099719** | Between-study variance of true effects (SMD² units) |
| τ | **≈ 0.316** | True effects vary by ~0.32 SD units about the mean |
| Approx. 95% prediction interval | **≈ −0.80 to 0.81** | A future trial could plausibly show moderate benefit *or* moderate harm |

The observed Q of 26.278 against 8 degrees of freedom is more than three times its expected value under homogeneity, and the associated p-value of 0.0009 rejects a common underlying effect with little ambiguity. An I² of 70.81% falls in the conventionally "substantial" band (50–90%), approaching the threshold at which pooling a single summary estimate becomes questionable as the primary analytic output.

The practical consequence is best appreciated through τ and the prediction interval. With τ ≈ 0.316, true effects are dispersed around the mean by roughly a third of a standard deviation. The approximate 95% prediction interval of about −0.80 to 0.81 means that the next comparable trial of resistance training during anticancer therapy could plausibly report a moderate-to-large fatigue *benefit* or a moderate-to-large fatigue *disadvantage*. **This dispersion, not the null point estimate, is the dominant finding of the analysis.** A pooled SMD of 0.005 with τ ≈ 0.32 does not describe a world in which resistance training reliably does nothing; it describes a world in which resistance training does different things in different trials, and those differences average out to approximately zero.

This reading is corroborated at the study level: study_5 (g = 0.515, CI 0.194 to 0.835) and study_8 (g = −0.754, CI −1.403 to −0.106) have non-overlapping confidence intervals, which cannot be reconciled by chance variation around a single true effect. A visual summary of the heterogeneity diagnostics is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`, and the shape and modality of the effect distribution — relevant to whether the included trials represent one population of effects or several — can be examined at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`.

## 8. Moderators

Given the degree of heterogeneity documented above, identification of effect modifiers would be the natural next analytic step. **No formal moderator analysis — meta-regression or subgroup analysis — was conducted in this synthesis, and no moderator coefficients, subgroup estimates, or R² values are available.** This absence should be stated plainly rather than substituted with speculation: with k = 9 studies, a meta-regression would support at most one covariate at acceptable power (the conventional guidance of ≥10 studies per covariate is not met), and subgroup analyses would rest on two to four studies per stratum, producing estimates too unstable to guide practice.

The only moderator-relevant information generated was **descriptive and precision-based**, visualized in the bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, which plots effect size against study size/precision. Reading Table 1 alongside that figure, the three smallest trials (n = 41, 47, 48) produced effects of −0.754, −0.244, and +0.465 — the widest internal spread in the dataset — whereas the four largest (n = 132, 155, 155, 164) produced +0.096, −0.236, +0.515, and +0.126. Small-study estimates are therefore both more variable and not systematically displaced in one direction, a pattern more consistent with imprecision than with a directional small-study effect (and concordant with the bias diagnostics in §10).

Several plausible sources of the observed heterogeneity could not be tested with the available data and are flagged as hypotheses for future, better-powered synthesis:

- **Fatigue instrument.** The eligibility criteria admitted FACIT-F/FACT-F, EORTC QLQ-C30 fatigue, BFI, Piper, MFI, FSI, and POMS fatigue. These instruments differ in dimensionality (unidimensional vs. multidimensional), recall window, and responsiveness, and standardization does not eliminate such differences.
- **Assessment timing within the ≤12-week window.** An outcome captured at 4 weeks and one captured at 12 weeks were pooled together, yet they may measure opposite phases of the adaptation curve — acute training load versus early training adaptation.
- **Exercise dose and supervision.** Frequency, intensity, progression, and supervised versus home-based delivery were not extractable as moderators but plausibly drive effect differences.
- **Cancer type and treatment modality.** Fatigue trajectories differ markedly between, for example, chemotherapy for breast cancer and radiotherapy for prostate cancer.
- **Comparator type.** Usual care, waitlist, and attention control arms impose different expectancy effects on a self-reported outcome.
- **Baseline fatigue severity.** Floor effects in cohorts with low baseline fatigue mechanically compress achievable benefit.

Until these are coded and tested, the 70.81% of variance attributable to heterogeneity remains **unexplained**.

## 9. Publication Bias

Small-study and publication-bias diagnostics were uniformly reassuring:

| Diagnostic | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | **0.4856** |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.2933** |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **0** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted SMD | **0.0053** |
| Overall assessed risk | **Low** |

Egger's regression test showed no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.2933, well above the conventional 0.10 threshold applied to this test), and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed **no** missing studies (k₀ = 0), indicating that the funnel plot is already symmetric and that the pooled estimate requires no adjustment — the adjusted value (0.0053) is identical to the unadjusted value. Together these diagnostics converge on a **low risk of publication/small-study bias**, so the meta-analytic effect is unlikely to be materially distorted by selective reporting of positive findings.

**Caveat.** These tests have limited statistical power when fewer than approximately 10 studies are pooled, and this analysis includes 9. A non-significant Egger's test is therefore weak evidence of the *absence* of bias rather than firm evidence that no bias exists. Furthermore, funnel asymmetry — and its absence — can reflect genuine heterogeneity or methodological differences rather than bias alone; in a dataset with I² = 70.81%, the funnel-based framework is operating under conditions it was not designed for. A final consideration specific to this result: publication bias classically inflates pooled estimates away from the null. Here the pooled estimate *is* the null, so bias diagnostics provide limited protection against the more relevant threats — unexplained heterogeneity and the exclusion of 3 of 12 identified studies from the quantitative pool.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 Principal findings

Across 9 controlled studies and 918 participants, resistance training delivered during anticancer therapy was **not associated with a reduction in cancer-related fatigue at ≤12 weeks**. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was 0.005 (95% CI −0.246 to 0.257; p = 0.9670) — a point estimate essentially identical to zero, with an interval centred almost exactly on the null. The recorded direction label designates the control arm, but at a magnitude of 0.005 SD units that designation is an artefact of the statistical sign convention rather than a substantive finding; the interpretable conclusion is the absence of a detectable difference.

Three features qualify this headline. First, **heterogeneity was substantial** (I² = 70.81%; τ² = 0.099719; Q = 26.278, p = 0.0009), with an approximate prediction interval of −0.80 to 0.81. The null summary is an average over genuinely divergent trial results, not a consistent demonstration of no effect. Second, **individual trials contradict each other at conventional significance levels**: study_5 showed a benefit (g = 0.515, CI 0.194 to 0.835) while study_4 and study_8 showed the reverse (g = −0.415 and −0.754, both intervals excluding zero). Third, **the confidence interval does not exclude small clinically relevant benefit** — effects up to g ≈ 0.26 remain compatible with the data, which encompasses the lower range of what supportive-care interventions typically achieve.

### 10.2 How this fits the wider literature

Meta-analyses of exercise and cancer-related fatigue generally report small-to-moderate pooled benefits, frequently in the g ≈ 0.2–0.4 range. The present null result is not necessarily inconsistent with that body of work, because this review deliberately isolates a narrower slice of it along three axes that plausibly matter:

- **Modality.** Only interventions in which resistance training was the defining component were eligible. Much of the broader favourable evidence derives from aerobic or combined aerobic-plus-resistance programmes, and effects attributed to "exercise" may not transfer to isolated strength training.
- **Timing of the fatigue assessment.** Only assessments at ≤12 weeks from baseline/randomization were eligible. If resistance-training benefits for fatigue emerge gradually — through accrued strength and functional gains that reduce perceived exertion in daily life — then a ≤12-week window may systematically under-detect them. Indeed, the early phase of a resistance programme imposes an additional recovery demand on patients concurrently receiving cytotoxic therapy, which could transiently offset any emerging benefit and may help explain the negative estimates in some trials.
- **Concurrency with active treatment.** All included interventions were delivered *during* anticancer therapy, a period when fatigue is dominated by treatment-driven mechanisms (anaemia, inflammatory cytokine activity, sleep disruption, treatment scheduling) that an exercise intervention may be poorly positioned to counteract. Post-treatment rehabilitation studies, in which fatigue has a larger deconditioning component, may yield more favourable results.

Read this way, the present analysis does not contradict the general recommendation that patients remain active during treatment. It indicates, more specifically, that **resistance training should not be prescribed during active therapy on the expectation of a reliable fatigue reduction within the first 12 weeks**, and that such an expectation, if communicated to patients, risks setting up disappointment and disengagement. Resistance training retains well-documented benefits for muscle mass, strength, and physical function during treatment; those — not short-term fatigue relief — are the defensible grounds for prescription on this evidence.

### 10.3 Clinical implications

- Clinicians may continue to recommend resistance training during anticancer therapy for its established effects on strength, lean mass, and function, but should **frame short-term fatigue benefit as unproven** rather than expected.
- The very wide prediction interval means individual patient responses may vary considerably; **monitoring individual fatigue response**, rather than assuming benefit, is the appropriate clinical posture.
- Patients reporting worsening fatigue after initiating a resistance programme during treatment should have their programme reviewed for excessive load or inadequate recovery, since several included trials reported fatigue outcomes favouring the non-exercising comparator.

### 10.4 Research implications

Priority needs are: (i) trials with **longer intervention and follow-up windows** capable of detecting delayed adaptation effects; (ii) **explicit reporting of resistance dose** (sets, repetitions, intensity as %1RM, progression rules, supervision, adherence) so that dose–response moderation becomes testable; (iii) **harmonized fatigue instrumentation** and pre-specified assessment timing; and (iv) **individual participant data synthesis**, which is the only realistic route to explaining 71% unexplained variance with an evidence base of this size.

## 11. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following validation issues arose during evidence assembly and analysis and are reported in full:

1. **Uncertain document-type classification (1 record).** The automated document-type gate **could not confidently classify the document type** for the record `dd7b532c_JCO.2006.08.2024` (internal subject identifier `manual:d6a11bee`). Because the review was run in curated/manual mode — which trusts user curation by default — the record was **kept as an input** and was not automatically excluded. This means at least one contributing record was admitted without automated confirmation that it is a primary trial report rather than a review, secondary analysis, or duplicate publication. Readers should treat this record's eligibility as resting on manual curation alone, and any residual risk that it duplicates another included trial or is not a primary report cannot be excluded from the automated pipeline evidence.

2. **Attrition from identified studies to pooled studies (12 → 9).** Twelve study reports entered the review, but only **9 contributed an effect size** to the meta-analysis (`n_studies = 12`, `n_effect_sizes = 9`). Three identified reports therefore supplied **no** quantitative data to the pooled estimate, because eligible ≤12-week fatigue data in extractable form were not available. The pooled result reflects 75% of the identified evidence base, and the direction and magnitude of the three non-contributing studies are unknown to the analysis.

3. **Direction-label versus sign convention.** The pooled estimate carries a nominally positive sign (+0.005306) under the convention in which positive favours resistance training, while the analysis `favors` field designates the **no-training control** arm. Both are reported here as recorded. This apparent tension is immaterial in substance because the estimate is within 0.006 SD units of zero and its confidence interval (−0.246 to 0.257) is essentially symmetric about the null; no directional claim of any kind is supportable from this result. Readers requiring an authoritative direction should refer to the `favors` field and the appended deterministic Effect Measure Summary table.

4. **Effect measure provenance.** The SMD was selected by explicit user specification (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`) with **no conflict** detected against the measures reported in the source studies (`effect_measure_conflict = null`), and **native pooling on the SMD scale was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`). ## 12. Limitations

1. **Small number of pooled studies (k = 9).** This limits the precision of both the pooled estimate and, more acutely, the τ² estimate on which the prediction interval depends; it also places the analysis below the ~10-study threshold at which funnel-based bias tests acquire meaningful power.

2. **Substantial unexplained heterogeneity.** With I² = 70.81% and τ ≈ 0.316, the single pooled value is a weak summary of a dispersed set of true effects, and no moderator analysis was available to decompose it.

3. **No moderator or subgroup analyses were performed.** Fatigue instrument, exercise dose, supervision, cancer type, treatment modality, comparator type, baseline fatigue, and exact assessment timing within the ≤12-week window were all left untested, despite being the most plausible explanations for the observed dispersion.

4. **Incomplete extraction.** Three of twelve identified studies contributed no data (see §11.2), raising the possibility of outcome-availability bias within an otherwise eligible evidence base.

5. **Pooling across heterogeneous instruments and timepoints.** Seven or more distinct fatigue instruments were eligible, and assessments anywhere from shortly after baseline up to 12 weeks were combined. Standardization harmonizes scale units but not construct coverage, recall period, or responsiveness.

6. **Self-reported outcome with unblindable intervention.** Participants in exercise trials cannot be blinded to allocation, and fatigue is measured by self-report. Both performance and expectancy effects (in either direction) are therefore uncontrolled, and the use of usual-care or waitlist rather than attention-matched comparators in at least some trials compounds this.

7. **Risk-of-bias and certainty assessment not reported.** No study-level risk-of-bias (e.g. RoB 2) ratings or GRADE certainty ratings accompanied this synthesis. Informally, the combination of imprecision (CI spanning small benefit to small harm), inconsistency (I² = 70.81%), and unblinded self-reported outcomes would be expected to yield **low certainty** for this body of evidence.

8. **Restricted analytic horizon by design.** Because eligibility capped assessment at 12 weeks, this review cannot speak to whether resistance training reduces cancer-related fatigue over longer intervention or follow-up periods. A null finding here is a null finding about the short term only.

9. **One record retained without confirmed document-type classification** (§11.1).

## 13. Conclusion

In this PRISMA-compliant random-effects meta-analysis of 9 controlled studies and 918 participants, **resistance training delivered during anticancer therapy was not associated with a reduction in cancer-related fatigue assessed at 12 weeks or less**. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was **0.005 (95% CI −0.246 to 0.257; p = 0.9670)**, computed natively on the requested SMD scale; the direction field records the point estimate as favouring the no-training control arm, but at this magnitude — five thousandths of a standard deviation, with a near-symmetric interval around zero — the finding is best characterized as **no detectable difference between arms**.

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant (**I² = 70.81%; τ² = 0.099719; Q = 26.278, df = 8, p = 0.0009**), with individual effects spanning −0.754 to +0.515 and an approximate prediction interval of −0.80 to 0.81 for a future trial. Publication-bias diagnostics were reassuring (Egger's p = 0.2933; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0; adjusted SMD 0.0053; low assessed risk), though underpowered at k = 9.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore **not** that resistance training is ineffective for cancer-related fatigue, but that **no reliable short-term fatigue benefit has been demonstrated** in this setting, that trial results are genuinely inconsistent for reasons the available data cannot explain, and that effects up to a small benefit (g ≈ 0.26) or a small disbenefit (g ≈ −0.25) remain compatible with the evidence. Resistance training during anticancer therapy should continue to be offered for its established strength, lean-mass, and functional benefits, with short-term fatigue relief presented to patients as unproven. Resolving the question will require longer assessment horizons, transparent dose reporting, harmonized fatigue instruments, and ideally individual participant data pooling.
