# Sustainable Longer-Term Effects of Exercise on Quality of Life in Adults With Knee Osteoarthritis: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Exercise is commonly recommended for knee osteoarthritis, but its sustained effect on quality of life after supervised or structured treatment has ended is uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate the longer-term effect of exercise alone, compared with no treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education, on quality of life measured after the end of the intervention and no later than 12 months post-intervention.

**Methods:** The supplied evidence set contained six studies, of which five contributed one independent effect size each to the quantitative synthesis. Where multiple eligible post-intervention assessments could have been available, the prespecified approach was to use the assessment closest to, but no later than, 12 months. Effects were expressed as standardized mean differences (SMDs), with positive values defined as favoring exercise. A random-effects meta-analysis using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was conducted. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were examined using Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Five studies comprising 370 participants were pooled. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 0.16532116249943815 (95% CI −0.042 to 0.373; p = 0.11889892282419934), favoring exercise, but the confidence interval included no effect. Heterogeneity was absent in the supplied analysis (Q = 3.246, p = 0.5176; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). Individual Hedges’ g estimates ranged from −0.577731 to 0.412004. Egger’s intercept was 0.412 (p = 0.427), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀ = 0), leaving the adjusted estimate at 0.1653. The reported risk of publication bias was low, although bias tests were underpowered with only five studies.

**Conclusions:** Exercise alone may provide a small sustained improvement in quality of life after treatment ends and within 12 months of follow-up, but the pooled result was not statistically significant and was compatible with no effect. The absence of detected heterogeneity supports consistency among the included estimates, but uncertainty remains because the evidence base was small and important study-level details—including instruments, exact follow-up times, risk-of-bias assessments, and full bibliographic information—were not included in the supplied results.

# Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis is a prevalent cause of pain, functional limitation, and impaired quality of life in adults. Exercise is a core non-pharmacological treatment, but benefits observed at the end of a structured program may diminish after formal treatment ceases. The clinically relevant question is therefore not only whether exercise works during treatment, but whether any improvement in quality of life persists afterward.

This review addressed the following question: among adults with knee osteoarthritis, what is the sustainable longer-term effect of an exercise-only intervention, compared with no treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education, on quality of life measured after the end of the intervention and up to and including 12 months post-intervention? Assessments taken immediately at the end of treatment were ineligible. The intended outcome-selection rule was to use the instrument explicitly identified by each paper as measuring quality of life and, when multiple eligible follow-ups were reported, to select the follow-up closest to 12 months without exceeding 12 months. Selecting one estimate per study avoids treating repeated measurements from the same participants as independent observations.

# Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Across five studies and 370 participants, the pooled **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** was **0.16532116249943815** (95% CI **−0.042 to 0.373**; p = **0.11889892282419934**). According to the prespecified direction and the supplied `favors` classification, the point estimate favored **exercise intervention alone for adults with knee osteoarthritis** over no treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education.

The estimate corresponds to a small standardized difference. However, the confidence interval included zero, and the null hypothesis of no average effect was not rejected at the conventional 0.05 threshold. The data are therefore compatible both with no sustainable effect and with a small beneficial effect of exercise. The lower confidence limit also permits a very small effect in the opposite direction.

The analysis used the Standardized Mean Difference as explicitly requested, and native pooling was recorded as used. The source data nevertheless noted a measure-selection conflict: the user-requested SMD was retained as authoritative even though the underlying papers reportedly expressed effects as mean differences. Standardization can be appropriate when studies use different quality-of-life instruments, but it yields an effect in standard-deviation units rather than in the directly interpretable units of any one instrument.

It should be interpreted as showing how the pooled estimate changes as studies are added in the ordering used by the analysis; because that ordering was not described in the supplied data, it should not automatically be interpreted as chronological accumulation.

# Heterogeneity

No statistical heterogeneity was detected:

- Cochran’s Q = **3.246** with 4 degrees of freedom
- Q-test p = **0.5176**
- I² = **0.0%**
- τ² = **0.0**

These statistics indicate that observed between-study dispersion did not exceed that expected from sampling error under the fitted model. The heterogeneity summary is available at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

An I² of 0.0% does not prove that the true effects are identical. With only five studies, heterogeneity estimators and the Q test have limited ability to detect real between-study variation. Moreover, potentially important clinical differences in exercise format, intervention dose, comparator content, quality-of-life instrument, and follow-up timing could not be evaluated because those characteristics were not supplied. The REML random-effects approach remains reasonable given the expected clinical and methodological diversity of exercise trials (Higgins et al., 2022).

# Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **0.412** with p = **0.427**. This was not statistically significant and provided no detected evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects (Egger et al., 1997). Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies, and the resulting estimate remained **0.1653**. The supplied analysis classified publication-bias risk as **low**.

These findings should be interpreted cautiously. With only five studies, Egger’s test has low power, funnel-plot patterns are difficult to distinguish from chance, and trim-and-fill can be unreliable. Accordingly, “low” here means that the supplied statistical diagnostics did not identify asymmetry; it does not establish the absence of publication bias, selective outcome reporting, or non-publication of unfavorable trials (Duval & Tweedie, 2000; Sterne et al., 2011).

# Discussion

## Principal findings

This synthesis found a small longer-term quality-of-life effect favoring exercise after the intervention had ended and within 12 months of follow-up. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was 0.16532116249943815, but its 95% confidence interval ranged from −0.042 to 0.373 and the p value was 0.11889892282419934. Thus, the available evidence did not demonstrate a statistically significant sustainable benefit.

The individual estimates were generally small and positive: four of five favored exercise numerically. The sole negative estimate came from the smallest study and was highly imprecise. Statistical heterogeneity was absent in the fitted analysis, and neither Egger’s regression nor trim-and-fill identified publication-bias signals. Collectively, these results are most consistent with a modest possible benefit rather than a large, reliably established improvement.

## Clinical interpretation

An SMD of approximately 0.17 is conventionally regarded as small. Such conventions are only rough guides, however, and do not establish clinical importance. Whether the estimated effect would be meaningful to patients depends on the quality-of-life instruments used, their reliability and responsiveness, baseline variability, and established minimal important differences. Those data were not available. The pooled SMD therefore supports comparison across studies but cannot be translated directly into a specific number of points on a familiar quality-of-life scale.

The timing criterion strengthens the review’s relevance to sustainability because immediate post-treatment outcomes were excluded. Nevertheless, “after treatment and within 12 months” may encompass materially different follow-up intervals. Benefits measured shortly after program completion may not represent the same degree of durability as benefits measured at 12 months. Exact study-level timing was unavailable, precluding examination of this issue.

The result applies specifically to exercise-only interventions compared with no treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education. It should not be generalized without qualification to multimodal programs combining exercise with intensive education, weight management, psychological treatment, medication changes, injections, or surgery.

## Statistical interpretation

The random-effects model yielded τ² = 0.0, making its pooled estimate numerically similar to a common-effect result. Even so, a random-effects framework was appropriate because genuine intervention and population differences would be plausible. The non-significant pooled result should not be described as evidence that exercise has no longer-term effect. Rather, the analysis was inconclusive: it failed to exclude no effect while also allowing a small favorable effect as large as SMD 0.373 at the upper confidence limit.

The analysis total of 370 participants is modest. Precision was particularly constrained by several small studies, including one with only 16 participants. Additional adequately powered trials with prespecified quality-of-life outcomes and complete longer-term reporting could materially change the estimate.

# Data Quality & Validation Notes

Although no separate `validation_flags` field was supplied, several data-quality issues were explicit in the results and materially affect interpretation:

1. Six studies were identified, but only five supplied effect sizes; the reason the sixth study was not pooled was not reported.
2. The requested SMD was retained even though the source papers reportedly presented mean differences. The pooled statistic should therefore be interpreted on the standardized scale selected for this review, not as a native-scale mean difference.
3. Study-level quality-of-life instrument names and the source descriptions identifying them as quality-of-life outcomes were unavailable, so outcome classification could not be independently checked.
4. Exact post-intervention follow-up times were unavailable, so application of the rule selecting the follow-up closest to 12 months could not be independently verified.
5. Bibliographic identifiers were not supplied, preventing linkage of internal labels to full primary-study citations.
6. No study-level risk-of-bias judgments or certainty-of-evidence assessment was provided.

# Limitations

This report has several important limitations.

First, the evidence base was small: only five studies and 370 participants contributed to the pooled analysis. Tests of heterogeneity and publication bias were consequently underpowered.

Second, the supplied data lacked the information required to reproduce the literature search and screening process. Search completeness, duplicate handling, full-text exclusions, and possible language or publication-status restrictions could not be assessed.

Third, study characteristics were unavailable. The analysis could not evaluate clinical diversity in participant populations, exercise prescriptions, adherence, comparator conditions, outcome instruments, or follow-up timing.

Fourth, no risk-of-bias assessment was supplied. Exercise trials may be particularly vulnerable to lack of participant blinding, attrition, deviations from assigned treatment, and selective outcome reporting. Without domain-level assessments, the direction and magnitude of any resulting bias are unknown.

Fifth, the pooled SMD was selected despite an explicit note that the papers reported mean differences. Although standardization facilitates pooling across different scales, it sacrifices native-scale interpretability and can be influenced by between-study differences in outcome variability.

Sixth, statistical non-significance does not establish equivalence or absence of benefit. The confidence interval remains compatible with a small favorable effect. Conversely, the point estimate alone does not establish clinical importance.

Finally, publication-bias diagnostics based on five studies cannot reliably rule out missing evidence. The reported low risk should therefore be considered provisional.

# Conclusion

Among adults with knee osteoarthritis, exercise alone produced a small pooled longer-term improvement in quality of life relative to no treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was **0.16532116249943815** (95% CI **−0.042 to 0.373**; p = **0.11889892282419934**), favoring exercise. Because the confidence interval included no effect, a sustainable benefit was not established statistically.

No heterogeneity was detected (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0; Q = 3.246, p = 0.5176), and the supplied publication-bias diagnostics did not indicate asymmetry. Nevertheless, the small number of studies, incomplete study-level information, lack of a supplied risk-of-bias assessment, and inability to verify outcome selection and follow-up timing limit confidence in the conclusion. Better-reported, adequately powered trials should measure explicitly designated quality-of-life outcomes at standardized post-intervention intervals and report sufficient data for both native-scale and standardized synthesis.
