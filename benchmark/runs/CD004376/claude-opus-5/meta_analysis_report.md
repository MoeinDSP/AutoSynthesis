# Exercise Therapy and Longer-Term Health-Related Quality of Life in Adults with Knee Osteoarthritis: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials with Follow-Up to 12 Months

## Abstract

**Background.** Therapeutic exercise is a core, first-line recommendation in every major clinical guideline for knee osteoarthritis (OA). Its short-term benefits on pain and physical function are well established, but it is far less clear whether exercise produces durable improvements in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) once the supervised programme has ended.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of land- or water-based exercise therapy, compared with no treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education, on HRQoL at post-intervention follow-up assessments up to and including 12 months in adults with knee OA.

**Methods.** Randomised controlled trials of adults with knee OA comparing an exercise therapy programme with no treatment, usual care, or a limited/minimal education control were eligible. The outcome was whichever instrument each trial designated as its quality-of-life measure (e.g. SF-36/SF-12 component summaries, EQ-5D index, AQoL, WOMAC/KOOS quality-of-life subscale, Nottingham Health Profile, or a disease-specific HRQoL questionnaire). Only assessments made after the end of the intervention period, and no later than 12 months, were eligible; end-of-intervention data were excluded by design. Six studies met the inclusion criteria and four contributed extractable data to the quantitative synthesis. Hedges' g was computed for each comparison and effects were pooled using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was characterised with Q, τ² and I²; small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Four effect sizes from 500 randomised participants were pooled. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 0.260 (95% CI 0.076 to 0.444; p = 0.0056), favouring exercise therapy (land- or water-based therapeutic exercise programme). Between-study heterogeneity was negligible (Q = 2.858, df = 3, p = 0.414; τ² = 0.000005; I² = 0.01%), indicating that the four trials were statistically consistent with a single common effect. Individual study estimates ranged from g = −0.578 (n = 16) to g = 0.412 (n = 56); three of four point estimates favoured exercise and only the largest trial (n = 342, g = 0.269, 95% CI 0.043 to 0.495) reached individual statistical significance. There was no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger's intercept = 0.4314, p = 0.3369) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), leaving the adjusted estimate unchanged at 0.2598; the risk of publication bias was graded as low. The number of studies was too small to support meaningful subgroup or meta-regression analyses.

**Conclusions.** Exercise therapy confers a small but statistically significant and remarkably consistent benefit on health-related quality of life that persists at follow-up up to 12 months after the intervention period in adults with knee osteoarthritis. The magnitude (SMD ≈ 0.26) is below conventional thresholds for a moderate effect and the lower confidence limit (0.076) approaches the null, so the clinical importance of the average benefit at the individual patient level should be interpreted conservatively. The evidence base is small (k = 4 pooled studies, N = 500) and dominated by a single large trial; adequately powered trials with pre-specified, instrument-consistent HRQoL endpoints and follow-up beyond the end of supervision remain a priority.


## 1. Introduction

Knee osteoarthritis (OA) is among the most prevalent and disabling musculoskeletal conditions worldwide. It is characterised not only by joint pain and loss of physical function but also by a pervasive reduction in health-related quality of life (HRQoL) — encompassing physical functioning, social participation, sleep, mood and perceived general health. Because OA is a chronic, non-curable condition managed over years rather than weeks, outcomes that capture the patient's global experience of living with the disease, and that are measured *after* an intervention has finished rather than only while it is being delivered, are arguably the outcomes of greatest relevance to patients and to health systems.

Therapeutic exercise — whether delivered on land (strengthening, neuromuscular, aerobic or mixed programmes) or in water (aquatic/hydrotherapy programmes) — is a universally endorsed, first-line, core treatment for knee OA. The short-term efficacy of exercise on pain and self-reported function is supported by a large and mature body of trial evidence. Two questions, however, remain considerably less well resolved:

1. **Does the benefit extend to quality of life**, a construct that is broader than pain or joint-specific function and that may be less responsive to a localised musculoskeletal intervention?
2. **Does any such benefit persist after the programme ends?** Supervised exercise has a well-documented tendency to produce effects that attenuate once supervision, contact and adherence support are withdrawn. Effects measured immediately at the end of the intervention therefore risk overstating what patients retain in the months that follow.

This review was designed specifically to isolate that second question. We restricted eligibility to HRQoL assessments collected at post-intervention follow-up, up to and including 12 months, and we explicitly excluded data captured immediately at the end of the intervention period. The comparator was restricted to inactive or minimally active controls — no treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education — so that the estimate reflects the effect of *providing* exercise therapy rather than the relative merit of one exercise prescription over another.

### 1.1 Objective

To estimate, by systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials, the effect of exercise therapy versus no treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education on health-related quality of life at longer-term follow-up (up to and including 12 months post-intervention) in adults with knee osteoarthritis.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

This report follows the PRISMA 2020 statement for the reporting of systematic reviews and meta-analyses. The review question was framed using the PICO structure set out in Table 1.

**Table 1. PICO eligibility framework**

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Adults with knee osteoarthritis (clinically and/or radiographically defined) |
| **Intervention** | Exercise therapy — land-based or water-based therapeutic exercise programme (strengthening, aerobic, neuromuscular, mixed, or aquatic) |
| **Comparator** | No treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education (i.e. inactive or minimally active control) |
| **Outcome** | Health-related quality of life, measured with whichever instrument the study itself designated as its QoL outcome (e.g. SF-36/SF-12 PCS or MCS, EQ-5D index, AQoL, WOMAC or KOOS quality-of-life subscale, Nottingham Health Profile, or a disease-specific HRQoL questionnaire) |
| **Timepoint** | Post-intervention follow-up assessments up to and including 12 months. **Data measured immediately at the end of the intervention period were not eligible for this timepoint.** |
| **Study design** | Randomised controlled trials |

### 2.2 Outcome definition and effect direction

Because trials in this field use heterogeneous HRQoL instruments, the review adopted an instrument-agnostic rule: the eligible outcome was whichever measure the trial authors themselves nominated as their quality-of-life outcome. Where a trial reported more than one candidate instrument or subscale, a single estimate per study was carried forward to avoid within-study dependency inflating the apparent precision of the pooled estimate.

All effect sizes were oriented so that **a positive Hedges' g / SMD indicates that the exercise group had better quality of life than the no-treatment, usual-care, or limited-education control group** at longer-term (up to 12-month) follow-up. Instruments scored in the reverse direction (where lower values denote better health) were sign-reversed before pooling.

### 2.3 Effect size computation and synthesis

For each eligible comparison, the standardized mean difference was computed from group means, standard deviations and sample sizes at the follow-up timepoint, and corrected for small-sample bias using Hedges' correction (Hedges' g). Variances were derived from the standard large-sample formula for the SMD.

Effects were pooled using a **random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of the between-study variance τ². A random-effects specification was chosen a priori on conceptual grounds: the included trials differ in exercise modality, dose, delivery setting, control condition and HRQoL instrument, so a common-effect assumption was not considered defensible in advance, irrespective of the observed heterogeneity statistics. The pooled statistic is reported as the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**.

### 2.4 Heterogeneity and small-study effects

Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q (with its degrees of freedom and p-value), the between-study variance τ², and I² (the proportion of total observed variance attributable to between-study rather than within-study variation). Small-study effects and potential publication bias were examined with Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and with Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure. Both were interpreted with explicit acknowledgement of their low power when the number of studies is small.

Subgroup analysis and meta-regression were planned for exercise modality (land vs water), control type, programme duration and follow-up length, but were pre-specified as contingent on a sufficient number of contributing studies.

## 3. Search and Screening

The screening and selection process is summarised in the PRISMA-style flow below, reflecting the records processed by the review pipeline.

**Table 2. Study flow**

| Stage | n |
|---|---|
| Studies identified as meeting the review's population / intervention / comparator / outcome / timepoint criteria | **6** |
| Studies contributing extractable quality-of-life data at the eligible longer-term timepoint | **4** |
| Studies included in the quantitative synthesis (meta-analysis) | **4** |
| Effect sizes pooled (one per study) | **4** |
| Participants contributing to the pooled estimate | **500** |

Six studies satisfied the eligibility criteria on the basis of design, population, intervention, comparator and the presence of a quality-of-life outcome at a post-intervention follow-up timepoint within 12 months. Of these, **four yielded data in a form permitting computation of a standardized effect size** (group means, dispersion and sample sizes, or statistics convertible to these) at the eligible timepoint, and these four constitute the quantitative synthesis. The remaining two eligible studies could not be pooled because usable numerical HRQoL data at the longer-term follow-up timepoint were not recoverable from the available reports; they are therefore carried as part of the qualitative evidence base but contribute no weight to the pooled estimate. This discrepancy between the number of eligible studies (6) and the number of pooled effect sizes (4) is addressed explicitly in the Data Quality & Validation Notes and the Limitations.

One effect size per study was carried into the analysis, so the four pooled estimates are statistically independent and no multiplicity or cluster-robust adjustment was required.

## 4. Included Studies

**Table 3. Studies contributing to the quantitative synthesis**

| Study (label) | n analysed | Hedges' g | 95% CI | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | 56 | **0.412** | −0.117 to 0.941 | Favours exercise (not significant) |
| study_2_R2 | 16 | **−0.578** | −1.609 to 0.454 | Favours control (not significant) |
| study_3_R3 | 86 | **0.272** | −0.153 to 0.697 | Favours exercise (not significant) |
| study_5_R5 | 342 | **0.269** | 0.043 to 0.495 | Favours exercise (significant) |
| **Pooled (random effects, REML)** | **500** | **0.260** | **0.076 to 0.444** | **Favours exercise therapy (p = 0.0056)** |

Three structural features of this evidence set are worth noting before the synthesis is interpreted.

**Marked asymmetry in study size.** Sample sizes span a more than twenty-fold range, from 16 to 342 participants. The single largest trial (study_5_R5) contributes 342 of the 500 participants — 68.4% of the total randomised sample — and, because inverse-variance weighting under a near-zero τ² approximates precision weighting, it necessarily dominates the pooled estimate. The pooled SMD (0.260) sits almost exactly on this trial's point estimate (0.269), which is a direct consequence of that weighting rather than an independent corroboration.

**Only one trial is individually conclusive.** Three of the four confidence intervals cross zero. The meta-analytic gain in precision therefore comes principally from the addition of smaller studies whose point estimates are concordant in direction with the large trial, rather than from an accumulation of independently positive trials.

**One small discordant estimate.** study_2_R2 (n = 16) is the only estimate favouring the control condition (g = −0.578). Its confidence interval is extremely wide (−1.609 to 0.454, a span of just over two standard deviation units), reflecting the very low precision of a 16-participant comparison. It receives correspondingly little weight and does not materially perturb the pooled result; its practical contribution is to the heterogeneity statistics rather than to the central estimate.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Effect sizes were computed as Hedges' g, the small-sample-corrected standardized mean difference, with positive values indicating better quality of life in the exercise group. The distribution of the four estimates is displayed in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`, and the study-level estimates with their confidence intervals and relative weights are shown in the forest plot at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`.

The observed estimates cluster tightly for the three larger trials (0.269, 0.272, 0.412 — a range of only 0.14 SD units across studies of n = 342, 86 and 56 respectively), with the single outlying negative value arising from the smallest trial. This pattern — close agreement among the better-powered studies with scatter confined to the least precise one — is the signature of a homogeneous underlying effect observed with heterogeneous precision, and it is the pattern that the heterogeneity statistics in Section 7 formally confirm.

### 5.1 Effect measure

The pooled statistic is reported as the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, and **native pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`): the analysis was conducted on the scale in which the effect measure is reported, so the value below is a confirmed result on the stated measure rather than an unconfirmed conversion.

One documented measure-selection issue should be stated transparently. Some contributing paper(s) explicitly report a *mean difference*, but the rows in this analysis group do not share a common `outcome_units` value. Because the mean difference is scale-dependent (unlike a risk ratio, odds ratio or hazard ratio, which are unit-free), a raw mean difference cannot be pooled coherently across trials that used different HRQoL instruments with different scoring ranges. The analysis therefore did not adopt the reported native mean-difference scale and instead proceeded to standardized-effect pooling on the SMD scale, in line with the review's explicit outcome specification that each trial's own designated instrument be used. Readers should bear in mind that an SMD expresses benefit in pooled-standard-deviation units and is not directly interpretable in the units of any single questionnaire.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was analysed: **health-related quality of life at longer-term follow-up (post-intervention, up to and including 12 months)**. No additional outcome/timepoint groups were defined, so no separate sub-syntheses are reported.

**Table 4. Random-effects meta-analysis (REML)**

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) |
| Native pooling used | Yes (confirmed on the stated measure) |
| Studies pooled (k) | 4 |
| Participants (N) | 500 |
| Pooled SMD | **0.260** |
| 95% confidence interval | **0.076 to 0.444** |
| p-value | **0.0056** |
| Favours | **Exercise therapy (land- or water-based therapeutic exercise programme)** |
| τ² (between-study variance) | 0.000005 |
| I² | 0.01% |
| Cochran's Q (df = 3) | 2.858 (p = 0.414) |
| Estimator | REML |

The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was **0.260 (95% CI 0.076 to 0.444; p = 0.0056)**, and this result **favours exercise therapy (land- or water-based therapeutic exercise programme)**. In plain terms, adults with knee OA who received a land- or water-based exercise programme reported, on average, quality of life approximately a quarter of a standard deviation better than adults allocated to no treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education, when assessed at a follow-up point after the programme had ended and within 12 months.

Interpretation of magnitude requires care. By conventional benchmarks an SMD of 0.26 is a **small** effect: it lies below the 0.4–0.5 range often invoked as a threshold for a clinically noticeable difference on patient-reported outcomes, and well below a moderate effect. The confidence interval is informative in both directions — it excludes zero (hence the significant p-value), but its lower bound of 0.076 is close to the null, so the data are compatible with a benefit that is genuinely trivial as well as with one approaching the small-to-moderate boundary (upper bound 0.444). The honest summary is that exercise therapy produces a *reliably positive but modest* average effect on longer-term quality of life.

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) illustrates the stabilisation of this estimate as studies accrue, and shows the characteristic narrowing of the interval upon entry of the large 342-participant trial.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **negligible**, by every available index:

- **Cochran's Q = 2.858 on 3 degrees of freedom, p = 0.414.** The observed dispersion among the four estimates is entirely compatible with sampling error alone; there is no statistical signal of genuine between-study variation.
- **τ² = 0.000005.** The estimated between-study variance is effectively zero, implying a between-study standard deviation of roughly 0.002 SMD units — indistinguishable from no variation at all.
- **I² = 0.01%.** Essentially none of the total observed variance is attributable to real differences between studies; virtually all of it is within-study imprecision.

Because τ² was estimated at essentially zero, the REML random-effects model converged on weights that are numerically almost identical to those of a common-effect model, which is why the pooled estimate tracks the largest trial so closely. The heterogeneity diagnostics are summarised graphically in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

Two interpretive cautions apply. First, **Q and I² have very low power with k = 4**; a non-significant Q with four studies does not establish homogeneity, it merely fails to detect heterogeneity. With so few studies, τ² is estimated with substantial uncertainty and a near-zero point estimate is entirely compatible with a modest amount of true between-study variation. Second, the near-zero τ² means that a formal prediction interval would be only slightly wider than the confidence interval at the level of the random-effects variance — but with only 3 degrees of freedom the t-multiplier is large (≈4.30), so an approximate 95% prediction interval spans roughly **−0.14 to 0.66**. That interval includes zero. In other words, while the *average* effect across this population of studies is positive, the data do not exclude the possibility that a new, exchangeable trial in a new setting would observe no benefit. This prediction interval should be regarded as indicative only, given how unstable such intervals are at k = 4.

Taken at face value, the homogeneity is nonetheless notable and substantively reassuring: four trials using different exercise prescriptions, different control conditions and different HRQoL instruments produced mutually consistent estimates, which argues that the small positive effect is not an artefact of one particular instrument or one particular delivery format.

---

## 8. Moderators

**No moderator analyses were conducted.** With k = 4 contributing studies, meta-regression and subgroup analysis are not statistically defensible: a single covariate in a meta-regression with four studies leaves two residual degrees of freedom, yielding estimates with essentially no power and a high probability of spurious findings. The pre-specified moderators of interest — exercise modality (land-based vs water-based), control condition type (no treatment vs usual care vs limited/minimal education), programme duration and intensity, length of post-intervention follow-up within the 0–12-month window, and choice of HRQoL instrument — could therefore not be examined. This is a genuine evidence gap rather than a null finding, and no inference should be drawn about the equivalence or non-equivalence of these variants.

The empirical justification for not pursuing moderators is also internal to the data: with τ² estimated at 0.000005 and I² at 0.01%, there is no residual between-study variance for moderators to explain. A bubble plot of effect size against study precision/sample size is provided for descriptive purposes at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`; it should be read as an exploratory visualisation of the size–effect relationship (and the influence of the one small discordant trial), not as a fitted meta-regression.

## 9. Publication Bias

Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed with two complementary methods.

**Table 5. Small-study effect diagnostics**

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.4314 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.3369 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate | 0.2598 |
| Overall assessed risk | **Low** |

Egger's regression test showed **no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept = 0.4314, p = 0.3369)**, and the **trim-and-fill procedure imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0)**, so the adjusted pooled estimate (0.2598) is identical to the unadjusted estimate (0.2598). Together these support a **low risk of publication (small-study) bias**, implying that the meta-analytic effect is unlikely to be materially inflated by the selective reporting of positive findings.

This conclusion must, however, be heavily qualified. Egger's test and trim-and-fill have **very limited statistical power when the number of studies is small**, and with k = 4 both are near the lower bound of applicability — guidance generally discourages formal asymmetry testing with fewer than ten studies. The absence of detected asymmetry is therefore *not* proof that no bias exists; it is a failure to detect bias in a dataset that could scarcely detect it. Two further considerations push in the same cautious direction: the positive Egger intercept (0.4314), although non-significant, is directionally consistent with the kind of asymmetry that small-study bias would produce; and the existence of **two eligible studies that could not be pooled for want of extractable longer-term HRQoL data** is itself a form of outcome-reporting attrition that funnel-based methods cannot detect.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis addressed a narrowly and deliberately specified question: does exercise therapy improve quality of life in knee osteoarthritis *after* the programme has finished? Four randomised comparisons involving 500 participants yielded a pooled Standardized Mean Difference of **0.260 (95% CI 0.076 to 0.444; p = 0.0056)** favouring **exercise therapy (land- or water-based therapeutic exercise programme)** over no treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education, at follow-up points up to and including 12 months post-intervention.

**What this adds.** Much of the exercise-for-knee-OA literature reports end-of-intervention outcomes, where effects are largest and where contact time, supervision and expectancy are maximal. By excluding end-of-intervention data by design, this synthesis estimates something closer to the *retained* benefit. The finding that a statistically significant positive effect on HRQoL survives this restriction is clinically meaningful in kind, if modest in degree: it suggests that the gains from a structured exercise programme are not wholly dissipated once supervision is withdrawn, at least within a 12-month horizon.

**Magnitude in context.** An SMD of 0.26 is small. Against the heterogeneous instruments involved — SF-36/SF-12 component summaries, EQ-5D, AQoL, WOMAC/KOOS QoL subscales and similar — a quarter-standard-deviation shift will often fall at or below the minimal important change that patients can reliably perceive on any single instrument. Two readings are legitimate and should be held together. On the one hand, the average individual patient may not experience a transformative improvement in quality of life. On the other, HRQoL is a deliberately broad construct influenced by comorbidity, mood, social circumstance and life events, much of which exercise cannot touch; recovering a consistent positive signal on such a diffuse outcome, months after the intervention ended, is a non-trivial result. At the population level, a small, low-cost, low-risk intervention that shifts a whole distribution by 0.26 SD can have substantial aggregate value.

**Consistency.** The most striking feature of the synthesis is its homogeneity (Q p = 0.414; τ² = 0.000005; I² = 0.01%). Across varying exercise prescriptions and varying QoL instruments, the three better-powered trials produced estimates within 0.14 SD units of one another. This coherence strengthens confidence that the effect is real rather than instrument- or protocol-specific — while recognising that homogeneity assessed on four studies is weakly determined.

**Dependence on one trial.** Counterbalancing this, 68.4% of participants came from a single trial, and the pooled estimate essentially reproduces that trial's result. The meta-analysis should therefore be understood as *one well-powered trial, corroborated in direction by two small trials and contradicted by one very small one*, rather than as an independent synthesis of four comparably informative studies. Any future large trial reporting a null or negative longer-term HRQoL result would have a substantial capacity to move this estimate.

**Comparison with the broader literature.** The result aligns with the general pattern in OA rehabilitation research: effects on pain and function are typically moderate at end of intervention and attenuate thereafter, while effects on generic HRQoL are consistently smaller than effects on joint-specific outcomes. A small, durable HRQoL benefit is precisely what would be predicted from that pattern, and the present estimate is consistent with it.

## 11. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-handling issues arose during the review and are reported plainly.

- **Two eligible studies could not be pooled.** Six studies met all eligibility criteria, but only four provided extractable quality-of-life data at the eligible post-intervention follow-up timepoint. The pooled estimate therefore rests on 4 of 6 eligible studies, and the two non-contributing studies exert no influence on the result. Their absence is a potential source of selective-availability bias that the funnel-based diagnostics cannot capture.
- **Effect measure conflict, resolved in favour of standardized pooling.** Contributing paper(s) explicitly report a *mean difference*, but the rows in this analysis group do not share a common `outcome_units` value. Because the mean difference is scale-dependent — unlike a risk ratio, odds ratio or hazard ratio — this signal was not trusted on its own, and the analysis fell through to standardized-effect pooling. The reported statistic is the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, with `native_pooling_used = true`, meaning the pooled value is a confirmed result on the SMD scale. It is *not* interpretable in the raw units of any individual questionnaire.
- **Instrument heterogeneity is inherent to the outcome definition.** By design, each trial's own nominated QoL instrument was used. This maximises fidelity to the research question but means the pooled SMD averages across conceptually related yet non-identical constructs (e.g. a generic physical component summary and a disease-specific QoL subscale are not the same thing).
- **One effect size per study was carried forward**, so no statistical dependency between estimates required correction and the precision of the pooled estimate is not artificially inflated by multiple correlated outcomes from the same trial.
- **A single outcome/timepoint group was analysed.** No separate sub-syntheses by outcome or timepoint were required or performed; all four effect sizes pertain to longer-term (post-intervention, ≤12-month) HRQoL.
- **Small-study diagnostics are underpowered.** Egger's test and trim-and-fill were run as specified but, at k = 4, both are below the number of studies at which such tests are conventionally considered informative. The "low risk" grading should be read as "no bias detected by underpowered tests", not as "bias excluded".

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small number of pooled studies (k = 4).** This constrains every downstream inference: τ² is poorly estimated, heterogeneity tests have little power, no moderator analysis is possible, publication-bias tests are unreliable, and the prediction interval (approximately −0.14 to 0.66) is wide and includes zero.
2. **Dominance of a single trial.** One study supplies 342 of 500 participants (68.4%) and effectively determines the pooled estimate. The synthesis is not robust to the removal or contradiction of that trial.
3. **One very small, discordant trial.** study_2_R2 contributed only 16 participants with a confidence interval spanning over two SD units; estimates from samples this small are highly unstable and contribute mainly noise.
4. **Instrument heterogeneity.** Pooling across SF-36/SF-12 summaries, EQ-5D, AQoL, WOMAC/KOOS QoL subscales and disease-specific questionnaires requires standardisation, which costs direct clinical interpretability and assumes that a standard-deviation unit means a comparable thing across instruments.
5. **Effect measure is standardized, not native-unit.** Because the contributing trials did not share common outcome units, a clinically interpretable mean difference could not be pooled; the result is expressed in pooled-SD units.
6. **Heterogeneous comparators.** "No treatment", "usual care" and "limited/minimal education" are not equivalent conditions and differ in attention, expectancy and co-intervention. With k = 4 this could not be examined as a moderator, yet it plausibly influences the magnitude of the estimated effect.
7. **Variable follow-up timing within the window.** Eligible assessments could fall anywhere from just after the intervention to 12 months later. Effects may well attenuate across that range, and the pooled estimate averages over an unexamined time gradient.
8. **Risk of bias in the primary trials was not formally incorporated.** Exercise trials cannot blind participants, and self-reported HRQoL is the outcome most vulnerable to unblinded expectancy effects — a systematic limitation of this entire literature that applies to the present estimate. No risk-of-bias-stratified or GRADE-based sensitivity analysis was performed; on these grounds the certainty of the evidence would most plausibly be rated **low to moderate**.
9. **Incomplete outcome availability.** Two eligible studies lacked extractable data, raising the possibility that longer-term HRQoL results were less completely reported where they were less favourable.
10. **No assessment of adherence or long-term exercise continuation**, which is likely the key mediator of whether benefit persists after supervision ends, and which the available data could not address.

---

## 13. Conclusion

Across four randomised comparisons including 500 adults with knee osteoarthritis, exercise therapy produced a **small but statistically significant improvement in health-related quality of life at post-intervention follow-up up to and including 12 months**, relative to no treatment, usual care, or limited/minimal education: pooled **Standardized Mean Difference 0.260 (95% CI 0.076 to 0.444; p = 0.0056)**, **favouring exercise therapy (land- or water-based therapeutic exercise programme)**. Between-study heterogeneity was negligible (Q = 2.858, df = 3, p = 0.414; τ² = 0.000005; I² = 0.01%), and no evidence of publication bias was detected (Egger's p = 0.3369; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted estimate unchanged at 0.2598), although both diagnostics are underpowered at k = 4.

The practical message is cautiously supportive. Exercise therapy — already recommended as core management for knee OA on the strength of its effects on pain and function — appears also to leave a modest, consistent and durable imprint on patients' broader quality of life that is still detectable months after the programme ends. The size of that benefit is small and its lower confidence bound is close to zero, so exercise should be presented to patients as a reliable, low-risk contributor to long-term wellbeing rather than as a transformative one.

The principal need is for more and better evidence at this timepoint: adequately powered trials that (i) pre-specify HRQoL as a primary or key secondary endpoint, (ii) use a consistent, clinically interpretable instrument reported in native units, (iii) follow participants well beyond the end of supervision with assessments at defined intervals across and beyond 12 months, and (iv) measure and report post-programme exercise adherence, so that the determinants of persistence — rather than merely its average magnitude — can be identified.
