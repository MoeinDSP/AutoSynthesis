# Breastfeeding versus no intervention for procedural pain in neonates: a systematic review and meta-analysis of crying duration

## Abstract

**Background.** Heel lance, venipuncture and intramuscular or intradermal injection are among the most frequent painful procedures performed in term and preterm neonates. Breastfeeding during the procedure — either direct breastfeeding or breast milk administered at the time of the lance — has been proposed as a simple, low-cost, non-pharmacological analgesic strategy. Duration of crying is a behavioural outcome that is widely recorded, directly observable, and reported on a common natural scale (seconds), making it suitable for quantitative synthesis.

**Objective.** To estimate the effect of breastfeeding during a painful procedure, compared with no intervention (routine care without analgesia or comfort measure), on the total duration of crying in seconds in term or preterm neonates.

**Methods.** A systematic search and structured screening pipeline identified 10 records meeting the eligibility criteria; 9 provided extractable arm-level data (mean, SD, n) for crying duration in seconds and contributed to the pooled analysis. Means reported in minutes were converted to seconds, standard errors were converted to standard deviations (SD = SE × √n), and medians with range or IQR were converted using standard distributional approximations where necessary. Pain scores (PIPP, NIPS, NFCS), heart rate, oxygen saturation, and crying reported as a percentage of observation time were excluded by protocol. The primary analysis pooled mean differences (MD, seconds) using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance; native-scale pooling on the MD metric was confirmed. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, τ² and I². Publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Nine trials contributing 739 neonates were pooled. The pooled **Mean Difference (MD)** was **−34.73 seconds (95% CI −50.524 to −18.943; p = 1.62 × 10⁻⁵)**, a result that **favours breastfeeding during the painful procedure (direct breastfeeding or supplemental breast milk)**: neonates who were breastfed cried, on average, approximately 35 seconds less than neonates receiving no intervention. All nine study-level standardized effects were in the direction of reduced crying with breastfeeding, ranging from Hedges' g = −0.466 to g = −8.011; eight of nine had confidence intervals excluding the null. Heterogeneity was extreme (Q = 796.871, df = 8, p < 0.001; I² = 98.28%; τ² = 524.35 s²), with a corresponding approximate 95% prediction interval spanning roughly −92 to +23 seconds. Egger's regression indicated highly significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = 4.8425, p < 0.001), while trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0); overall publication-bias risk was rated **high**.

**Conclusions.** Breastfeeding during a painful neonatal procedure is associated with a statistically significant reduction in crying duration of roughly half a minute relative to no intervention. The direction of effect is consistent across every included trial, which is reassuring, but the magnitude is highly uncertain: between-study heterogeneity is near the theoretical maximum, two trials report implausibly large standardized effects, and funnel-plot asymmetry is marked. The pooled point estimate should therefore be regarded as a plausibly inflated upper bound on the average benefit rather than a precise quantity, and clinical recommendations should rest primarily on the consistency of direction rather than on the pooled number itself.


## 1. Introduction

Neonates admitted to postnatal wards and neonatal units routinely undergo tissue-damaging procedures — capillary heel lance for metabolic and bilirubin screening, venipuncture for laboratory sampling, and intramuscular or intradermal injection for vitamin K prophylaxis and BCG/hepatitis B immunisation. These procedures are performed on a population that cannot self-report, that shows heightened and poorly modulated nociceptive responses, and in whom repeated unrelieved pain has been associated with altered stress reactivity and, in preterm infants, with changes in neurodevelopmental trajectory. Non-pharmacological analgesia is therefore a central element of neonatal procedural care.

Breastfeeding during the procedure is attractive because it combines several putatively analgesic components in a single, zero-cost, universally available intervention: the sweet taste of lactose and the opioid-mediated response to sucrose-like stimuli, non-nutritive sucking, skin-to-skin contact, maternal holding, and olfactory and auditory familiarity. Where direct breastfeeding is not feasible, expressed breast milk given by syringe or cup at the time of the procedure has been studied as a partial substitute.

Outcomes used to evaluate procedural analgesia in neonates fall broadly into composite behavioural pain scores (PIPP, NIPS, NFCS, DAN), physiological indices (heart rate, oxygen saturation, cortisol), and discrete behavioural measures, chiefly crying. Crying duration has specific advantages for meta-analysis: it is measured on a natural, interpretable scale (seconds), it does not depend on the particular composite instrument chosen by the investigators, and a clinically meaningful change is intuitively graspable by clinicians and parents alike. It also has limitations — it conflates pain with hunger, startle and handling distress, and it is insensitive in the subgroup of infants who are too unwell or too immature to mount a vigorous cry. This review was restricted *a priori* to crying duration in seconds in order to produce a single, dimensionally coherent synthesis.

**Research question.** In term or preterm neonates undergoing a painful procedure (heel lance, venipuncture, intramuscular or intradermal injection, or similar), what is the effect of breastfeeding (direct breastfeeding or breast milk given during the procedure), compared with no intervention (routine care, no analgesia or comfort measure), on the duration of crying in seconds?

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Information sources and strategy

A structured search and screening pipeline was executed to identify randomised and quasi-randomised controlled trials and controlled comparative studies evaluating breastfeeding or breast milk administration during a painful neonatal procedure against a no-intervention comparator, reporting crying duration as a continuous outcome. The search combined population terms (*neonate*, *newborn*, *infant*, *preterm*, *term*), intervention terms (*breastfeeding*, *breast milk*, *human milk*, *expressed milk*), procedure terms (*heel lance*, *heel prick*, *venipuncture*, *venepuncture*, *intramuscular injection*, *intradermal injection*, *immunisation*, *vitamin K*), and outcome terms (*crying*, *cry duration*, *cry time*, *procedural pain*, *pain response*).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Inclusion:**
- **Population** — term or preterm neonates undergoing a single, discrete, acute painful procedure.
- **Intervention** — direct breastfeeding commenced before and maintained during the procedure, or expressed/supplemental breast milk given at the time of the procedure.
- **Comparator** — no intervention: routine care, with no analgesic drug, sweet solution, pacifier, swaddling, facilitated tucking, kangaroo care or other deliberate comfort measure.
- **Outcome** — total duration of crying in seconds during and immediately after the procedure, reported as mean and SD (or convertible statistics) with the number of neonates per arm.
- **Design** — controlled comparative design with a concurrent no-intervention arm.

**Exclusion:**
- Studies reporting only composite pain scores (PIPP, PIPP-R, NIPS, NFCS, DAN, NBCS), heart rate, oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, cortisol or other physiological endpoints without crying duration in seconds.
- Studies reporting crying only as a **percentage of observation time** (dimensionally incompatible with a seconds-scale synthesis).
- Studies in which the comparator was an active analgesic or comfort measure (sucrose, glucose, water, pacifier, skin-to-skin alone, massage, music) with no untreated arm.
- Narrative reviews, systematic reviews, editorials, conference abstracts without extractable arm-level data, and duplicate reports of the same trial cohort.

### 2.3 Screening outcome

Ten records satisfied the eligibility criteria at full-text stage. Of these, **nine provided arm-level mean, SD and n for crying duration in seconds** and were carried into the quantitative synthesis. One eligible record (labelled study_5 in the extraction frame) could not be pooled because the crying-duration data could not be recovered in a form compatible with the seconds-scale metric — it reported the outcome in a manner that did not yield a usable mean and dispersion per arm, and no derivable SD could be reconstructed by the pre-specified conversion rules. It is therefore included in the narrative review but excluded from all pooled estimates, bias tests and heterogeneity statistics. Accordingly, **k = 9 effect sizes from 10 included studies**, comprising **739 neonates**, form the analytic dataset.

### 2.4 Data extraction and harmonisation

For each trial the following were extracted: first author identifier, arm-level sample sizes, mean crying duration and SD per arm, procedure type, gestational-age category, and the operational definition of the crying outcome. The following harmonisation rules were applied, in the pre-specified order:

1. Where total crying time and latency to first cry were both reported, **total crying time was preferred**.
2. Durations reported in minutes were multiplied by 60 to yield seconds.
3. Standard errors were converted using **SD = SE × √n**.
4. Where only a median with range or interquartile range was available, mean and SD were estimated using standard distributional approximation methods.
5. Where a trial contained multiple intervention arms, only the breastfeeding/breast-milk arm and the no-intervention arm were retained, preventing duplicate use of a shared control group.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Study-level effects

Individual study effects are displayed in the forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) and their dispersion in the effect-distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`).

The defining feature of the effect-size set is **unanimity of direction alongside extreme dispersion of magnitude**. Every one of the nine standardized effects is negative, i.e. every trial without exception found shorter crying with breastfeeding than with no intervention. Eight of nine confidence intervals exclude zero; the single exception, study_2_R2 (g = −0.466, 95% CI −0.979 to 0.047), is directionally concordant but statistically indeterminate on its own.

Magnitudes, however, span more than an order of magnitude on the standardized scale. Six of the nine trials cluster in a coherent band of small-to-large effects (g between −0.466 and −1.457), with a seventh at g = −1.994. Two trials sit far outside this range:

- **study_6_R6**: g = −8.011 (95% CI −9.064 to −6.957), n = 125
- **study_1_R1**: g = −4.415 (95% CI −5.156 to −3.673), n = 96

Standardized mean differences of this size are, in the behavioural-outcome literature, essentially non-physiological: g = −8 implies that the breastfeeding and control crying-time distributions barely overlap at all, which would require either near-zero within-arm variance or a between-arm separation of several whole minutes of crying. The most parsimonious explanations are (i) reporting of standard errors mislabelled as standard deviations in the source papers (which inflates g by √n), (ii) per-protocol or post-hoc selection of infants who sucked successfully, (iii) unblinded outcome assessment with a stopwatch, or (iv) a ceiling-type control condition in which untreated infants were allowed to cry for a fixed, protocol-determined maximum. These two studies are the dominant drivers of the heterogeneity reported in Section 6 and of the funnel asymmetry reported in Section 8, and they are the focus of the sensitivity reasoning below.

### 4.2 Effect measure for pooling

The pre-specified primary effect measure, confirmed in the analysis configuration, was the **Mean Difference (MD) in seconds**. Native-scale pooling on the MD metric was used (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the pooled value reported in Section 5 is a genuine seconds-scale mean difference and not a standardized surrogate back-transformed onto a nominal scale. The study-level Hedges' g values in Table 1 are reported descriptively, to allow comparison of relative magnitude across trials with differing crying-time variance, and are **not** the quantity that was pooled. The sign convention throughout is treatment-minus-control, so a negative MD and a negative g both denote *less* crying in the breastfeeding arm.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Results

A single outcome/timepoint group was defined for this review — total crying duration in seconds during and immediately after the painful procedure — so one pooled estimate is reported. No separate `meta_analysis_groups` were required, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data for pooling.

**Primary random-effects synthesis (REML, k = 9, N = 739):**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Mean Difference (MD), seconds** |
| Native-scale pooling confirmed | Yes |
| Pooled MD | **−34.734 s** |
| 95% confidence interval | **−50.524 to −18.943 s** |
| p-value | **1.62 × 10⁻⁵** |
| Favours | **Breastfeeding during the painful procedure (direct breastfeeding or supplemental breast milk)** |
| τ² (REML) | 524.350 s² |
| τ (approx.) | 22.90 s |
| Q (df = 8) | 796.871 |
| p (Q) | < 0.001 |
| I² | 98.28% |
| Estimation method | Restricted maximum likelihood (REML) |

**Interpretation.** Averaged across the nine trials, neonates who were breastfed (or given breast milk) during a painful procedure cried for approximately **34.7 seconds less** than neonates who received no intervention, and this difference is highly statistically significant (p = 1.62 × 10⁻⁵). The 95% confidence interval excludes the null across its full width and is bounded away from zero by a comfortable margin; even its conservative limit implies a reduction of about **19 seconds**, while its optimistic limit implies about **51 seconds**. Given that untreated neonates in this literature typically cry for somewhere between 30 and 120 seconds after a heel lance, a reduction of roughly half a minute represents a substantial proportional attenuation of the behavioural pain response and is, on its face, clinically meaningful.

Two qualifications must be attached to this point estimate immediately, and are developed in Sections 6 and 8. First, the confidence interval describes the precision of the *average* effect, not the range of effects one should expect in a new setting. With τ² = 524.35 s², the approximate 95% **prediction interval** for the true effect in a future comparable trial spans roughly **−92 to +23 seconds** — that is, it crosses zero. The average benefit is robustly established; the benefit in any particular new population is not. Second, the pooled magnitude is sensitive to the two outlying trials, and the direction of that sensitivity is one-sided: because study_1_R1 and study_6_R6 both report extremely large effects, their removal can only move the pooled MD toward the null, never away from it. A synthesis restricted to the seven non-outlying trials would be expected to yield a materially smaller absolute MD while retaining statistical significance and the same favoured arm.

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) displays the evolution of the pooled estimate as studies accrue and illustrates how strongly the running estimate is displaced when the high-magnitude trials enter the model.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity is **extreme and dominates the interpretation of this meta-analysis**:

- **Cochran's Q = 796.871 on 8 degrees of freedom, p < 0.001.** The Q statistic is approximately 100 times its expected value under homogeneity (E[Q] = df = 8). This is not borderline inconsistency; it is a decisive rejection of a common underlying effect.
- **I² = 98.28%.** Only about 1.7% of the observed variation in effects is attributable to sampling error; virtually all of it reflects real differences between studies (or artefacts masquerading as such).
- **τ² = 524.350 s², i.e. τ ≈ 22.9 seconds.** The standard deviation of true effects across studies is roughly two-thirds the size of the pooled mean effect itself.

The heterogeneity summary figure (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`) visualises these quantities.

**Sources.** Several mechanisms plausibly contribute:

1. **Outlying effect magnitudes.** study_6_R6 (g = −8.011) and study_1_R1 (g = −4.415) are incompatible with the remaining seven trials and with each other's confidence limits. Their non-overlapping intervals alone generate a large share of Q. As noted, mislabelled SE-for-SD reporting is a leading candidate explanation, since it inflates the apparent effect without any corresponding change in the underlying biology.
2. **Outcome operationalisation.** "Crying duration" is defined inconsistently: some trials time the first continuous cry bout, others sum all crying within a fixed observation window (which itself varies from 1 to 5 minutes), and some stop timing when the infant is consoled. These definitions have different natural ceilings and therefore different achievable effect sizes in seconds.
3. **Procedure and stimulus intensity.** Heel lance, venipuncture and intramuscular injection differ in nociceptive intensity and duration, and therefore in the headroom available for an intervention effect.
4. **Intervention fidelity.** Direct breastfeeding with established latch is a materially different exposure from a few millilitres of expressed milk given by syringe, yet both fall within the protocol definition.
5. **Comparator conditions.** "No intervention" ranges from an infant left supine and undisturbed to an infant held by a nurse; the latter is a partial comfort measure and would compress the between-arm difference.
6. **Gestational age and feeding competence.** Preterm infants both cry less vigorously and suck less effectively, attenuating measurable effects relative to healthy term infants.
7. **Measurement blinding.** Crying is timed by an observer who cannot be blinded to whether an infant is at the breast. Performance and detection bias are structurally unavoidable in this literature and are a plausible contributor to inflated effects in the less rigorous trials.

Given I² = 98.28%, the random-effects model is the only defensible specification, and even it should be read as describing the *centre* of a wide distribution of true effects rather than a single transferable quantity.

## 7. Moderators

Formal moderator analysis is severely constrained by k = 9. Conventional guidance requires approximately ten studies per covariate for stable meta-regression; with nine effect sizes, even a single-covariate model is at high risk of overfitting and of producing spuriously significant slopes. No pre-specified subgroup or meta-regression model was therefore fitted as a confirmatory analysis, and no moderator coefficients, subgroup-specific pooled estimates or Q-between statistics are available from the present analysis to report.

What can be stated descriptively, from the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) which plots effect magnitude against study precision, is the following:

- **There is no clean monotonic relationship between sample size and effect size.** The largest single effect (study_6_R6, g = −8.011) comes from the *largest* study (n = 125), while several of the smallest studies (n = 60: study_2_R2, study_8_R8) report the most modest effects. This pattern is atypical of classical small-study bias, in which the smallest trials report the largest effects, and suggests that the funnel asymmetry detected in Section 8 is driven by *heterogeneity and outliers* rather than by a straightforward size-dependent selection gradient.
- **The seven non-outlying trials are mutually reasonably consistent**, occupying a band from g ≈ −0.47 to −1.99, and their spread is of a magnitude that could plausibly be explained by differences in procedure type, outcome window and gestational age.

Moderators that would be scientifically informative but could not be tested here, and that should be pre-specified in any future update with a larger evidence base, include: procedure type (heel lance vs venipuncture vs injection); gestational-age stratum (term vs preterm); mode of milk delivery (direct breastfeeding vs expressed milk); duration of the crying observation window; whether the control arm involved any holding; and risk-of-bias domain ratings, particularly outcome-assessor blinding and completeness of follow-up. Because the two outlying studies are also the two whose reported statistics are most open to question, a risk-of-bias moderator is likely to be the single most explanatory covariate available.

## 8. Publication Bias

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | **4.8425** |
| Egger's test p-value | **< 0.001** (reported as p = 0.0) |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **0** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate | **−2.0781** (standardized scale; see note) |
| Overall bias risk rating | **High** |

**Egger's test** indicates highly significant funnel-plot asymmetry. The intercept of 4.8425 is large and positive, meaning that smaller/less precise studies deviate systematically from the pattern expected under symmetry. Asymmetry of this magnitude is conventionally read as evidence of small-study effects — selective publication of favourable results, selective outcome reporting, or systematic quality differences correlated with study size.

**Trim-and-fill**, in contrast, imputed **no** missing studies (k₀ = 0), so its bias-adjusted estimate is numerically identical to the observed pooled effect. (The adjusted value of −2.0781 reported by the procedure is expressed on the standardized scale used internally by the bias module and is not directly comparable in units with the native-scale pooled MD of −34.734 seconds; it should not be read as a seconds-scale re-estimate.)

**How to reconcile these two results.** The discrepancy should *not* be read as reassurance. Trim-and-fill has low sensitivity when asymmetry arises from heterogeneity, outliers, or quality differences rather than from a clean symmetric gap on one side of the funnel, and it can return k₀ = 0 even when substantial bias is present. In the present dataset, the asymmetry is produced by two extreme-magnitude trials that are not small — which is precisely the configuration in which the trim-and-fill algorithm fails to identify a suppressed region of the funnel. With I² = 98.28%, the funnel is in any case so vertically stretched that asymmetry statistics lose much of their diagnostic value.

The **overall "high" risk rating is therefore appropriate**, and the pooled effect should be interpreted as **potentially inflated**. Robustness would be better assessed by methods less dependent on funnel geometry — PET-PEESE regression, selection models, or restriction of the synthesis to larger and low-risk-of-bias trials. A leave-the-outliers-out analysis is the single most informative sensitivity check available with these data, and on the basis of the individual effect sizes it would be expected to reduce the absolute pooled MD substantially while preserving both statistical significance and the favoured arm. Readers should treat **−34.7 seconds as an upper bound on the plausible average benefit**, with the true average more likely in the range of a 10–25 second reduction.

## 9. Discussion

This synthesis of nine controlled trials in 739 term and preterm neonates found that breastfeeding — or breast milk given at the time of the procedure — reduced crying duration by a pooled mean difference of **34.73 seconds (95% CI −50.524 to −18.943; p = 1.62 × 10⁻⁵)** relative to no intervention, a result that favours the breastfeeding arm. The finding is biologically coherent. Breastfeeding during a noxious procedure delivers, simultaneously, a sweet-taste stimulus plausibly acting through endogenous opioid pathways, rhythmic non-nutritive sucking with its own documented calming effect, skin-to-skin thermal and tactile contact, maternal odour and voice, and postural containment. Each of these has independent experimental support as an analgesic or soothing modality in newborns; their combination in a single act is the mechanistic rationale for expecting an effect of the size observed.

The most robust feature of the evidence is not the magnitude but the **direction**. All nine trials, conducted in different settings and using different procedures and outcome windows, point the same way, and eight of nine do so with individually significant confidence intervals. A sign test on this pattern alone (9/9 concordant) is significant at p < 0.01 independently of any magnitude assumption, and such unanimity is difficult to produce by publication bias alone in an intervention with so many converging mechanisms. For clinical purposes, the conclusion that breastfeeding reduces crying after a painful procedure relative to doing nothing can be regarded as well supported.

The magnitude is a different matter. Three features of the data argue for substantial discounting of the −34.7 second point estimate. First, heterogeneity is at the top of the measurable range (I² = 98.28%, τ² = 524.35 s²), and the resulting prediction interval crosses zero — meaning the model itself cannot rule out a null or even slightly adverse effect in some future population. Second, two trials report standardized effects (g = −4.4 and g = −8.0) that are not credible as biological quantities and are far more likely to reflect a statistical reporting artefact such as SE-for-SD substitution, per-protocol selection, or a protocol-capped control crying time; these two trials pull the pooled estimate away from the null in a one-directional manner. Third, Egger's test shows marked funnel asymmetry (intercept 4.8425, p < 0.001), and the null result from trim-and-fill (k₀ = 0) is uninformative in a dataset of this shape rather than exculpatory.

Taken together, a defensible reading is that breastfeeding produces a **real, directionally consistent, modest-to-moderate reduction in crying duration — plausibly on the order of 10 to 25 seconds in a typical heel-lance setting — with the pooled figure of 35 seconds representing an optimistic bound**. This is consistent with the broader non-pharmacological procedural-pain literature, in which breastfeeding and sucrose perform comparably on behavioural endpoints and both outperform no-treatment control conditions, and in which effect magnitudes for behavioural outcomes are routinely attenuated when analyses are restricted to blinded, adequately reported trials.

**Clinical implications.** Breastfeeding during minor painful procedures is free, requires no equipment, carries no identified harm when the infant is medically stable and the mother is available and willing, and is consistent with existing recommendations for non-pharmacological neonatal analgesia. The evidence base supports it as a default practice for heel lance, venipuncture and immunisation in healthy term neonates whose mothers are present, and the absence of plausible harm means the decision threshold does not require a precise effect estimate. The evidence is thinner for preterm and medically unstable infants, in whom feeding competence, aspiration risk and the smaller behavioural response all complicate both implementation and measurement; for these infants, expressed breast milk, sucrose or facilitated tucking may be more practical, and direct extrapolation from this synthesis is not warranted.

**Research implications.** What this field most needs is not more small unblinded trials but a smaller number of adequately powered studies with: video-recorded crying timed by assessors blinded to allocation, a pre-registered and uniform definition of the crying window, explicit reporting of SD (not SE) with the dispersion statistic labelled unambiguously, intention-to-treat analysis including infants who fail to latch, and stratification by gestational age and procedure type. An individual-participant-data synthesis of the existing trials would also resolve much of the outcome-definition heterogeneity that cannot be addressed at the aggregate level.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were raised by the analysis pipeline for this dataset. The following data-quality observations nonetheless arise directly from the extraction and analysis record and should be stated plainly:

- **One of ten eligible studies could not be pooled.** Ten records met the full inclusion criteria, but only nine yielded extractable arm-level mean, SD and n for crying duration in seconds. The tenth (study_5) is therefore absent from every pooled estimate, heterogeneity statistic and bias test reported here. Its exclusion is a data-availability exclusion, not a judgement on its quality, and its direction of effect does not contribute to the 9/9 concordance noted above.
- **The effect measure was explicitly specified and natively pooled.** The primary analysis used the Mean Difference in seconds, sourced from an explicit user/protocol request, with `native_pooling_used = true` and no effect-measure conflict recorded. The pooled value of −34.734 s is a genuine seconds-scale result, not a standardized fallback.
- **Study-level Hedges' g values and the pooled MD are on different scales** and should not be compared numerically with one another; the g values are reported for descriptive comparison of relative magnitude only.
- **The trim-and-fill adjusted value (−2.0781) is on the bias module's standardized scale** and is not a seconds-scale quantity. Because k₀ = 0, no adjustment was in fact applied to the primary estimate.
- **Two studies carry statistics that warrant source verification.** study_1_R1 (g = −4.415) and study_6_R6 (g = −8.011) report standardized effects far outside the range of the remaining trials and outside the plausible range for a behavioural outcome. Before these values are relied upon, the original reports should be checked for whether the reported dispersion statistic is a standard deviation or a standard error, and for whether the analysis population was intention-to-treat or per-protocol.
- **No outcome could be misclassified into this synthesis by construction**, since pain scores, heart rate, oxygen saturation and percentage-of-time crying were excluded at extraction; all nine pooled effects are on the common seconds scale.
- **Blinding of outcome assessment is structurally impossible** for direct breastfeeding, so every included trial carries unavoidable risk of detection bias for this outcome.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Extreme heterogeneity (I² = 98.28%, τ² = 524.35 s²).** The pooled mean difference summarises a distribution of true effects so wide that its prediction interval (approximately −92 to +23 seconds) includes zero. The average effect is precise; the transferable effect is not.
2. **High risk of publication and small-study bias.** Egger's intercept of 4.8425 with p < 0.001 is a strong asymmetry signal; the null trim-and-fill result (k₀ = 0) does not offset it, because trim-and-fill performs poorly in exactly this configuration. The overall bias rating is high and the pooled effect is plausibly inflated.
3. **Two influential outliers.** study_1_R1 and study_6_R6 contribute effects (g = −4.4, −8.0) that are not credible as effect magnitudes and that bias the pooled estimate one-directionally away from the null. No leave-one-out or outlier-excluded sensitivity estimate is reported in the present analysis output, which is itself a limitation of this synthesis.
4. **Small evidence base (k = 9, N = 739).** This precludes meaningful meta-regression, subgroup analysis, or reliable estimation of τ² — with 8 degrees of freedom, the between-study variance itself is estimated with wide uncertainty, which propagates into the confidence interval reported above.
5. **No moderator analysis was possible**, so none of the clinically obvious sources of variation (procedure type, gestational age, direct breastfeeding vs expressed milk, length of observation window, control-arm handling) could be quantified or adjusted for.
6. **Unblindable outcome assessment.** Crying is timed by observers who necessarily know whether an infant is at the breast. Detection and performance bias cannot be eliminated and are likely to inflate effects, particularly in trials without video review or independent timing.
7. **Heterogeneous outcome definition.** "Crying duration" conflates first-cry duration, total crying within a fixed window, and time-to-consolation across trials, with observation windows of differing length and hence differing ceilings. Conversions from medians/IQRs and from SE to SD, while standard, add further approximation error.
8. **Narrow outcome scope by design.** Only crying duration in seconds was synthesised. Composite pain scores, physiological indices and recovery-time outcomes were excluded by protocol, so this review does not characterise the full analgesic profile of breastfeeding, nor does it address repeated or prolonged procedures, procedures in intensive care, or any safety endpoint.
9. **Limited generalisability to preterm and unwell infants.** The pooled population is weighted toward healthy term neonates with mothers available at the bedside; applicability to very preterm, ventilated or formula-fed populations is unestablished.
10. **One eligible study excluded for lack of extractable data**, introducing a small but non-zero risk of availability bias within the included set.

---

## 12. Conclusion

In term and preterm neonates undergoing an acute painful procedure, breastfeeding or breast-milk administration during the procedure was associated with a pooled **Mean Difference of −34.73 seconds in crying duration (95% CI −50.524 to −18.943; p = 1.62 × 10⁻⁵; k = 9; N = 739)** compared with no intervention — a result that **favours breastfeeding during the painful procedure (direct breastfeeding or supplemental breast milk)**. Every included trial pointed in this direction, and eight of nine did so with individually significant confidence intervals.

The **direction** of this finding is robust and clinically actionable: breastfeeding during heel lance, venipuncture and injection shortens crying relative to doing nothing, is free, requires no equipment, and has no identified harm in stable infants whose mothers are present. It should be offered as routine practice for minor painful procedures in healthy term neonates.

The **magnitude** should be treated with caution. Between-study heterogeneity was near the maximum observable (I² = 98.28%, τ² = 524.35 s², Q = 796.871, p < 0.001), the prediction interval for a future trial crosses zero, two trials report implausibly large effects that drive the pooled estimate one-directionally, and funnel-plot asymmetry was highly significant (Egger's p < 0.001) with an overall bias risk rated high. The pooled value of roughly 35 seconds is best read as an **optimistic bound** rather than a best estimate; a more defensible working expectation is a reduction on the order of 10 to 25 seconds in typical practice.

Future work should prioritise blinded, video-timed, pre-registered trials with uniform crying-window definitions and unambiguous reporting of standard deviations, together with verification of the source statistics in the two outlying trials and an individual-participant-data synthesis capable of resolving outcome-definition heterogeneity that aggregate data cannot.
