# Breastfeeding or Breast Milk for Procedural Crying in Neonates: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Breastfeeding and breast milk may reduce behavioral distress during painful neonatal procedures. This review evaluated their effect on total crying duration during and immediately after a procedure compared with routine care involving no analgesic or comfort measure.

**Methods:** Eligible participants were term or preterm neonates aged up to 28 days undergoing a painful procedure. The intervention was direct breastfeeding or breast milk administered during the procedure; the comparator was no intervention/routine care without an analgesic or comfort measure. The prespecified outcome was crying duration in seconds. Ten studies were represented in the review dataset, and six studies contributed to the quantitative synthesis (551 neonates). A random-effects model estimated the pooled mean difference (MD) using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Under the prespecified convention, an MD below zero favors breastfeeding or breast milk.

**Results:** The pooled MD was −29.746451070772686 seconds (95% CI −49.238 to −10.254; p = 0.002779963691385151; k = 6; N = 551), favoring direct breastfeeding or breast milk. Heterogeneity was extreme (Q = 786.682, df = 5, p reported computationally as 0.0; I² = 98.89%; τ² = 562.57224 seconds²). Egger’s intercept was 4.5863 (p reported as 0.0), indicating pronounced funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀ = 0), but this did not resolve the concern about publication bias. The supplied trim-and-fill effect (−2.4791, labeled “g” in the source output) was not on the confirmed pooled MD scale and therefore was not interpreted as an adjusted MD.

**Conclusions:** Direct breastfeeding or breast milk given during painful neonatal procedures was associated with approximately 30 seconds less crying than unassisted routine care. Confidence in the magnitude and generalizability of this estimate is substantially limited by extreme between-study heterogeneity, evidence of small-study effects, incomplete reporting of study-level clinical and extraction details, and an internal inconsistency between the supplied individual standardized effects and the native-scale pooled MD.

## Introduction

Painful procedures such as heel lance, venipuncture, and intramuscular or intradermal injection are common in neonatal care. Neonates display measurable behavioral responses to these procedures, including crying, and repeated untreated procedural pain is an important clinical concern. Direct breastfeeding may provide multisensory analgesia through sucking, skin contact, maternal odor, taste, and soothing; expressed breast milk may also provide gustatory and nutritional comfort.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among term or preterm neonates up to 28 days of age undergoing a painful procedure, what is the effect of direct breastfeeding or breast milk given during the procedure, compared with no intervention or routine care without an analgesic or comfort measure, on total crying duration during and immediately after the procedure?

The eligible outcome was total crying duration, crying time, or duration of first cry as defined by the original report. Total crying time was preferred when several crying measures were available. Pain scores, latency to first cry, and percentage of time crying were not the target outcome. The prespecified native effect was the untransformed mean difference (MD) in seconds, calculated as intervention minus control; consequently, an MD below zero favors breastfeeding or breast milk.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **4.5863** with a p value reported as **0.0** (interpreted as p < 0.0001 given the supplied narrative). This is strong evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Such asymmetry may arise from publication bias, selective outcome reporting, genuine heterogeneity related to study size, or methodological differences; the test does not identify the mechanism by itself.

Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0**, meaning that no missing studies were imputed by that algorithm. This does not negate the significant Egger result and does not rule out reporting bias, especially with only six studies and extreme heterogeneity. The output also supplied a trim-and-fill estimate of **−2.4791**, labeled `g`. Because this value is not on the confirmed MD-in-seconds scale, it was not interpreted as an adjusted mean difference or directly compared numerically with the pooled MD. Overall, the supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **high**.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across six studies involving 551 neonates, direct breastfeeding or breast milk administered during painful procedures was associated with a statistically significant reduction in crying duration compared with routine care without an analgesic or comfort measure. The random-effects pooled MD indicated 29.746451070772686 seconds less crying, with a 95% CI from 10.254 to 49.238 seconds less crying.

The direction of effect was consistent across all six supplied study-level standardized estimates. Nevertheless, the estimated magnitude varied dramatically, and heterogeneity was extreme. The average effect therefore summarizes a highly inconsistent evidence base rather than a common effect expected in every neonatal population or procedure.

### Clinical interpretation

A reduction of approximately 30 seconds in immediate procedural crying may be clinically meaningful for brief neonatal procedures, particularly because breastfeeding is generally accessible, familiar, and capable of combining nutritive, sensory, and relational soothing. However, clinical applicability depends on whether direct breastfeeding or breast milk can be delivered safely during the specific procedure and whether the enrolled populations and procedures resemble the target setting.

The result applies specifically to comparison with no analgesic or comfort measure. It does not establish superiority over other active strategies such as oral sucrose, non-nutritive sucking, facilitated tucking, skin-to-skin contact, topical anesthetics, or multimodal care.

### Certainty and robustness

Confidence in the existence of some benefit is supported by the pooled confidence interval and the direction of the six study-level estimates. Confidence in the exact magnitude is much lower. The principal concerns are extreme heterogeneity, statistically pronounced small-study effects, only six studies in the meta-analysis, absence of risk-of-bias assessments, and inability to reconcile the standardized study-level records with the pooled native-scale MD using arm-level data.

No sensitivity analyses, influence diagnostics, leave-one-out analyses, alternative estimators for τ², Hartung–Knapp adjustments, or prediction interval were provided. Robustness to model choice and individual influential studies therefore remains unknown.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

- The review dataset contained 10 studies, but only six supplied effect sizes for pooling; reasons for non-contribution by four studies were not provided.
- The individual records were labeled as Hedges’ g, while the pooled result was labeled as a native-scale Mean Difference in seconds. Arm-level data were unavailable to validate this transition.
- The metadata explicitly noted that study rows did not share a common `outcome_units` value, even though MD is scale-dependent. The pooled output nevertheless marked native pooling as used. This unresolved conflict materially limits reproducibility.
- Original outcome units and any minute-to-second conversions were not supplied.
- It was not possible to verify whether reported dispersion values were SDs or SEs, whether SE-to-SD conversions were required, or whether any means and SDs were estimated from medians and ranges/interquartile ranges.
- No information was available to confirm handling of multi-arm trials, multiple reports of the same trial, or separate procedure-specific findings.
- The publication-bias output mixes an MD primary analysis with a trim-and-fill value labeled `g`; these values should not be treated as estimates on the same scale.

## Limitations

This report is constrained by the aggregate context provided. First, bibliographic details and study characteristics were absent, preventing appraisal of clinical comparability and independent source verification. Second, no study-level risk-of-bias judgments were available. Third, the search and screening process could not be evaluated against all PRISMA requirements because databases, dates, strategies, duplicate removal, reviewer methods, and exclusion reasons were not reported.

Fourth, only six studies were pooled, limiting the reliability of funnel-asymmetry methods and moderator analyses. Fifth, heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 98.89%), so the average MD may obscure clinically important differences among procedures, populations, and intervention formats. Sixth, no prediction interval was supplied. Seventh, the effect-scale and outcome-unit inconsistency prevents independent verification that the pooled MD is a valid synthesis of crying duration measured uniformly in seconds. Finally, the comparator was an untreated routine-care condition; results should not be generalized to comparisons with active analgesic interventions.

## Conclusion

Direct breastfeeding or breast milk given during a painful neonatal procedure was associated with a pooled reduction in crying duration of **29.746451070772686 seconds** relative to no intervention/routine care (pooled MD **−29.746451070772686 seconds**, 95% CI **−49.238 to −10.254**; p = **0.002779963691385151**; six studies, 551 neonates). The supplied direction field favors breastfeeding or breast milk.

This finding should be interpreted cautiously. Between-study heterogeneity was extreme, Egger’s test indicated pronounced asymmetry or small-study effects, publication-bias risk was classified as high, and inconsistencies in the supplied effect scales and units could not be resolved. Better-reported trials and meta-analyses using verified arm-level means, SDs, sample sizes, and uniformly converted seconds are needed to establish the magnitude of benefit and identify the procedures and neonatal subgroups for which it is most applicable.
