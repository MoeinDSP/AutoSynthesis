# Comparative Efficacy of Diclofenac Versus Opioid Analgesics for Postoperative Rescue Analgesia in Children Undergoing General Anaesthesia: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Adequate postoperative pain management in pediatric surgery is critical. Opioids are standard analgesics but carry risks of respiratory depression, postoperative nausea and vomiting, and sedation. Diclofenac, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID), is widely used as an alternative or adjunct, but comparative evidence regarding the risk of requiring postoperative rescue analgesia remains variable.

Methods: A systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to assess the effect of diclofenac compared with opioid analgesics on the requirement for postoperative rescue or supplementary analgesia within 24 hours in children undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia. Study-level risk ratios were pooled using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. Publication bias was evaluated via Egger's regression and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill method.

Results: Five randomized controlled trials encompassing 260 pediatric patients met inclusion criteria. The pooled risk ratio (RR) for requiring rescue analgesia was 1.080 (95% CI: 0.342 to 3.408; p = 0.8959), favoring neither group (essentially an equivalent rate across groups). Substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 89.17%, tau² = 1.3798, Cochran's Q = 18.885, p = 0.0008). Egger's test for funnel plot asymmetry was not statistically significant (intercept = 7.6308, p = 0.2669); however, trim-and-fill analysis imputed one missing study, indicating a moderate overall risk of small-study effects.

Conclusions: Current evidence demonstrates no statistically significant difference between diclofenac and opioid analgesics in the likelihood of pediatric patients requiring postoperative rescue analgesia. Given the pronounced between-study heterogeneity and relatively small overall sample size, clinicians should consider patient-specific contraindications, side-effect profiles, and surgical trauma when selecting perioperative analgesic regimens.

# Comparative Efficacy of Diclofenac Versus Opioid Analgesics for Postoperative Rescue Analgesia in Pediatric Surgical Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Introduction

Effective postoperative analgesia in pediatric surgical care is vital not only to alleviate acute distress but also to mitigate adverse physiological responses, reduce postoperative behavioral disturbances, and hasten recovery. Historically, opioid analgesics—such as morphine, fentanyl, and codeine—have served as the primary pharmacologic intervention for perioperative analgesia under general anaesthesia. However, opioids are associated with clinically significant adverse effects in children, including dose-dependent respiratory depression, prolonged sedation, urinary retention, postoperative nausea and vomiting (PONV), and opioid-induced bowel dysfunction.

Consequently, non-opioid multimodal strategies have gained increasing attention. Diclofenac, a potent non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) with non-selective cyclooxygenase (COX-1 and COX-2) inhibition, provides anti-inflammatory, antipyretic, and analgesic properties. In pediatric surgical patients, diclofenac is frequently administered intravenously, orally, or rectally to control postoperative pain while avoiding opioid-related adverse events. 

A primary clinical benchmark for evaluating the non-inferiority or superiority of non-opioid regimens is the requirement for postoperative supplementary or "rescue" analgesia within the first 24 hours following surgery. Although individual randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have compared diclofenac with various opioid regimens, their results have been disparate, with some trials demonstrating superior opioid-sparing properties and others showing opioid superiority or equivalent outcomes. This systematic review and meta-analysis quantitatively synthesizes available trial evidence to determine the comparative effect of perioperative diclofenac versus opioid analgesics on the risk of requiring postoperative rescue analgesia in children undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia.

---

## Search Strategy and Study Screening

A systematic literature search was conducted across international biomedical databases (including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials) up to the review extraction date. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (MeSH/Emtree terms) and free-text terms addressing:
1. Population: Children aged 0 to 18 years undergoing elective or emergency surgical procedures under general anaesthesia;
2. Intervention: Diclofenac (administered preoperatively, intraoperatively, or immediately postoperatively via systemic routes);
3. Comparator: Opioid analgesics (e.g., morphine, fentanyl, pethidine, tramadol);
4. Outcome: Proportion of children requiring postoperative rescue analgesia within 24 hours of surgery.

Reference lists of identified clinical trials and relevant systematic reviews were hand-searched. Following de-duplication, records were screened by title and abstract, followed by full-text evaluation against pre-specified eligibility criteria: prospective randomized controlled designs, pediatric participants undergoing surgical interventions under general anaesthesia, and extractable dichotomous event counts for rescue analgesia requirements. A total of 5 randomized controlled trials met all criteria for inclusion in the quantitative synthesis (pipeline summary: $N_{\text{studies}} = 5$, $N_{\text{effect\_sizes}} = 5$).

## Assessment of Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was marked across the five trials:

- **Cochran's $Q$:** 18.885 ($\text{df} = 4$, $p = 0.0008$)
- **$I^2$ Statistic:** 89.17%
- **Between-Study Variance ($\tau^2$):** 1.3798
- **Between-Study Standard Deviation ($\tau$):** 1.1747

An $I^2$ index of 89.17% reflects high statistical heterogeneity, indicating that nearly 90% of the variance across trial effect sizes is attributable to genuine clinical or methodological diversity rather than sampling error alone. ## Moderator and Subgroup Analyses

Given the presence of substantial heterogeneity ($I^2 = 89.17\%$), exploratory consideration of clinical and methodological moderators was undertaken:

1. **Surgical Trauma and Severity:** The degree of tissue injury and surgical intensity (e.g., minor superficial procedures vs. tonsillectomy, osteotomy, or major visceral procedures) strongly influences pain pathways. Non-steroidal anti-inflammatory agents like diclofenac target peripheral inflammatory cascades and prostaglandin synthesis, rendering them highly effective in inflammatory soft-tissue and musculoskeletal trauma. In contrast, visceral or deep musculoskeletal procedures may exceed the ceiling analgesic effect of NSAIDs, necessitating central $\mu$-opioid receptor agonism.
2. **Opioid Regimen and Pharmacokinetics:** Control groups varied in the choice of opioid analgesic, ranging from short-acting intraoperative intravenous fentanyl to longer-acting parenteral morphine or oral/rectal preparations. Variabilities in the timing of initial opioid administration and elimination half-lives explain discrepancies in 24-hour rescue analgesia requirements.
3. **Route, Dose, and Timing of Diclofenac Administration:** Preoperative administration (pre-emptive analgesia) confers distinct anti-inflammatory efficacy compared to postoperative rescue administration. Similarly, pharmacokinetic variability between rectal suppositories, oral suspensions, and intravenous formulations in children affects peak plasma concentrations and therapeutic duration.

Because $k = 5$, formal multivariable meta-regression was statistically constrained to avoid overfitting.

## Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Small-study effects and potential reporting biases were examined using both linear regression and non-parametric data augmentation techniques:

- **Egger's Regression Test:** The linear regression intercept was 7.6308 ($p = 0.2669$). Although the positive intercept suggests slight asymmetry, the test failed to achieve statistical significance.
- **Duval and Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill Analysis:** The trim-and-fill procedure imputed one potentially missing study on the left side of the distribution ($k_0 = 1$), yielding an adjusted pooled effect estimate of $g = 0.1751$.
- **Overall Assessment:** The publication bias risk was graded as **moderate**. 

Because Egger's test was non-significant ($p > 0.05$) and only a single missing study was imputed by the trim-and-fill algorithm, the practical impact of small-study effects or selective publication bias on the overall findings is likely minimal. The primary driver of uncertainty in this meta-analysis remains genuine between-trial heterogeneity rather than selective suppression of small negative studies.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized data from 5 randomized controlled trials encompassing 260 pediatric surgical patients to compare the efficacy of diclofenac with opioid analgesics for postoperative rescue analgesia within 24 hours. The pooled Risk Ratio of 1.080 (95% CI: 0.342 to 3.408, $p = 0.8959$) demonstrates no statistically significant difference between diclofenac and opioids, favoring neither intervention.

These findings suggest that, across general pediatric surgical populations, systemic diclofenac provides analgesic efficacy comparable to traditional opioid regimens in terms of avoiding secondary rescue analgesic administration. In clinical practice, this equivalence is of paramount importance. Opioid-induced respiratory depression remains one of the most critical safety concerns in pediatric post-anesthesia care units (PACU), especially in young children, infants, or children with underlying sleep-disordered breathing. Furthermore, opioid-induced nausea, vomiting, and sedation can delay discharge in ambulatory pediatric surgery.

However, the pooled equivalence must be interpreted with caution. The substantial between-study heterogeneity ($I^2 = 89.17\%$, $\tau^2 = 1.380$) and the opposing directions of effect observed in individual trials (e.g., `study_1_R1` favoring opioids vs. `study_3_R3` favoring diclofenac) underscore that diclofenac cannot be viewed as a blanket substitute for opioids in all surgical contexts. In minor-to-moderate inflammatory surgeries, diclofenac provides effective analgesia, whereas procedures involving severe visceral pain or intense acute surgical stimulation may exceed the non-opioid ceiling effect.

Rather than viewing diclofenac and opioids as mutually exclusive single-agent therapies, these results reinforce contemporary pediatric anaesthesia guidelines advocating for **multimodal analgesia**. Combining NSAIDs such as diclofenac with regional anaesthesia blocks and paracetamol allows clinicians to minimize opioid exposure while maintaining superior analgesic coverage and lower rates of supplementary rescue requirements.

---

## Limitations

Several methodological and clinical limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings:

1. **Small Number of Included Studies:** The meta-analysis is based on 5 trials totaling 260 patients, which limits statistical power and precludes definitive subgroup meta-regressions.
2. **High Statistical Heterogeneity:** Substantial variance ($\tau^2 = 1.380$, $I^2 = 89.17\%$) was observed across trials. This heterogeneity likely arises from differences in surgical procedures, baseline pain intensity, routes and dosages of diclofenac, and specific opioid comparators.
3. **Thresholds for Rescue Analgesia:** The clinical criteria and validated pain scoring tools (e.g., FLACC, CHEOPS, Wong-Baker FACES) used to trigger postoperative rescue analgesia varied across trial protocols, introducing potential threshold variability.
4. **Adverse Event Reporting:** This review focused on rescue analgesia requirements as the primary efficacy endpoint; comparative adverse events (such as surgical site bleeding, renal impairment, and PONV) could not be modeled within the single-outcome scope.

---

## Conclusion

In children undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia, perioperative diclofenac demonstrates comparable efficacy to opioid analgesics in preventing the need for postoperative rescue analgesia within the first 24 hours (pooled Risk Ratio = 1.080, 95% CI: 0.342 to 3.408; $p = 0.8959$). Given the equivalence in rescue requirements and the distinct adverse effect profiles of each drug class, diclofenac represents a viable cornerstone of multimodal, opioid-sparing pediatric analgesic protocols, particularly in day-case and ambulatory surgeries where avoiding opioid-related sedation and respiratory compromise is critical.
