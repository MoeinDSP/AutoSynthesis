# Perioperative Diclofenac Versus Opioid Analgesia for Preventing Postoperative Rescue Analgesic Use in Children: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Diclofenac may reduce the need for supplementary analgesia after paediatric surgery while avoiding some opioid-related adverse effects. This review evaluated perioperative diclofenac against opioid analgesia in children undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia.

**Objective:** To determine whether perioperative diclofenac, administered by any route before, during, or after surgery, changes the risk that a child will require any rescue or supplementary analgesic medication within the first 24 postoperative hours compared with an opioid analgesic.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons included children younger than 18 years undergoing any surgical procedure under general anaesthesia, with diclofenac as the intervention and an opioid as the comparator. The outcome was the number of children receiving any postoperative rescue or supplementary analgesic within the longest reported cumulative window up to 24 hours. Five studies, providing five effect sizes and 260 participants, were included. Risk ratios (RRs) were pooled using a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model.

**Results:** The pooled risk ratio was **1.0412006887031535** (95% CI **0.365 to 2.973**; p = **0.9398871096560963**; k = **5**; N = **260**). The supplied favourability classification was **neither intervention**, consistent with an essentially null pooled estimate and a confidence interval spanning substantial benefit and harm. Heterogeneity was considerable (I² = **87.55%**; τ² = **1.132307**; Q = **19.623**, p = **0.0006**). Egger’s intercept was **8.1065** (p = **0.1179**), while trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study and produced an adjusted value of **0.1194** in the supplied output. Publication-bias risk was classified as moderate.

**Conclusions:** The available evidence does not establish that perioperative diclofenac differs from opioid analgesia in the risk of postoperative rescue analgesic use among children. The estimate is highly imprecise and accompanied by substantial between-study heterogeneity. Consequently, clinically important benefit or harm cannot be excluded.

## Introduction

Effective perioperative analgesia is central to paediatric surgical care. Opioids are commonly used because of their potent analgesic effects, but their use may be accompanied by nausea, vomiting, sedation, respiratory depression, pruritus, and delayed recovery. Diclofenac, a non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drug, offers an opioid-sparing or opioid-alternative strategy and can be administered through several perioperative routes. Whether diclofenac provides postoperative analgesia comparable or superior to an opioid can be assessed pragmatically by determining how often children subsequently require rescue or supplementary medication.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among children younger than 18 years undergoing any surgical procedure under general anaesthesia, what is the effect of perioperative diclofenac, compared with an opioid analgesic, on the risk of requiring any rescue or supplementary analgesic medication within the first 24 postoperative hours?

## Search/Screening

### Eligibility criteria

The prespecified population comprised children younger than 18 years undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia. The intervention was perioperative diclofenac at any dose, timing, or route, including rectal, intravenous, intramuscular, or oral administration. Eligible controls received an opioid analgesic by any route, including systemic, caudal, or epidural administration.

The outcome was a single dichotomous study-level count of children who received any rescue or supplementary analgesic after surgery within the first 24 hours. Equivalent descriptions included analgesic complementation, supplementary analgesia, postoperative analgesia given, or the number requiring an opioid, paracetamol, or another rescue medicine. The intended extraction rule was to use the longest cumulative postoperative observation window reported up to 24 hours rather than an earlier count when a longer eligible window was available. Pain scores, time to first rescue medication, rescue dose, and total analgesic consumption were not eligible substitutes.

For multi-arm trials, the planned approach was to retain the diclofenac arm and combine all eligible opioid arms by summing event counts and denominators. Placebo or no-analgesia arms, local-anaesthetic-only arms, paracetamol arms, and other-NSAID arms were outside the comparison of interest.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood produced a **pooled risk ratio of 1.0412006887031535** (95% CI **0.365 to 2.973**; p = **0.9398871096560963**; k = **5**; N = **260**).

The supplied result classified the pooled estimate as favouring **neither intervention**, reflecting an essentially null point estimate. The confidence interval crosses the null value of 1 and is very wide: it remains compatible both with a substantial reduction and with a substantial increase in rescue-analgesic use under diclofenac relative to opioid analgesia. Thus, the analysis does not demonstrate superiority of either diclofenac or opioids for this outcome.

The cumulative-analysis figure is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`. No numerical cumulative estimates were supplied, so the sequence-specific results are not restated here.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- **I² = 87.55%**
- **τ² = 1.132307**
- **Cochran’s Q = 19.623**
- **Q-test p = 0.0006**

The statistically significant Q test and high I² indicate that most observed variation was not readily attributable to within-study sampling error alone. The random-effects model was therefore appropriate, but it does not explain the heterogeneity. Plausible clinical sources could include surgical procedure, patient age, diclofenac dose or route, opioid type and route, timing of administration, rescue threshold, and duration of outcome ascertainment. These explanations remain hypotheses because the necessary study-level characteristics were not provided.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression yielded an intercept of **8.1065** with p = **0.1179**. This was not statistically significant and therefore did not provide strong evidence of small-study-effect or funnel-plot asymmetry. Nevertheless, only five studies were available, making Egger’s test underpowered and potentially unstable.

Trim-and-fill estimated **1** potentially missing study and returned an adjusted value of **0.1194** in the supplied output. Because the scale and confidence interval for this adjusted value were not documented, it should not be interpreted as a confirmed adjusted RR. Considering the nonsignificant Egger test, the trim-and-fill result, and the very small number of studies, the supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **moderate**. Trim-and-fill is exploratory and cannot distinguish publication bias from genuine heterogeneity or other small-study effects.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across five studies and 260 participants, perioperative diclofenac was not shown to reduce or increase the risk of requiring rescue or supplementary analgesia compared with opioid analgesia. The pooled RR of **1.0412006887031535** was close to the null, and the supplied favourability classification was neither arm. However, the 95% CI of **0.365 to 2.973** was too wide to establish clinically meaningful equivalence or non-inferiority.

The principal interpretive concern is the high heterogeneity. An I² of **87.55%** indicates marked inconsistency among the included comparisons. Such inconsistency is clinically plausible in a broad question that permits any paediatric surgical procedure, multiple diclofenac doses and routes, diverse opioids and administration routes, and outcome windows ranging from early recovery through 24 hours. Without trial-level clinical details and event counts, the sources and consequences of this variability cannot be resolved.

### Clinical interpretation

The current result should not be read as evidence that diclofenac and opioids are definitively equivalent. A nonsignificant result demonstrates only that a difference was not detected under the available data and model. The interval estimate permits a potentially important reduction in rescue analgesia with diclofenac as well as a potentially large increase. Treatment decisions should therefore continue to account for the surgical context, contraindications to NSAIDs, renal and bleeding considerations, opioid-related adverse effects, and multimodal analgesic protocols. These additional efficacy and safety outcomes were outside the scope of the present analysis.

### Implications for research

Future trials should report, by randomised arm, the number of children who received at least one rescue analgesic and the corresponding denominator at standard cumulative windows, ideally including 24 hours. Protocols should define rescue thresholds and permitted medications in advance. Trials should also report allocation concealment, masking, attrition, all randomised participants, and adverse events. A larger evidence base could support subgroup analyses by age, procedure, route, dose, timing, and opioid comparator.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations. First, only five studies and 260 participants were available, producing low precision and limited power for pooled inference, heterogeneity investigation, and publication-bias testing. Second, heterogeneity was considerable and could not be explained through subgroup analysis or meta-regression. Third, the absence of arm-level event counts prevented independent recalculation of individual study RRs, continuity-correction assessment, and verification of multi-arm pooling. Fourth, study characteristics and risk-of-bias assessments were unavailable, preventing evaluation of internal validity, indirectness, and overall certainty of evidence. Fifth, search methods and screening counts were not supplied, so review completeness and PRISMA flow cannot be verified. Sixth, rescue-medication use may depend on local prescribing protocols and thresholds as well as analgesic efficacy. Finally, publication-bias analyses based on five studies are inherently unreliable.

## Conclusion

In children undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia, the available meta-analysis does not show a difference between perioperative diclofenac and opioid analgesia in the risk of requiring rescue or supplementary analgesia within the first 24 postoperative hours. The pooled risk ratio was **1.0412006887031535** (95% CI **0.365 to 2.973**), with the result classified as favouring neither intervention. Considerable heterogeneity (**I² = 87.55%**) and substantial imprecision preclude firm conclusions about comparative effectiveness. Better reported and adequately powered randomised trials are required.
