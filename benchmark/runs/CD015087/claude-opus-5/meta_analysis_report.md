# Perioperative Diclofenac versus Opioid Analgesia and the Need for Rescue Analgesia in Children Undergoing Surgery under General Anaesthesia: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Opioids remain the default perioperative analgesic for children, but concerns about respiratory depression, sedation, nausea and vomiting have motivated the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs such as diclofenac as opioid-sparing or opioid-replacing agents. Whether diclofenac alone controls early postoperative pain well enough to avoid rescue analgesia as reliably as an opioid is uncertain.

**Objective.** To determine whether, in children under 18 years undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia, perioperative diclofenac by any route compared with an opioid analgesic by any route reduces the number of children requiring rescue or supplementary analgesia within the first 24 hours after surgery.

**Methods.** Randomised controlled trials comparing perioperative diclofenac (rectal, intravenous, intramuscular or oral; pre-, intra- or post-operative) with any opioid comparator (morphine, fentanyl, sufentanil, pethidine/meperidine, papaveretum, nalbuphine; any route including caudal/epidural) in children undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia were eligible. The outcome was the dichotomous count of children receiving any rescue or supplementary analgesic after surgery, taken over the longest reported postoperative window up to 24 hours. In multi-arm trials the diclofenac arm formed the treatment group and all opioid arms were summed into a single control group; placebo/no-analgesia, local anaesthetic, paracetamol and other NSAID arms were disregarded. Data were pooled on the risk ratio scale using a random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Five trials contributing five effect estimates and 260 randomised children were included. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for requiring rescue or supplementary analgesia with diclofenac versus an opioid was 1.0412 (95% CI 0.365 to 2.973; p = 0.9399; k = 5; n = 260), a result that favours neither arm — it is essentially a statistical tie, and for a ratio measure the direction of benefit additionally depends on which event was counted. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial to considerable: Q = 19.623 on 4 degrees of freedom (p = 0.0006), I² = 87.55%, τ² = 1.1323. Individual standardized study estimates ranged from −0.9373 (95% CI −1.590 to −0.284) to 1.4981 (95% CI 0.941 to 2.055), with one trial strongly favouring diclofenac, one trial neutral and three favouring the opioid comparator, two of them at or beyond conventional significance. Egger's test was not statistically significant (intercept 8.1065, p = 0.1179) and trim-and-fill imputed a single missing study (adjusted estimate 0.1194), yielding an overall small-study-effect risk rated moderate.

**Conclusions.** On the available evidence, perioperative diclofenac is neither clearly superior nor clearly inferior to an opioid analgesic for preventing the need for rescue or supplementary analgesia in the first 24 postoperative hours in children. The point estimate is almost exactly null, but the confidence interval is wide enough to encompass both a large reduction and a near-tripling of the proportion of children needing additional analgesia, and heterogeneity is considerable (I² = 87.55%). The evidence base is small (5 trials, 260 children), clinically diverse in drug route, comparator opioid, surgical procedure and observation window, and carries a moderate risk of small-study effects; firm clinical recommendations cannot be drawn and adequately powered, procedure-specific trials with standardised rescue-analgesia criteria are required.

## 1. Introduction

Pain after surgery in childhood is frequently undertreated, and the choice of the primary perioperative analgesic has consequences that extend well beyond pain scores. Opioids — morphine, fentanyl, sufentanil, pethidine (meperidine), papaveretum and the agonist–antagonist nalbuphine — provide reliable analgesia across a wide range of paediatric procedures and remain the reference standard in many institutions. Their adverse-effect profile, however, is a persistent clinical concern: dose-dependent respiratory depression and apnoea (especially in infants, in children with obstructive sleep apnoea and after adenotonsillectomy), sedation that delays discharge from day-case units, postoperative nausea and vomiting, urinary retention and pruritus. These concerns have driven interest in non-opioid regimens built around non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs).

Diclofenac is attractive in this role. It is a potent cyclo-oxygenase inhibitor with established paediatric dosing, is available in rectal suppository form that can be administered after induction of anaesthesia (avoiding the distress of oral or injected premedication in a frightened child), and has a duration of action that spans the early postoperative period. Against this must be set the theoretical and empirical risks of NSAIDs in children: platelet inhibition and perioperative bleeding (notably post-tonsillectomy haemorrhage), renal effects in the hypovolaemic patient, and bronchospasm in aspirin-sensitive asthma.

The pragmatic clinical question is therefore not whether diclofenac produces lower pain scores on some scale, but whether a diclofenac-based regimen is good enough — whether children given diclofenac need to be rescued with additional analgesia more or less often than children given an opioid. "Number of children requiring rescue or supplementary analgesia" is a patient-relevant, binary, clinically actionable endpoint that integrates the adequacy of analgesia over time, is recorded in routine practice, and is reported (under a variety of names: *analgesic complementation*, *supplementary analgesia*, *postoperative analgesia given*, *number requiring opioid/paracetamol postoperatively*) in many paediatric analgesia trials.

This systematic review and meta-analysis was undertaken to answer the following question: **In children under 18 undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia, does perioperative diclofenac (any route) compared with an opioid analgesic (any route) reduce the number of children requiring rescue or supplementary analgesia within the first 24 hours after surgery?**

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible according to the following PICOS framework.

| Element | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Children under 18 years of age undergoing surgery of any type under general anaesthesia |
| **Intervention** | Diclofenac by any route — rectal, intravenous, intramuscular or oral — administered pre-operatively, intra-operatively or immediately post-operatively |
| **Comparator** | Any opioid analgesic (morphine, fentanyl, sufentanil, pethidine/meperidine, papaveretum, nalbuphine and congeners), by any route including caudal and epidural administration |
| **Outcome** | Dichotomous count of children who received any rescue or supplementary analgesic after surgery, out of the number randomised/analysed in each arm, within the first 24 postoperative hours |
| **Study design** | Randomised controlled trial with a parallel diclofenac arm and at least one opioid arm |

### 2.2 Information sources and screening

Records were identified through electronic database searching and screened in two stages — title/abstract followed by full text — against the criteria above. Records were excluded at full text when they were not randomised, when no diclofenac arm or no opioid arm was present, when the population included adults without separable paediatric data, or when no extractable dichotomous count of rescue/supplementary analgesia within 24 hours could be obtained.

### 2.3 Data extraction conventions

Several pre-specified conventions governed extraction and are important for interpreting the pooled result:

1. **Longest window up to 24 hours.** Where a trial reported the number of children needing supplementary analgesia at several timepoints, the count covering the longest postoperative window up to 24 hours was used (e.g. recovery-ward stay, first four hours, until discharge, or the full 24 hours). A count limited to the first two hours was never used when a longer window was also reported.
2. **Percentage-to-count conversion.** Where results were presented as percentages — including percentages read from figures and bar charts — these were converted back to integer counts using the reported group size.
3. **Multi-arm trials.** The diclofenac arm served as the treatment group and *all* opioid arms were summed into a single control group. Arms receiving no analgesia or placebo, local anaesthetic, paracetamol, or a different NSAID were ignored, so that each trial contributed a single, non-overlapping diclofenac-versus-opioid comparison and no participant was double-counted.

### 2.4 Screening yield

Screening and eligibility assessment yielded **5 randomised trials**, contributing **5 independent effect estimates** and **260 randomised/analysed children** in total, with no trial contributing more than one estimate to the analysis. Because each study provided exactly one effect size for one outcome construct at one harmonised timepoint window, the analysis comprises a **single meta-analytic group**; no separate outcome or timepoint subsets required independent pooling.

## 3. Included Studies

Five trials met all criteria. Their characteristics, as carried through the analysis pipeline, are summarised below. Study identifiers correspond to the effect-size labels used in the forest plot.

| Study ID | Effect label | n analysed (both arms) | Standardized estimate (Hedges' g) | 95% CI | Direction of the individual estimate |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Study 1 | study_1_R1 | 80 | 1.4981 | 0.941 to 2.055 | Favours diclofenac (fewer children needed rescue analgesia) |
| Study 2 | study_2_R2 | 50 | 0.0869 | −0.468 to 0.642 | Essentially null |
| Study 3 | study_3_R3 | 40 | −0.9373 | −1.590 to −0.284 | Favours opioid |
| Study 4 | study_4_R4 | 40 | −0.5937 | −1.228 to 0.040 | Favours opioid (CI touches the null) |
| Study 5 | study_5_R5 | 50 | −0.5323 | −1.096 to 0.032 | Favours opioid (CI touches the null) |

Key features of the evidence base:

- **Size.** The trials are small, with 40 to 80 analysed children each (median 50). No trial exceeded 80 participants, and the entire evidence base comprises 260 children — smaller than many single modern paediatric analgesia trials.
- **Clinical diversity.** The eligibility criteria were deliberately broad with respect to diclofenac route (rectal, intravenous, intramuscular, oral), timing (pre-, intra- or post-operative), comparator opioid (from short-acting fentanyl/sufentanil to long-acting morphine and papaveretum) and comparator route (systemic versus caudal/epidural), as well as surgical procedure. This breadth maximises the yield of trials and the generalisability of any pooled answer, but it also means the included studies are not estimating a single common treatment effect.
- **Outcome definition heterogeneity.** The same construct was reported under at least four different labels and over observation windows ranging from the recovery-room stay to the full 24 hours. Even after applying the "longest window up to 24 h" rule, the effective follow-up period differs across trials, and the threshold at which clinicians elected to give rescue analgesia was not standardised.

### 3.1 Risk of bias considerations

Formal domain-level risk-of-bias ratings were not available in the extracted dataset and are therefore not tabulated here; this is noted as a limitation in Section 10. Two generic concerns deserve emphasis for this outcome. First, **blinding of the person deciding to give rescue analgesia** is critical, because the outcome is a clinician decision rather than an objective measurement; unblinded assessors who know a child received "only an NSAID" may have a lower threshold for supplementation. Second, trials comparing a rectal suppository with an intravenous or caudal opioid cannot be blinded without a double-dummy design, which small single-centre trials often omit.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure

The outcome is a count of events (children requiring rescue analgesia) out of a group total, and the analysis was therefore conducted on the **risk ratio (RR)** scale. The effect measure was set by explicit specification for this review (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`), there was no conflict between the specified and detected measures, and **native pooling on the RR scale was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`). The pooled result reported in Section 5 is therefore a confirmed native-scale risk ratio, not a standardized surrogate. It must **not** be described as a Hedges' g.

For display and cross-study comparison, the pipeline additionally expressed each trial's result as a standardized estimate (Hedges' g); those per-study values are the ones tabulated in Section 3 and plotted in the distribution figure. Readers should note the dual reporting: **per-study standardized values are on the g scale with the convention that a positive g favours diclofenac (equivalently RR < 1), whereas the pooled estimate in Section 5 is a risk ratio with the opposite raw sign convention** (RR > 1 indicating a higher proportion requiring rescue analgesia in the diclofenac arm). Because of this, the raw sign of the pooled number should not be used to infer favourability; the direction is reported explicitly in Section 5.

### 4.2 Pattern across studies

The five estimates do not tell a consistent story. One trial (Study 1, the largest at n = 80) produced a large effect strongly in favour of diclofenac (g = 1.4981, 95% CI 0.941 to 2.055) with a confidence interval excluding the null by a wide margin. At the other extreme, Study 3 produced a large effect in the opposite direction (g = −0.9373, 95% CI −1.590 to −0.284), also excluding the null. Studies 4 and 5 point in the same direction as Study 3 with confidence intervals that just touch the null (upper limits 0.040 and 0.032 respectively), and Study 2 is almost exactly null (g = 0.0869, 95% CI −0.468 to 0.642).

The confidence intervals of Study 1 and Study 3 do not overlap at all. This is the clearest possible visual signal that the trials are not estimating a common effect, and it anticipates the formal heterogeneity statistics reported in Section 6.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model was fitted by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**, which is appropriate given the anticipated clinical diversity and performs acceptably with small numbers of studies while remaining less biased than DerSimonian–Laird for τ².

> **Primary result.** Pooled **Risk Ratio (RR) = 1.0412** (95% CI **0.365 to 2.973**), p = **0.9399**, k = **5** trials, n = **260** children; REML random-effects model, native RR-scale pooling.

**Direction.** This result **favours neither arm** — it is essentially a statistical tie, and because this is a ratio measure the direction of favourability additionally depends on which event was counted. There is no detectable difference between perioperative diclofenac and an opioid analgesic in the proportion of children requiring rescue or supplementary analgesia within the first 24 postoperative hours.

**Precision.** The p-value of 0.9399 is about as far from significance as is arithmetically possible, and the point estimate (1.0412) is within 4% of unity. It is essential, however, not to misread this as a demonstrated equivalence. The 95% confidence interval spans **0.365 to 2.973** — that is, from a 63.5% *relative reduction* in the number of children needing rescue analgesia to a nearly **three-fold increase**. Both of these boundary scenarios would be clinically decisive and would lead to opposite practice recommendations. The honest summary is therefore *uninformative with a null point estimate*, not *equivalent*.

**Interpretation in absolute terms.** If a baseline risk of requiring rescue analgesia with opioids is taken as, say, 40%, the pooled RR and its interval translate to a diclofenac risk of roughly 42% (95% CI approximately 15% to >99%) — illustrating that the data are compatible with diclofenac being substantially better, identical, or very much worse.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **substantial to considerable** and is the dominant feature of this analysis.

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 19.623 (df = 4) | Observed dispersion far exceeds that expected from sampling error alone |
| p-value for Q | 0.0006 | Highly significant; the null hypothesis of homogeneity is firmly rejected |
| I² | 87.55% | ~88% of the observed variability reflects real between-study differences rather than chance; in Cochrane terms this is "considerable heterogeneity" |
| τ² | 1.1323 | Large between-study variance on the analysis scale |
| k | 5 | Few studies, so τ² and I² are themselves imprecisely estimated |

Q = 19.623 against 4 degrees of freedom means the observed dispersion is nearly five times what random sampling would generate, with p = 0.0006. I² = 87.55% sits at the top of the conventional heterogeneity scale. With τ² = 1.1323, the implied 95% **prediction interval** for the effect in a future, comparable trial is far wider than the confidence interval around the mean and would comfortably include large effects in both directions — meaning that the result of the next trial of diclofenac versus an opioid cannot be forecast from this evidence with any useful precision.

Three observations follow. First, the pooled mean of 1.0412 is a mean of genuinely conflicting findings, not a consensus; the near-null value arises from the cancellation of one large pro-diclofenac result against three pro-opioid results, rather than from agreement. Second, with k = 5 the heterogeneity estimates have wide uncertainty, so the true I² could plausibly be lower (or higher) than 87.55%, though the significant Q statistic makes genuine heterogeneity very likely. Third, the clinical and methodological diversity catalogued in Section 3 — route of diclofenac, choice and route of opioid (systemic versus caudal/epidural), surgical procedure and pain intensity, and the length and definition of the rescue-analgesia observation window — provides ample candidate explanations.

## 7. Moderators

Given I² = 87.55%, identifying moderators would be the most scientifically valuable step available — but with **k = 5** it is not statistically feasible. Conventional guidance recommends at least ten studies per covariate for meta-regression; with five studies, any meta-regression would be essentially unidentified, and subgroup analyses would reduce to comparisons of one or two trials per subgroup with no useful power and a high false-positive rate.

A meta-regression of effect size against study size was explored descriptively and is displayed as a bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`), in which marker area is proportional to study weight. The visual pattern is consistent with the small-study diagnostics in Section 8: the single largest trial (n = 80) carries the one strongly pro-diclofenac estimate, while the smaller trials (n = 40–50) cluster on the pro-opioid side. This association of effect direction with study size is a correlation in five data points and must not be over-interpreted — it is equally compatible with chance, with genuine clinical differences between the trials, or with small-study effects.

**No formal moderator analyses were conducted, and none should be inferred from this report.** Pre-specified moderators that would be worth testing once the evidence base is larger include: (i) route of diclofenac (rectal versus parenteral versus oral); (ii) timing of administration (pre-emptive versus post-incision); (iii) comparator opioid class and route, especially systemic versus caudal/epidural, since a caudal block is a qualitatively different intervention from an intravenous bolus; (iv) surgical procedure and expected pain intensity (e.g. myringotomy versus tonsillectomy versus orthopaedic surgery); (v) length and definition of the rescue-analgesia observation window; and (vi) blinding of the clinician authorising rescue analgesia.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 8.1065 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.1179 |
| Trim-and-fill studies imputed (k₀) | 1 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate | 0.1194 |
| Overall small-study-effect risk rating | **Moderate** |

**Interpretation.** Egger's regression test did not detect statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.1179, above the conventional 0.05 threshold), and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed only one hypothetically missing study — indicating that any small-study effects are minor and that the bias-adjusted pooled estimate would likely differ little from the original.

**Caveat.** The non-significant p-value should not be read as evidence of *no* bias. Egger's test has low statistical power when the number of included studies is small, and with k = 5 it is seriously underpowered; a p-value in this intermediate range leaves genuine uncertainty rather than reassurance. The intercept itself (8.1065) is numerically large, which is what drives the ambiguity: the direction of the asymmetry signal is not negligible, it is simply too imprecisely estimated to reach significance. Note also that Egger's test is known to be anti-conservative for ratio measures of binary outcomes, and that any asymmetry in a field this heterogeneous may reflect genuine between-trial differences rather than selective publication.

**Overall.** The "moderate" risk rating appropriately reflects this ambiguity — the quantitative diagnostics are reassuring but not conclusive, so the pooled effect should be interpreted with mild caution and ideally corroborated by visual funnel plot inspection and sensitivity analyses (e.g. excluding the smallest studies). The single imputed study and a trim-and-fill adjusted value of 0.1194 (reported on the pipeline's internal computation scale) suggest that correcting for the suspected asymmetry would not overturn the broad conclusion of "no demonstrated difference", though it would shift the estimate.

Beyond publication bias narrowly defined, this literature is susceptible to related small-study phenomena: single-centre trials with idiosyncratic rescue-analgesia protocols, selective reporting of the timepoint that showed the most favourable result (partially mitigated here by the pre-specified "longest window up to 24 h" extraction rule), and the extraction of several counts from figures and bar charts rather than from tabulated numbers, which introduces reading error.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Main finding

Across 5 randomised trials and 260 children, perioperative diclofenac and perioperative opioid analgesia did not differ detectably in the proportion of children who required rescue or supplementary analgesia within the first 24 hours after surgery (pooled RR 1.0412, 95% CI 0.365 to 2.973, p = 0.9399). The result **favours neither arm**. The practical message is that the available randomised evidence is **too sparse and too inconsistent to establish either superiority or non-inferiority** of a diclofenac-based regimen on this endpoint.

### 9.2 Why "no significant difference" is not "no difference"

Three features of this analysis make an equivalence claim untenable. (1) The confidence interval spans a 63.5% relative reduction to a near-tripling of the rescue-analgesia rate; an equivalence claim would require the interval to lie within a pre-specified margin, which it does not remotely do. (2) Heterogeneity is considerable (I² = 87.55%, Q = 19.623, p = 0.0006, τ² = 1.1323), so the pooled mean describes the centre of a broad distribution of true effects rather than a single common effect; the prediction interval for a future trial is wider still. (3) Total sample size (260) is roughly an order of magnitude below what would be needed to detect a clinically modest difference in a binary outcome with a baseline event rate in the 30–50% range.

### 9.3 Reconciling the conflicting trials

The disagreement between Study 1 (strongly favouring diclofenac) and Studies 3–5 (favouring the opioid) is biologically plausible rather than paradoxical, and the broad inclusion criteria supply several candidate explanations:

- **Comparator potency and route.** An opioid delivered as a caudal or epidural block provides dense, prolonged, site-specific analgesia that an NSAID is unlikely to match; a short-acting intraoperative fentanyl bolus, by contrast, may have largely worn off by the time rescue decisions are made in recovery, favouring the longer-acting diclofenac. Trials of these two comparator types are, in effect, asking different questions.
- **Surgical pain intensity.** In low-to-moderate pain procedures (e.g. myringotomy, minor day-case surgery), diclofenac may be fully sufficient; in high-intensity procedures, NSAID monotherapy is more likely to require supplementation.
- **Timing and pharmacokinetics.** Rectal diclofenac given at induction has a slow, variable absorption profile; whether its peak effect coincides with the period of maximum pain determines whether rescue analgesia is needed, and this varies with the length of the operation.
- **Observation window and rescue threshold.** A trial counting supplementation over 24 hours will record more events than one counting only the recovery-ward stay, and the direction of the resulting bias depends on which arm's analgesia wanes first. Unblinded clinicians may also apply different thresholds to a child known to have received "only a suppository".

### 9.4 Clinical implications

Clinicians should not read this meta-analysis as licence to substitute diclofenac for an opioid on the grounds of proven equivalent analgesic adequacy, nor as evidence that diclofenac is inadequate. The decision should continue to rest on the expected pain intensity of the specific procedure, patient-specific contraindications to NSAIDs (bleeding risk, renal compromise, aspirin-sensitive asthma) and to opioids (obstructive sleep apnoea, age, day-case discharge pressures), and on multimodal regimens that combine agents rather than pitting them against one another. It is also worth noting that "requiring rescue analgesia" is only one dimension of comparative value: opioid-sparing strategies may be justified by reductions in nausea, vomiting, sedation and respiratory events even if the rescue-analgesia rate is unchanged — outcomes this analysis did not address.

### 9.5 Research implications

What is needed is not another 40-patient trial. Priorities are: adequately powered, procedure-specific randomised trials (single surgical population, single diclofenac route, single comparator opioid and route); a standardised, protocolised rescue-analgesia trigger (e.g. a defined score on a validated paediatric pain scale) with blinded assessors; a pre-specified, uniform 24-hour counting window; and full reporting of numerators and denominators in text or tables so that counts need not be read from bar charts. Co-reporting of opioid-related adverse events and NSAID-related bleeding would allow the trade-off, rather than just the analgesic endpoint, to be quantified.

## 10. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base.** Five trials and 260 children provide limited power; the analysis can neither confirm a difference nor exclude a clinically important one.
2. **Considerable unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 87.55% with τ² = 1.1323 and Q = 19.623 (p = 0.0006). The pooled mean averages estimates whose confidence intervals do not overlap (Study 1 versus Study 3), so it should be read as a summary of a disparate distribution rather than a single effect.
3. **Moderators could not be investigated.** With k = 5, meta-regression and subgroup analysis were not statistically feasible, so the sources of heterogeneity remain hypothesised rather than tested. The bubble chart is descriptive only.
4. **Broad, lumped comparisons.** All diclofenac routes and all opioids and routes (including caudal/epidural) were pooled by design. This clinical lumping is a deliberate trade of specificity for feasibility and is itself a likely driver of heterogeneity.
5. **Outcome definition variability.** The endpoint was reported under at least four different labels and over observation windows from recovery-room stay to 24 hours. The "longest window up to 24 h" rule harmonises extraction but does not make the windows equal.
6. **Data reconstruction.** Some counts were derived by converting percentages — including percentages read from figures and bar charts — back to integers using group sizes, introducing rounding and reading error.
7. **Blinding and outcome ascertainment.** The outcome is a clinician decision. Where blinding was absent or a double-dummy design was not used (likely in rectal-versus-intravenous comparisons), detection bias is plausible and would tend to inflate apparent differences in either direction.
8. **No formal risk-of-bias assessment table.** Domain-level risk-of-bias ratings were not available in the extracted dataset, so the certainty of evidence could not be graded formally; informally, the body of evidence would rate low to very low for imprecision and inconsistency alone.
9. **Residual uncertainty about small-study effects.** Egger's p = 0.1179 with an intercept of 8.1065 and one trim-and-fill imputed study yields a *moderate* risk rating — not an all-clear. With k = 5 these diagnostics are underpowered.
10. **Dual effect-measure reporting.** Pooling was performed natively on the risk ratio scale, while per-study estimates are displayed as standardized values with the opposite sign convention. Readers comparing the study table with the pooled estimate should rely on the explicitly stated direction ("favours neither arm") rather than on the arithmetic sign.
11. **Single outcome, single timepoint.** Only the 24-hour rescue-analgesia count was analysed. Pain scores, opioid consumption, adverse events, parental satisfaction and time to discharge were outside scope.

---

## 11. Conclusion

In children under 18 undergoing surgery under general anaesthesia, the available randomised evidence does **not** show that perioperative diclofenac reduces the number of children requiring rescue or supplementary analgesia in the first 24 postoperative hours relative to an opioid analgesic — nor does it show the reverse. Pooling 5 trials and 260 children on the native risk ratio scale with a REML random-effects model gave a **pooled Risk Ratio of 1.0412 (95% CI 0.365 to 2.973; p = 0.9399)**, a result that **favours neither arm**, accompanied by considerable heterogeneity (I² = 87.55%, τ² = 1.1323, Q = 19.623, p = 0.0006) and a moderate risk of small-study effects (Egger's intercept 8.1065, p = 0.1179; one study imputed by trim-and-fill).

The near-perfect null point estimate is the arithmetic consequence of averaging one trial that strongly favoured diclofenac against three that favoured the opioid, not of consistent agreement between trials; the confidence interval remains compatible with both a large benefit and a large harm. Practice should therefore continue to be guided by procedure-specific pain expectations, individual contraindications and multimodal principles rather than by any presumed equivalence of these two drug classes. Resolving the question will require adequately powered, procedure-specific, properly blinded trials with standardised rescue-analgesia criteria and a uniform 24-hour observation window.
