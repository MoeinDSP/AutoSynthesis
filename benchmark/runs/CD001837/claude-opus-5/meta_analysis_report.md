# Bupropion versus placebo or usual care for smoking cessation in hospitalised adults: a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of abstinence at ≥6 months

## Abstract

**Background.** Hospitalisation is a potential "teachable moment" for smoking cessation, and sustained-release bupropion is an established first-line pharmacotherapy in ambulatory settings. Whether its efficacy extends to smokers initiating treatment during an inpatient admission — including admissions for acute cardiovascular disease — remains uncertain.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of bupropion (alone or added to counselling) compared with placebo or no bupropion (usual care/counselling only) on smoking abstinence at the longest reported follow-up of at least six months in hospitalised adults who smoke.

**Methods.** Randomised controlled trials of hospitalised adult smokers comparing bupropion with placebo or no bupropion were eligible. The pre-specified outcome was the number of participants abstinent at the longest follow-up of ≥6 months, using the strictest abstinence definition each trial reported (sustained/continuous and biochemically validated in preference to point prevalence), analysed by intention-to-treat with all participants lost to follow-up counted as continuing smokers and denominators taken from the randomised numbers in the participant-flow diagram. Risk ratios (RR) were pooled on the native risk-ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Four studies were carried through screening and data extraction; three contributed analysable abstinence data at ≥6 months and were pooled (k = 3; N = 788 randomised participants). The pooled risk ratio was 1.164 (95% CI 0.905 to 1.496; p = 0.238), a result that favours neither arm — it is essentially a statistical tie, and for a ratio measure the direction of benefit additionally depends on which event was counted. Between-study heterogeneity was absent (Q = 1.523, df = 2, p = 0.467; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). Egger's regression showed no funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept 0.4656, p = 0.4532) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized estimate g = 0.1338), giving an overall low rated risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** In this small body of evidence, bupropion initiated in hospitalised smokers did not significantly increase long-term abstinence relative to placebo or usual care. The pooled confidence interval is compatible with anything from a ~10% relative reduction to a ~50% relative increase in quitting, so a clinically worthwhile benefit is neither demonstrated nor excluded. With only three pooled trials, the evidence is imprecise rather than null, and larger inpatient trials with biochemically validated sustained abstinence at ≥6 months are needed.


## 1. Introduction

Tobacco smoking remains the leading preventable cause of premature death worldwide, and smokers are over-represented among hospital inpatients. Admission to hospital creates an enforced period of abstinence, intensive clinical contact, and — particularly after a cardiovascular event — a heightened perception of personal risk. Together these have led to hospitalisation being described as a "teachable moment" at which cessation interventions may be unusually effective. Health-system guidance accordingly recommends that every hospitalised smoker be offered cessation support, and that pharmacotherapy be considered alongside behavioural counselling.

Sustained-release bupropion is a norepinephrine–dopamine reuptake inhibitor with established efficacy for smoking cessation in community and primary-care populations, where it approximately doubles long-term quit rates relative to placebo. Extrapolating that efficacy to the inpatient setting is not straightforward. Hospitalised smokers differ systematically from ambulatory quitters: they are more likely to have serious comorbidity, polypharmacy, and contraindications or cautions to bupropion (notably seizure risk, hepatic impairment, and acute cardiac instability); they begin treatment during a period of involuntary abstinence rather than at a self-chosen quit date; and the behavioural support available after discharge is frequently discontinuous. Concerns about bupropion in patients with acute coronary syndromes have also made clinicians cautious, even though cardiovascular safety signals in trials have generally been reassuring.

The question addressed here is therefore specific and clinically actionable: **in adults who smoke and are hospitalised for any reason, including cardiovascular admissions, does bupropion (alone or added to counselling) increase smoking abstinence at the longest follow-up of at least six months compared with placebo or no bupropion?** A risk ratio greater than 1 indicates that more participants were abstinent in the bupropion arm — that is, that bupropion increased long-term quitting among hospitalised smokers.

Only durable abstinence was of interest. Self-reported reduction in cigarette consumption, short-term (≤3 month) quitting, adverse events, cardiovascular events and withdrawal symptoms were explicitly outside the scope of the pooled analysis, since short-term abstinence in hospital is substantially confounded by the smoke-free inpatient environment and does not reliably predict sustained cessation.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults (≥18 years) who smoked cigarettes at the time of an inpatient hospital admission, for any admitting diagnosis, including but not restricted to acute cardiovascular admissions (myocardial infarction, unstable angina, revascularisation).

**Intervention.** Sustained-release bupropion, administered alone or added to behavioural counselling, with treatment initiated during or immediately around the index hospitalisation.

**Comparator.** Placebo or no bupropion — that is, usual care, or identical counselling without bupropion.

**Outcome.** The number of participants abstinent from smoking (events) out of the number randomised to each arm (total) at the longest reported follow-up of at least six months (typically 6 or 12 months). The strictest abstinence definition reported by each trial was used, preferring sustained or continuous abstinence and biochemically validated outcomes where available, and falling back to point-prevalence abstinence otherwise; the definition actually used was recorded for each trial. All analyses were intention-to-treat: participants lost to follow-up were counted as continuing smokers, and denominators were the numbers randomised as given in the participant-flow diagram. Where trials reported only percentage quit rates, these were converted to counts using the randomised denominators.

**Design.** Randomised controlled trials.

### 2.2 Information sources and search

A systematic search of the electronic literature was conducted for randomised trials of bupropion for smoking cessation in hospitalised populations, combining controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for the intervention (*bupropion*, *Zyban*, *amfebutamone*, *sustained-release bupropion*), the behaviour (*smoking cessation*, *tobacco use disorder*, *smoking abstinence*), and the setting (*hospitalised*, *inpatient*, *hospital discharge*, *myocardial infarction*, *acute coronary syndrome*). Reference lists of retrieved trials and of prior systematic reviews on hospital-initiated cessation interventions were screened for additional eligible reports.

### 2.3 Study selection and data extraction

Records were screened on title and abstract and then in full text against the eligibility criteria above. Reviews, editorials, protocols, duplicate records, and secondary publications of trials already represented in the dataset were removed so that each randomised cohort contributed only once. For each included trial the following were extracted: randomised denominators per arm, number abstinent per arm at the longest follow-up ≥6 months, the abstinence definition used (continuous/sustained vs point prevalence; biochemically validated vs self-reported), follow-up duration, admitting diagnosis mix, bupropion dose and duration, and the intensity of co-administered behavioural support.

### 2.4 Statistical analysis

The effect measure was the **risk ratio (RR)**, pre-specified by the review question and applied on its native scale; no conversion from another scale was required for the primary pooling (`native_pooling_used = true`, computation scale = RR, effect-measure source = explicit user request, with no measure conflict recorded). Trial-level risk ratios were pooled in a random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of the between-study variance τ². Heterogeneity was summarised by Cochran's Q with its p-value, the I² statistic, and τ². Small-study effects and potential publication bias were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure; because these diagnostics operate on a standardized scale, standardized (Hedges' g) equivalents of the trial effects were used for those ancillary computations only, and are reported as such.

Cumulative meta-analysis, an effect-size distribution plot, a bubble plot of effect size against study size, and a heterogeneity summary panel were generated as supporting diagnostics.

## 3. Search and Screening

The search-and-screening pipeline carried **four studies** through to the data-extraction stage. Of these, **three** yielded an extractable pair of event counts and randomised denominators for smoking abstinence at a follow-up of at least six months and entered the quantitative synthesis; the fourth did not contribute an analysable effect size at the pre-specified outcome and timepoint and therefore appears in the review as an included-but-not-pooled study (see §8, Data Quality & Validation Notes). The final quantitative dataset thus comprises **k = 3 effect sizes from 788 randomised participants**.

Records excluded during screening were removed for the conventional reasons: non-randomised design, review or commentary article type, outcomes limited to short-term (≤3 month) abstinence or to smoking reduction only, populations that were not hospitalised, and duplicate or companion publications of an already-represented randomised cohort.

The study sizes span a factor of about 2.6 (149 to 392 participants), so no single trial dominates the synthesis, though Trial 3 contributes close to half of the randomised participants.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Trial-level effects, expressed as standardized mean differences (Hedges' g) for diagnostic purposes, were:

| Study | Hedges' g | 95% CI | N |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trial 1 (study_1_R1) | −0.0564 | [−0.378, 0.265] | 149 |
| Trial 2 (study_2_R2) | 0.2444 | [−0.006, 0.495] | 247 |
| Trial 3 (study_3_R3) | 0.1369 | [−0.061, 0.335] | 392 |

Two of the three trials point in the direction of more quitting on bupropion (Trials 2 and 3), and one points marginally the other way (Trial 1); **all three individual confidence intervals include the null**, so no single trial independently demonstrates a statistically significant effect of bupropion on abstinence at ≥6 months. Trial 2 comes closest, with a lower confidence limit essentially touching zero (−0.006). The spread of point estimates is modest — all lie within about 0.3 standardized units of one another — which foreshadows the absence of heterogeneity in the formal pooling.

These standardized values are presented for transparency and because the publication-bias diagnostics are computed on that scale. **The primary synthesis below was performed on the native risk-ratio scale and should be read in risk-ratio units.** The distribution of trial-level effects is shown in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`, and their relationship to study size in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was specified, corresponding to the single pre-defined outcome (abstinence at the longest follow-up ≥6 months); no separate outcome- or timepoint-specific subgroups were required.

**Pooled result (random-effects, REML; k = 3; N = 788):**

- **Effect measure: Risk Ratio (RR)** — pooled on its native scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), so this is a confirmed native-scale risk-ratio result and not a standardized fallback.
- **Pooled risk ratio = 1.164** (95% CI **0.905 to 1.496**)
- **p = 0.238**
- **Direction: favours neither arm** — the result is essentially a tie; because this is a ratio measure, favourability additionally depends on which event was counted.

The point estimate corresponds to roughly a 16% relative increase in the probability of being abstinent at ≥6 months among participants allocated to bupropion, but the confidence interval spans from a 9.5% relative *decrease* to a 49.6% relative *increase*. The interval comfortably includes 1.0, and the two-sided p-value of 0.238 provides no evidence against the null hypothesis of no difference. In plain terms: in hospitalised smokers, bupropion did not significantly increase long-term abstinence compared with placebo or usual care, and the data are equally compatible with a null effect, a small harm, and a clinically meaningful benefit of the magnitude seen in ambulatory bupropion trials.

The forest plot of individual and pooled estimates is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. A cumulative meta-analysis, ordered to show how the pooled estimate evolved as evidence accrued, is at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; it illustrates that the pooled estimate has remained above 1.0 but with a confidence interval that has never excluded the null, reflecting the persistent imprecision of this small body of evidence rather than any instability in direction.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **absent** on every index examined:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 1.523 (df = 2) |
| Q p-value | 0.467 |
| I² | 0.0% |
| τ² (REML) | 0.0 |

Q is well below its degrees of freedom and the associated p-value of 0.467 gives no indication of inconsistency; I² of 0.0% implies that none of the observed variability in trial effects exceeds what sampling error alone would produce; and the REML estimate of the between-study variance is exactly zero, meaning the random-effects model collapses to the fixed-effect solution and the pooled estimate is effectively an inverse-variance-weighted average of three mutually consistent results.

This consistency is reassuring in one respect — the three trials are telling the same story, and the non-significant pooled result does not conceal opposing effects in different inpatient populations — but it must be interpreted cautiously. With only three studies, Q has very low power to detect genuine heterogeneity, and I² is known to be severely downwardly biased and unstable when k is small. An I² of 0.0% here should therefore be read as "no heterogeneity detectable", not as "heterogeneity confidently excluded". The heterogeneity diagnostics are summarised graphically at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 8. Moderators

No moderator or subgroup analyses were performed, and none could be defensibly performed. With k = 3 effect sizes, any meta-regression would have at most one degree of freedom for residual error, and conventional guidance (a minimum of roughly ten studies per covariate) is violated by an order of magnitude. Clinically interesting moderators — admitting diagnosis (cardiovascular versus general medical/surgical), bupropion dose and duration, the intensity and continuity of post-discharge behavioural support, follow-up length (6 versus 12 months), and abstinence definition (continuous and biochemically validated versus point prevalence) — could not be examined, and residual confounding by these factors cannot be ruled out despite the apparent statistical homogeneity.

The bubble plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) displays effect size against study size as an informal check for a size–effect gradient; with three points it is descriptive only and supports no inferential claim. A τ² of exactly zero does, however, mean there is no unexplained between-study variance for moderators to explain in this dataset — any moderator model fitted to these three trials would necessarily attribute all variation to sampling error.

## 9. Publication Bias

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.4656 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.4532 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (standardized g) | 0.1338 |
| Overall rated risk | Low |

Egger's regression test is non-significant (p = 0.4532), indicating no statistically detectable funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effect in this meta-analysis. This is corroborated by the trim-and-fill procedure, which imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), meaning the pooled estimate is unchanged after adjustment for potentially suppressed results — consistent with the "low" risk-of-publication-bias rating. The trim-and-fill adjusted standardized estimate of g = 0.1338 is, as expected, identical to the unadjusted standardized pooling.

A caveat must be stated alongside this: **absence of evidence is not evidence of absence.** These tests have low statistical power when fewer than about ten studies are pooled, or when between-study heterogeneity is high, and they detect only bias that manifests as a precision–effect size relationship. Outcome-reporting bias, selective analysis, and bias that affects large and small studies equally would not be captured. With k = 3, Egger's test is essentially uninformative, and the "low risk" rating should be understood as the absence of a detectable signal rather than positive assurance that the published inpatient bupropion literature is complete. Given that bupropion trials in hospitalised patients have often been investigator-initiated and modest in size, the possibility of unpublished neutral or negative trials cannot be dismissed.

## 10. Discussion

Across three randomised trials enrolling 788 hospitalised adult smokers, bupropion did not significantly increase the probability of smoking abstinence at the longest follow-up of at least six months relative to placebo or no bupropion (pooled risk ratio 1.164, 95% CI 0.905 to 1.496, p = 0.238). The result favours neither arm. Statistical heterogeneity was nil (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q p = 0.467), so this is a consistent set of individually non-significant trials rather than a mixture of divergent findings.

Three interpretive points follow. **First, the result is imprecise rather than genuinely null.** The upper confidence limit of 1.496 remains compatible with a 50% relative increase in long-term quitting — an effect that would be clinically important and, if real, would easily justify prescribing bupropion at discharge. The lower limit of 0.905 excludes only substantial harm to cessation. A meta-analysis of three trials totalling fewer than 800 participants simply does not have the precision to adjudicate effects of the size that cessation pharmacotherapy plausibly produces. The honest summary is that the evidence is inconclusive.

**Second, the point estimate is smaller than the effect bupropion achieves in ambulatory smokers**, where relative risks of long-term abstinence around 1.6 to 1.8 versus placebo are typical. Several features of the inpatient setting could plausibly attenuate the drug's effect: treatment is started during an enforced abstinence that blunts the withdrawal relief bupropion normally provides; motivation at the bedside may not survive discharge; post-discharge behavioural support is often brief or absent, and pharmacotherapy performs best when paired with sustained counselling; and adherence after discharge in sick, polypharmacy-burdened patients is frequently poor, including early discontinuation once the acute illness resolves. Comorbidity-related exclusions may also mean that inpatient trials enrol a population in whom the drug is less effective or less tolerable. Any of these mechanisms could shrink a true effect from the ambulatory benchmark toward the modest, non-significant estimate seen here.

**Third, the finding should not be read as evidence against cessation intervention in hospital generally.** The comparator in these trials was placebo or no bupropion, frequently on a background of counselling offered to both arms. A null pharmacological increment says nothing about the value of the behavioural scaffolding itself, which other literature supports — particularly intensive counselling with post-discharge follow-up. Nor does it address nicotine replacement therapy or varenicline in the same setting, which were outside the scope of this review.

For clinical practice, the implication is that bupropion initiated at hospitalisation cannot currently be recommended on the strength of demonstrated efficacy for long-term abstinence in this population, but neither is there evidence that it is ineffective. Where it is used, it should be embedded in a structured post-discharge support pathway rather than prescribed as a stand-alone intervention, since discontinuity of support is the most likely explanation for attenuated efficacy. For research, the clear need is for adequately powered inpatient trials — plausibly several thousand participants to detect a risk ratio of 1.2 with reasonable power — using biochemically validated continuous abstinence at twelve months, reporting adherence after discharge, and stratifying by admitting diagnosis.

## 11. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-handling observations should be stated plainly:

- **Four studies entered data extraction but only three contributed a pooled effect size** (`n_studies = 4`, `n_effect_sizes = 3`). One included study did not yield an extractable event/denominator pair for smoking abstinence at a follow-up of at least six months under the pre-specified intention-to-treat rules, and was therefore described narratively but excluded from the quantitative synthesis. Its omission means the pooled estimate does not represent all identified randomised evidence.
- **Duplicate and companion reports of the same randomised cohort were removed** during screening so that no trial population contributed more than once; review articles, commentaries and non-randomised reports were likewise excluded at that stage.
- **The effect measure was specified by the review question (explicit user request) and pooled natively as a risk ratio**; no effect-measure conflict was recorded, and no standardized fallback was required for the primary result. The Hedges' g values reported per study in §5, and the trim-and-fill adjusted value of g = 0.1338 in §9, are standardized quantities used for the publication-bias diagnostics only and must not be read as the primary effect.
- **Abstinence definitions were not uniform across trials.** The protocol required the strictest definition each trial reported, which means some contributing estimates are continuous/biochemically validated abstinence and others may be point-prevalence abstinence. This definitional mixing is a known source of non-comparability that could not be resolved or tested with k = 3.
- **Heterogeneity statistics (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0) are based on only three studies** and are statistically unstable at this k; they should not be treated as confirming homogeneity.
- **No moderator analyses were run**, because k = 3 makes meta-regression uninterpretable; this is a stated limitation, not an omission.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base.** Three pooled trials and 788 randomised participants give wide confidence intervals; the analysis is underpowered to detect the effect sizes of clinical interest, and the non-significant result must not be equated with demonstrated equivalence.
2. **Unstable heterogeneity and bias diagnostics.** With k = 3, Cochran's Q, I², τ², Egger's regression and trim-and-fill all have minimal power. The reassuring values obtained (I² = 0.0%, Egger p = 0.4532, k₀ = 0) are weak evidence, not strong evidence.
3. **Incomplete inclusion of identified evidence.** One of four extracted studies could not supply an analysable ≥6-month abstinence outcome and is absent from the pooled estimate.
4. **Heterogeneous outcome definitions.** Mixing continuous/validated and point-prevalence abstinence across trials introduces measurement non-comparability that could bias the pooled risk ratio in either direction.
5. **Clinical heterogeneity not assessable.** Admitting diagnosis, bupropion dose and duration, co-interventions, post-discharge support intensity, follow-up length and adherence could not be explored as moderators, so the pooled estimate averages over potentially important clinical variation.
6. **Risk of bias within trials not quantitatively incorporated.** Domain-level risk-of-bias judgements (allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessment, completeness of follow-up, selective reporting) were not available for sensitivity weighting; loss to follow-up in inpatient cessation trials is typically substantial, and although the intention-to-treat rule of counting non-responders as smokers is conservative, it can attenuate true differences.
7. **Scope restrictions.** Safety outcomes, cardiovascular events, withdrawal symptoms, smoking reduction and short-term (≤3 month) abstinence were deliberately not pooled, so this review speaks only to long-term cessation efficacy and makes no statement about the benefit–harm balance of bupropion in acutely ill inpatients.
8. **Generalisability.** The pooled population is dominated by one trial contributing nearly half the participants; findings may not transfer to inpatient groups under-represented in these trials, such as psychiatric, obstetric or surgical admissions.

## 13. Conclusion

Pooling three randomised controlled trials of 788 hospitalised adult smokers, bupropion — given alone or added to counselling — produced a pooled risk ratio for abstinence at the longest follow-up of at least six months of **1.164 (95% CI 0.905 to 1.496; p = 0.238)**, a confirmed native-scale risk-ratio estimate that **favours neither arm**. Between-study heterogeneity was undetectable (Q = 1.523, df = 2, p = 0.467; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), and there was no detectable publication bias (Egger's p = 0.4532; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0; low rated risk). The point estimate leans toward more quitting on bupropion but falls well short of statistical significance, and the confidence interval remains compatible both with no effect and with a clinically worthwhile ~50% relative increase in long-term abstinence. The appropriate conclusion is that bupropion initiated during hospitalisation has **not been shown** to increase long-term smoking abstinence in this population, and equally has **not been shown** to be ineffective. Resolving this will require substantially larger inpatient trials with biochemically validated continuous abstinence at twelve months, documented post-discharge adherence and support, and pre-specified stratification by admitting diagnosis.
