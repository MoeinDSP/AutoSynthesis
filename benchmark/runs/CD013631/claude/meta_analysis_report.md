# Probiotic Supplementation and Serum Creatinine in Chronic Kidney Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials (8–24 Weeks)

## Abstract

**Background.** Gut dysbiosis and the accumulation of gut-derived uraemic retention solutes are increasingly implicated in the progression of chronic kidney disease (CKD). Probiotic supplementation has been proposed as a low-cost adjunct capable of modifying the intestinal microbiota and, potentially, of preserving residual kidney function. Whether probiotics measurably alter serum creatinine — the most widely used clinical marker of excretory kidney function — remains uncertain.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of probiotic supplementation (single- or multi-strain capsules/sachets, or probiotic-containing foods such as probiotic yoghurt, soy milk or honey) compared with placebo or no treatment on serum creatinine (mg/dL) in adults with CKD of any stage, including diabetic nephropathy and dialysis-dependent disease, after approximately 8 to 24 weeks of treatment.

**Methods.** Randomised controlled trials comparing probiotics with placebo or no treatment in CKD populations and reporting serum creatinine at the end of an 8–24-week treatment period were eligible. End-of-treatment means, standard deviations and per-arm sample sizes were extracted; where only change-from-baseline data were available, mean change and SD of change were used consistently for both arms. Values reported in µmol/L were converted to mg/dL by dividing by 88.4; standard errors were converted to SDs as SE × √n. Multi-arm trials contributed a single pooled probiotic arm. Data were synthesised using a random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), with the mean difference (MD) in mg/dL as the pre-specified, user-requested effect measure. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Five randomised controlled trials contributing five independent effect sizes and 253 randomised participants were included. The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was **−0.153 mg/dL (95% CI −0.237 to −0.069; p = 0.00038; k = 5; N = 253)**, a result that **favours probiotic supplementation** over placebo/no treatment. Between-study heterogeneity was absent (Q = 1.404, df = 4, p = 0.8435; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), indicating that the five trials are statistically consistent with a single common underlying effect. Study-level standardised effects ranged from −1.108 (95% CI −1.774 to −0.443) to 0.000 (95% CI −0.506 to 0.506), with four of five point estimates on the side favouring probiotics and one exactly null. Egger's regression showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = 1.2704, p = 0.1444) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), supporting a low assessed risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** In this small but statistically homogeneous evidence base, probiotic supplementation for 8–24 weeks was associated with a statistically significant reduction in serum creatinine of approximately 0.15 mg/dL relative to placebo or no treatment in people with CKD. The magnitude is modest and of uncertain clinical importance at the individual-patient level, and the evidence base comprises only five small trials with short follow-up. Adequately powered trials reporting hard kidney endpoints (eGFR slope, progression to kidney failure) are required before probiotics can be recommended as a renoprotective intervention.

**Registration.** Not registered; conducted and reported in accordance with PRISMA 2020.

## 1. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects an estimated 8–16% of the world's adult population and is a leading contributor to cardiovascular morbidity, premature mortality and health-system expenditure. Despite the success of renin–angiotensin system blockade and, more recently, sodium-glucose co-transporter-2 inhibition and non-steroidal mineralocorticoid receptor antagonism, a substantial residual risk of progression persists. This has motivated a search for complementary, low-risk, low-cost interventions that target mechanisms not addressed by current pharmacotherapy.

One such mechanism is the "gut–kidney axis". As glomerular filtration declines, urea and other nitrogenous waste products diffuse into the intestinal lumen, where bacterial ureases, tryptophanases and tyrosine phenol-lyases generate ammonia, indoles and phenols. The resulting shift in microbial ecology favours proteolytic, toxin-producing taxa at the expense of saccharolytic commensals, loosens epithelial tight junctions and promotes endotoxin translocation, systemic inflammation and oxidative stress. The gut-derived protein-bound uraemic toxins indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate are, in turn, associated with tubulointerstitial fibrosis, endothelial dysfunction and faster CKD progression in observational cohorts. If probiotic organisms — typically *Lactobacillus*, *Bifidobacterium* and *Streptococcus* species delivered as capsules, sachets, or fermented foods such as probiotic yoghurt, soy milk or honey — can shift this ecology, they might plausibly slow the loss of excretory function.

Serum creatinine remains the most frequently reported and most widely available index of excretory kidney function in randomised trials of CKD, and is the input variable for all routinely used estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) equations. Although individual randomised controlled trials (RCTs) of probiotics in CKD have reported reductions in serum creatinine, most have been small, of short duration, and heterogeneous in strain composition, dose, vehicle and population (pre-dialysis CKD, diabetic nephropathy, haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis). Narrative reviews have therefore reached divergent conclusions.

**Objective.** The present systematic review and meta-analysis was undertaken to answer a single, pre-specified question: *in people with CKD of any stage, including diabetic nephropathy and dialysis-dependent disease, what is the effect of probiotic supplementation compared with placebo or no treatment on serum creatinine (mg/dL) measured at the end of approximately 8 to 24 weeks of treatment?*

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Information sources and search strategy

The evidence base for this synthesis was assembled through a structured, automated literature retrieval and screening pipeline directed at the research question stated above. The search targeted bibliographic records of randomised controlled trials evaluating probiotic supplementation in populations with chronic kidney disease, combining controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for the population (*chronic kidney disease, chronic renal failure, chronic renal insufficiency, diabetic nephropathy, haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, end-stage renal disease, uraemia*), the intervention (*probiotic, Lactobacillus, Bifidobacterium, Streptococcus thermophilus, synbiotic, fermented milk, probiotic yoghurt, probiotic soy milk, probiotic honey*), the comparator (*placebo, control, usual care*), and the outcome (*serum creatinine, kidney function, renal function, glomerular filtration rate*), restricted by a randomised-trial filter.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

Records were screened against the following pre-specified PICOS criteria.

| Domain | Inclusion | Exclusion |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** | Adults with CKD of any stage (1–5), including diabetic nephropathy and patients receiving maintenance haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis | Acute kidney injury only; kidney transplant recipients without CKD; healthy volunteers; paediatric-only cohorts |
| **Intervention** | Probiotic supplementation as single- or multi-strain capsules, sachets or powders, or probiotic-containing foods (yoghurt, soy milk, honey) | Prebiotic-only or fibre-only interventions; synbiotic arms where the probiotic effect could not be isolated; faecal microbiota transplantation |
| **Comparator** | Placebo or no treatment (no probiotic) | Active comparator only (e.g. another probiotic, a prebiotic, or an adsorbent) |
| **Outcome** | Serum creatinine at the end of treatment, extractable as mean, SD and n per arm, in mg/dL or convertible units | Creatinine not reported; no dispersion measure reported and none derivable |
| **Study design / timing** | Randomised controlled trial (parallel or cross-over) with treatment duration of approximately 8–24 weeks | Non-randomised, single-arm, observational; treatment duration <8 weeks or >24 weeks without an intermediate ≤24-week assessment |

### 2.3 Screening and selection

Retrieved records were de-duplicated and screened in two stages — title/abstract, then full text — against the criteria above. Non-trial document types (narrative reviews, systematic reviews, editorials, conference abstracts without extractable data), studies in non-CKD populations, studies without a probiotic-versus-placebo/no-treatment contrast, and trials that did not report serum creatinine with an extractable measure of dispersion at a timepoint between 8 and 24 weeks were excluded. Where multiple reports described the same randomised cohort, a single report was retained to prevent double-counting of participants.

### 2.4 Study flow

Screening culminated in **five randomised controlled trials** that satisfied all eligibility criteria and contributed **five independent effect sizes** and **253 randomised participants** to the quantitative synthesis. No included trial contributed more than one effect size, so no within-study dependency adjustment (multilevel modelling or robust variance estimation) was required.

### 2.5 Data extraction and preparation

For each included trial the following were extracted: first author and year, country, CKD stage/setting (pre-dialysis, diabetic nephropathy, dialysis), probiotic genera/strains and vehicle, daily colony-forming-unit dose, treatment duration, randomised and analysed sample size per arm, and the serum creatinine mean and SD per arm at the end of treatment.

Pre-specified data-handling rules were applied uniformly:

1. **End-of-treatment values were preferred.** Where a trial reported only change from baseline, the mean change and the SD of the change were used for *both* arms; end-of-treatment values and change scores were never mixed within a study, and the use of change scores was recorded.
2. **Multiple follow-ups.** Where a trial reported more than one post-randomisation assessment, the last assessment falling within 24 weeks was used.
3. **Unit conversion.** Creatinine reported in µmol/L was converted to mg/dL by division by 88.4; the original unit and the conversion were recorded for each affected study.
4. **Dispersion conversion.** Standard errors were converted to standard deviations as SE × √n, with table footnotes checked to establish whether "±" denoted SD or SE.
5. **Sign preservation.** Minus signs on change scores were preserved (a fall in creatinine is negative) and the reported number of decimal places was retained exactly.
6. **Arm assignment.** Arms were assigned by group definition rather than by column position, recognising that the placebo arm is frequently tabulated first; where column headings were ambiguous or lost, the arm was inferred from the narrative text and the inference recorded.
7. **Multi-arm trials.** Only the probiotic and placebo/no-treatment arms were used; where a trial randomised participants to more than one probiotic arm, those arms were combined into a single group using the standard formulae for the combined mean and combined SD, and arms receiving other (non-probiotic) supplements were ignored.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure

The pre-specified and user-requested effect measure for this synthesis was the **Mean Difference (MD)** in serum creatinine, expressed in the native clinical unit of **mg/dL**. The pooling was carried out **natively on the MD scale** (`native_pooling_used = true`; `effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`; `computation_scale = MD`), with no conflict detected between the requested and the computed measure. The headline pooled statistic reported in Section 5 is therefore a **confirmed native-scale mean difference**, not a standardised surrogate.

By the convention adopted here, **a negative mean difference indicates that serum creatinine was lower in the probiotic arm than in the placebo/no-treatment arm**, i.e. a result favouring probiotics and corresponding to better excretory kidney function. Readers should note that for this outcome a *lower* raw value is the *better* value; the direction of benefit reported below is taken directly from the `favors` field of the analysis output rather than inferred from the arithmetic sign.

### 4.2 Study-level effect estimates

In addition to the native-scale mean differences used for pooling, the extraction pipeline computed a standardised study-level effect (Hedges' *g*, small-sample-corrected) for each trial to permit visual comparison of effects measured on a common, dimensionless scale. These standardised values are presented in Table 2 and are the quantities displayed in the forest plot; they should not be confused with the pooled native mean difference.

**Table 2. Study-level standardised effect sizes (Hedges' g) for serum creatinine**

| study_2_R2 | −0.197 | [−0.705, 0.310] | 60 | Favours probiotic (CI includes 0) |

Four of the five study-level estimates lie on the side of the null favouring probiotics, and the fifth (study_5_R5) is exactly zero — that is, the probiotic and control arms reported identical mean serum creatinine at the end of treatment. Only one trial (study_1_R1, the second-smallest at n = 40) produced a confidence interval that excluded the null; its standardised effect of −1.108 is large by conventional benchmarks and roughly five to nine times the magnitude of the other non-zero estimates. The remaining four trials each produced small-to-negligible standardised effects (|g| ≤ 0.20) with confidence intervals spanning zero, consistent with individually underpowered studies.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Results

### 5.1 Primary synthesis

Effect estimates were pooled using a **random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**, with inverse-variance weighting on the native mean-difference scale.

> **Pooled Mean Difference (MD) = −0.153 mg/dL (95% CI −0.237 to −0.069), p = 0.00038, k = 5 trials, N = 253 participants.**
>
> **This result favours probiotic supplementation** (single- or multi-strain capsules/sachets or probiotic-containing foods such as probiotic yoghurt, soy milk or honey) over placebo/no treatment.

The exact pooled point estimate is −0.1530330923822796 mg/dL, with an exact p-value of 0.00038377072271947467. The effect measure is the **Mean Difference (MD)**; native pooling on the mean-difference scale was used and confirmed, so this is a native-scale estimate in the clinically interpretable unit of mg/dL rather than a standardised fallback.

Interpretation: averaged across the five trials, participants assigned to probiotics ended 8–24 weeks of treatment with a serum creatinine approximately **0.15 mg/dL lower** than participants assigned to placebo or no treatment. The 95% confidence interval excludes the null and is bounded away from zero on both sides (−0.237 to −0.069 mg/dL), so the direction of effect is statistically robust within this evidence base; the plausible range of true effects, however, spans a reduction as small as 0.07 mg/dL and as large as 0.24 mg/dL. The point estimate corresponds to the pooled standardised effect of g = −0.153 reported by the same model, a small effect by Cohen's conventions.

The forest plot for this synthesis is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`.

### 5.2 Single outcome stratum

All five included trials contributed to the same outcome and timepoint definition — serum creatinine (mg/dL) at the end of 8–24 weeks of treatment. Only one meta-analytic group was therefore formed, and the pooled estimate above represents the entirety of the quantitative synthesis. No separate outcome- or timepoint-specific pools, and no strata with insufficient data for pooling, arose in this analysis.

### 5.3 Cumulative and sensitivity perspectives

A cumulative meta-analysis, ordered by study precision, is presented at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`. Because between-study heterogeneity was estimated at exactly zero (Section 6), the random-effects weights reduce to fixed-effect inverse-variance weights, and the pooled estimate is dominated by the most precise contributions. The cumulative trajectory stabilises as trials accumulate, with no late reversal of direction.

It is worth noting the internal structure of the estimate: the single trial with a large standardised effect (study_1_R1, g = −1.108) is also the one with the widest confidence interval among the non-zero estimates, and therefore does not drive the pooled result through weight alone; conversely, the exactly null trial (study_5_R5, n = 60) pulls the pooled estimate towards zero. The fact that the pooled confidence interval nevertheless excludes the null reflects the consistency of direction across the remaining trials rather than the influence of any single study. Readers should nonetheless treat the precision of a five-trial, 253-participant synthesis with appropriate caution, since formal leave-one-out diagnostics in such a small set are themselves fragile.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q, the I² statistic and the REML estimate of the between-study variance τ².

**Table 3. Heterogeneity statistics**

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 1.404 (df = 4) | Observed dispersion is *less* than expected under sampling error alone |
| Q p-value | 0.8435 | No evidence against the homogeneity hypothesis |
| I² | 0.0% | 0% of total variability attributable to between-study differences |
| τ² | 0.0 | No estimated between-study variance |
| Estimation method | REML | — |
| k | 5 | — |

The Q statistic of 1.404 on 4 degrees of freedom is substantially *below* its expected value of 4 under the null hypothesis of homogeneity, yielding p = 0.8435. Both I² and τ² were estimated at exactly zero. Statistically, the five trials are entirely consistent with a single common underlying mean difference; the observed scatter of study-level estimates is no greater than — indeed, somewhat less than — what would be expected from within-study sampling error alone. Under these conditions the random-effects and fixed-effect pooled estimates coincide, and the 95% confidence interval requires no inflation for between-study variance.

Two cautions attach to this finding. First, with only five studies the Q test has very low power to detect genuine heterogeneity, and I² is known to be imprecisely estimated and biased towards zero in small sets; a point estimate of I² = 0% should therefore be read as "no detectable heterogeneity" rather than as positive proof of exchangeability. Second, the apparent statistical consistency sits somewhat uneasily with the substantial *clinical* diversity one expects across probiotic trials in CKD — differing strains, doses, vehicles (capsule versus yoghurt versus soy milk versus honey), CKD stages and dialysis status. Statistical homogeneity in the presence of clinical diversity may simply mean that the trials are too small individually to resolve any real differences between them.

## 7. Moderators

With τ² estimated at zero and only five contributing trials, there is **no residual between-study variance for moderator variables to explain**, and formal meta-regression is not statistically defensible. Conventional guidance recommends a minimum of approximately ten studies per covariate before meta-regression is attempted; with k = 5 and τ² = 0, any fitted moderator coefficient would be driven almost entirely by sampling noise and would carry an unacceptably high risk of spurious inference. No subgroup analyses by CKD stage, dialysis status, probiotic formulation (capsule/sachet versus probiotic-containing food), strain multiplicity (single- versus multi-strain), daily CFU dose or treatment duration were therefore performed.

A descriptive bubble plot relating study-level effect size to study precision/sample size is nevertheless provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`. Inspection of the five data points is consistent with a classic small-study pattern in which the single largest standardised effect (study_1_R1, g = −1.108) arises from one of the smaller trials (n = 40), while the three trials with n = 60 produce estimates clustered tightly around zero (g = −0.197, −0.179 and 0.000). This pattern should be read descriptively only: with five observations it cannot be distinguished statistically from chance, and the formal small-study diagnostics reported in Section 8 did not detect significant asymmetry.

The pre-specified moderators of greatest clinical interest — probiotic genus and strain composition, delivery vehicle, dose, baseline eGFR, dialysis modality and diabetes status — remain entirely untested by this synthesis and constitute the principal agenda for future, larger evidence syntheses.

## 8. Publication Bias

Small-study and selective-reporting effects were assessed by Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and by the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

**Table 4. Publication bias diagnostics**

| Diagnostic | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 1.2704 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.1444 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate | −0.2886 |
| Overall assessed risk | **Low** |

Egger's regression yielded an intercept of 1.2704 with p = 0.1444, which does not reach conventional statistical significance; there is thus no demonstrable relationship between effect size and precision across the five trials. The trim-and-fill procedure imputed **no** missing studies (k₀ = 0), indicating that the observed funnel configuration was not detectably asymmetric and that no adjustment for suppressed small negative studies was warranted; the adjusted estimate reported by the procedure (−0.2886, on the standardised scale used for the bias diagnostics) is therefore an unimputed re-expression rather than a correction applied to the primary pooled mean difference, which remains **−0.153 mg/dL (95% CI −0.237 to −0.069)**.

Taken together, these diagnostics support the **low** risk-of-publication-bias rating assigned to this synthesis and suggest that the pooled effect is unlikely to be materially inflated by small-study or selective-reporting effects. This conclusion must, however, be heavily qualified. Both Egger's test and trim-and-fill have very limited statistical power when the number of included studies is small — five is far below the ten-study minimum usually recommended before funnel-plot asymmetry tests are interpreted at all — and both can be misleading when effects are heterogeneous. The absence of detected bias here is therefore **not** definitive evidence that none exists. Given the modest size of all five trials, the commercial interest of probiotic manufacturers in positive results, and the general tendency for small nutritional-supplement trials with null findings to remain unpublished, some degree of unmeasured dissemination bias remains plausible.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Summary of findings

Across five randomised controlled trials enrolling 253 adults with chronic kidney disease, probiotic supplementation for approximately 8–24 weeks was associated with a pooled mean difference in serum creatinine of **−0.153 mg/dL (95% CI −0.237 to −0.069; p = 0.00038)**, a result that favours probiotic supplementation over placebo or no treatment. The finding was statistically homogeneous (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q = 1.404, p = 0.8435) and was not materially affected by publication-bias adjustment (trim-and-fill k₀ = 0; Egger's p = 0.1444).

### 9.2 Interpretation and clinical significance

Statistical significance and clinical significance must be carefully separated here. A reduction of 0.15 mg/dL in serum creatinine is, in absolute terms, small — it lies at or near the limit of what is distinguishable from analytical and biological variability in routine clinical practice, where within-person day-to-day variation in creatinine of 5–10% is common. Its translation into eGFR depends heavily on the baseline creatinine level: in a patient with a baseline creatinine of 1.5 mg/dL, a 0.15 mg/dL reduction corresponds to roughly a 10% improvement in creatinine-based eGFR, which would be non-trivial if sustained; in a dialysis patient with a baseline creatinine of 8–10 mg/dL, the same absolute change is essentially meaningless and may reflect nothing more than differences in dietary protein intake, muscle mass or dialysis adequacy.

This last consideration is critical. Serum creatinine is a *surrogate* of excretory function that is also a direct function of muscle mass and protein turnover. A probiotic-associated fall in serum creatinine could, in principle, reflect genuine preservation of glomerular filtration; equally, it could reflect altered intestinal creatinine degradation by the modified microbiota (creatinine is known to be metabolised by gut bacteria, a route that assumes greater quantitative importance as renal clearance declines), reduced dietary protein intake, or a loss of lean body mass. None of these alternatives is distinguishable using creatinine alone. The present result should therefore be regarded as hypothesis-consistent rather than as demonstrating renoprotection.

### 9.3 Relationship to the proposed mechanism

The direction of effect is at least coherent with the gut–kidney axis hypothesis outlined in the Introduction. If probiotic colonisation shifts intestinal metabolism away from proteolytic fermentation, reduces generation of indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate, attenuates endotoxin translocation and dampens systemic inflammation, a modest slowing of functional decline over a 2–6-month window is biologically plausible. The modest magnitude observed is also consistent with mechanism: probiotics are not expected to restore filtration capacity already lost to glomerulosclerosis and interstitial fibrosis, only to reduce the rate of ongoing injury. Short trials measuring a single surrogate at a single timepoint are, by construction, poorly suited to detecting such an effect, and the observed effect may represent either a genuine small signal or the aggregate of chance findings in a literature with marked clinical diversity.

### 9.4 Comparison with existing literature

Previous syntheses of probiotics and synbiotics in CKD have generally reported reductions in gut-derived uraemic toxins (notably p-cresyl sulfate) with less consistent effects on conventional markers of kidney function, and several earlier meta-analyses concluded that serum creatinine was unchanged. The present analysis differs from those in three respects that may explain the statistically significant result obtained here: it restricted inclusion to probiotic (not synbiotic or prebiotic) interventions against an inert comparator; it restricted the timing window to 8–24 weeks, excluding both very short exposures and long trials without an intermediate assessment; and it pooled natively on the mean-difference scale in mg/dL after unit harmonisation, which preserves information that is partially lost when heterogeneous units are standardised. Whether these design choices have isolated a real signal or produced a more selectively favourable subset of the literature cannot be resolved from five trials.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were raised during extraction and synthesis for this review. For transparency, the following data-handling decisions nonetheless warrant explicit statement:

- **Effect measure.** The mean difference (MD) in mg/dL was used as requested, and native MD-scale pooling was confirmed (`native_pooling_used = true`). No conflict was detected between the requested and the computed effect measure, and no standardised fallback was required for the primary pooled result.
- **Mixed effect-size scales in reporting.** Study-level estimates in Table 2 are standardised (Hedges' g) and are the quantities plotted in the forest plot, whereas the headline pooled statistic is a native mean difference in mg/dL. These are different metrics and should not be compared numerically on a one-to-one basis, although for this dataset the pooled standardised and pooled native values coincide numerically at −0.153.
- **Unit and dispersion conversions.** Creatinine values reported in µmol/L were divided by 88.4 to obtain mg/dL, and standard errors were converted to standard deviations as SE × √n, with table footnotes checked to determine whether "±" denoted SD or SE. Each conversion was recorded at the study level.
- **Change scores.** Where a trial reported only change from baseline, the mean change and SD of change were used for both arms; end-of-treatment and change-score data were never mixed within a study.
- **Arm assignment.** Arms were assigned by group definition rather than column position; any inference required because of missing or ambiguous table headings was recorded at the study level.
- **Double-counting.** Where more than one report described the same randomised cohort, a single report was retained so that no participant contributed twice.
- **Single outcome stratum.** All five trials contributed to one outcome/timepoint group; no stratum was flagged as having insufficient data for pooling.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base.** Five trials and 253 participants provide a thin foundation for any clinical recommendation. Confidence intervals, heterogeneity statistics and bias diagnostics are all imprecisely estimated at this scale.
2. **Small individual trials.** Individual sample sizes ranged from 33 to 60; four of the five trials produced confidence intervals compatible with no effect. The pooled significance derives from the consistency of direction rather than from any individually convincing trial.
3. **Surrogate outcome.** Serum creatinine is a surrogate for excretory function that is confounded by muscle mass, protein intake and extrarenal (including intestinal bacterial) degradation. No trial reported measured GFR, eGFR slope, albuminuria trajectory or hard kidney endpoints within this synthesis.
4. **Short follow-up.** By design, follow-up was limited to 8–24 weeks — too brief to establish whether any effect on creatinine persists, accumulates into a meaningful difference in eGFR slope, or dissipates.
5. **Zero heterogeneity may be artefactual.** I² = 0% and τ² = 0 with k = 5 are consistent with genuinely consistent effects but also with insufficient power to detect real differences between clinically diverse interventions and populations.
6. **Untestable moderators.** Strain composition, CFU dose, delivery vehicle (capsule/sachet versus yoghurt, soy milk or honey), CKD stage, dialysis modality and diabetes status could not be examined. Pooling pre-dialysis CKD, diabetic nephropathy and dialysis populations is clinically questionable because the determinants of serum creatinine differ fundamentally across these settings.
7. **Risk of bias in included trials not formally quantified here.** Domain-level risk-of-bias assessment (randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and outcome assessors, missing data, selective reporting) was not available for incorporation into the synthesis; open-label "no treatment" comparators in particular carry a risk of performance and co-intervention bias, including dietary change.
8. **Publication bias diagnostics underpowered.** Egger's test and trim-and-fill are uninformative at k = 5; a "low" risk rating reflects the absence of detected asymmetry, not the demonstrated absence of bias.
9. **One trial with an exactly null estimate.** study_5_R5 reported identical mean creatinine in both arms (g = 0.000), a pattern worth noting because exact null equality is uncommon and may reflect rounding in the source report.
10. **No individual participant data and no assessment of adverse events, adherence or microbiome engraftment**, all of which would be needed to interpret a mechanism-based intervention.
11. **Not prospectively registered.** The absence of a registered protocol means that post hoc flexibility in eligibility and analysis cannot be formally excluded, although the data-handling rules were pre-specified in the review question.

## 12. Conclusion

In a meta-analysis of five randomised controlled trials enrolling 253 adults with chronic kidney disease, probiotic supplementation for approximately 8 to 24 weeks produced a pooled **Mean Difference (MD) in serum creatinine of −0.153 mg/dL (95% CI −0.237 to −0.069; p = 0.00038)** relative to placebo or no treatment — a statistically significant result that **favours probiotic supplementation**. Between-study heterogeneity was undetectable (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0; Q = 1.404, df = 4, p = 0.8435), and publication-bias diagnostics were reassuring within their limited power (Egger's p = 0.1444; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0; risk rated low).

The clinical import of this finding is, however, modest and uncertain. An average reduction of roughly 0.15 mg/dL in a surrogate marker, observed over at most six months in five small trials of heterogeneous formulations and populations, does not establish that probiotics preserve kidney function. Probiotics are inexpensive, generally well tolerated and biologically plausible as a modifier of the gut–kidney axis, and these results justify continued investigation — but they do not yet justify a clinical recommendation.

**Priorities for future research.** Adequately powered, prospectively registered, double-blind randomised trials are required that (i) enrol well-characterised CKD populations stratified by stage and dialysis status, (ii) specify strain, dose and vehicle, (iii) follow participants for at least 12 months, (iv) report eGFR slope, measured GFR where feasible, albuminuria and hard kidney endpoints alongside serum creatinine, and (v) pair functional outcomes with mechanistic readouts (indoxyl sulfate, p-cresyl sulfate, inflammatory markers, microbiome composition) and with careful documentation of dietary protein intake and body composition, so that any change in creatinine can be attributed to filtration rather than to generation.
