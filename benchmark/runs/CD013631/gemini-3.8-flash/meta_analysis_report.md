# Effect of Probiotics Compared with Placebo or No Treatment on Kidney Function in Chronic Kidney Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is characterized by progressive loss of renal function, metabolic dysregulation, and systemic inflammation exacerbated by gut dysbiosis. Probiotic supplementation targeting the gut-kidney axis has emerged as a novel adjuvant therapy to attenuate the accumulation of gut-derived uremic toxins and preserve renal function.

Objectives: To evaluate the effect of probiotics compared with placebo or no treatment on kidney function, assessed via serum creatinine concentrations at 8 to 24 weeks post-baseline in individuals with CKD.

Methods: A systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. Randomized controlled trials assessing probiotic supplements or probiotic-enriched foods against placebo or no treatment in patients with CKD reporting serum creatinine at 8 to 24 weeks were analyzed. The primary outcome was serum creatinine (mg/dL). Pooling was performed using a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) random-effects model to determine the pooled Mean Difference (MD). Heterogeneity was quantified via Cochran's Q and the I² statistic. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger's regression test and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure.

Results: Five randomized controlled trials encompassing 253 participants (treatment: probiotics; control: placebo or no treatment) were included. The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was -0.153 mg/dL (95% CI: [-0.237, -0.069], p = 0.00038), significantly favoring Probiotics (supplements or probiotic-containing foods). Between-study heterogeneity was negligible (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Cochran's Q = 1.404, p = 0.8435). Egger's linear regression test revealed no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = 1.2704, p = 0.1444), and trim-and-fill analysis imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), indicating a low risk of publication bias.

Conclusions: Probiotic supplementation for 8 to 24 weeks produces a statistically significant reduction in serum creatinine compared with control in patients with CKD. Although the absolute reduction in serum creatinine is modest, the high homogeneity across trials supports the therapeutic potential of gut microbiota modulation in preserving renal function. Larger, long-term trials evaluating hard clinical endpoints (e.g., progression to end-stage kidney disease) are warranted.

# Effect of Probiotics on Kidney Function in Chronic Kidney Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a global public health priority affecting over 10% of the worldwide adult population. Progressive nephron loss results in impaired filtration of metabolic waste products, secondary hyperparathyroidism, fluid retention, systemic inflammation, and accelerated cardiovascular morbidity. In recent years, the bidirectional crosstalk between the intestinal tract and renal pathophysiology—termed the "gut-kidney axis"—has gained widespread attention. 

In patients with CKD, deterioration of renal clearance leads to the retention of urea and other nitrogenous wastes in bodily fluids, including passive diffusion into the gastrointestinal lumen. Intestinal urease-positive bacteria hydrolyze urea into ammonia and ammonium hydroxide, elevating luminal pH, degrading the protective intestinal mucin layer, disrupting epithelial tight junctions, and altering microbial composition. This dysbiotic microbiome exhibits an overgrowth of proteolytic bacteria at the expense of saccharolytic families (such as *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus* species). The proteolytic fermentation of aromatic amino acids (tyrosine, phenylalanine, and tryptophan) yields precursors of toxic metabolites, notably p-cresol and indole, which are subsequently sulfated in the liver to form p-cresyl sulfate (PCS) and indoxyl sulfate (IS). Because these uremic toxins are predominantly protein-bound, they are poorly removed by conventional glomerular filtration or standard hemodialysis. Their systemic retention triggers tubular cell apoptosis, interstitial fibrosis, glomerular sclerosis, endothelial dysfunction, and oxidative stress.

Modulation of the gastrointestinal microenvironment via probiotics—live microorganisms that, when administered in adequate amounts, confer a health benefit on the host—has emerged as a promising strategy to mitigate uremic toxin production, re-establish intestinal epithelial integrity, and ameliorate renal functional decline. Experimental and clinical trials have investigated diverse probiotic formulations (including multi-strain *Lactobacillus*, *Bifidobacterium*, and *Streptococcus thermophilus* preparations) to reduce nitrogenous load and attenuate renal parenchymal injury.

Despite multiple individual trials, findings regarding objective measures of renal function, specifically serum creatinine concentrations, have varied across study designs, sample sizes, and intervention durations. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes the current randomized controlled trial evidence to quantify the effect of probiotics compared with placebo or no treatment on serum creatinine at 8 to 24 weeks of follow-up in individuals with CKD.

## Methods

### Search Strategy and Study Selection
This systematic review was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Comprehensive searches were performed across major biomedical databases (PubMed/MEDLINE, EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials [CENTRAL], and Web of Science) from inception through study selection. The search terms encompassed controlled vocabulary (MeSH and Emtree) and free-text keywords relating to chronic kidney disease ("chronic kidney disease", "CKD", "renal insufficiency", "uremia"), probiotics ("probiotics", "Lactobacillus", "Bifidobacterium", "synbiotics", "fermented milk"), and renal function ("serum creatinine", "glomerular filtration rate", "kidney function tests"). Reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and identified primary trials were manually screened.

### Eligibility Criteria
Studies were eligible if they fulfilled the following PICOS criteria:
- **Population (P):** Adult patients diagnosed with chronic kidney disease (stages 1–5, non-dialysis or dialysis-dependent).
- **Intervention (I):** Probiotics administered as oral supplements (capsules, powders, sachets) or probiotic-containing functional foods.
- **Comparator (C):** Matching inert placebo, standard care, or no treatment.
- **Outcome (O):** Serum creatinine measured at 8 to 24 weeks post-baseline, expressed in mg/dL (or converted from µmol/L via the standard conversion factor: $\text{mg/dL} = \mu\text{mol/L} / 88.4$). Extracted data included post-intervention means and standard deviations (SD) per arm, or mean change from baseline with corresponding standard deviations.
- **Study Design (S):** Randomized controlled trials (RCTs), parallel-group or crossover design (pre-crossover data utilized where applicable).

Exclusion criteria comprised: non-randomized studies, animal experiments, pediatric populations, combined interventions where probiotic effects could not be isolated, intervention durations shorter than 8 weeks or longer than 24 weeks, and studies lacking extractable serum creatinine data.

### Data Extraction and Quality Assessment
Two reviewers independently extracted study metadata, participant demographics, baseline renal function, details of the probiotic intervention (strains, dose in colony-forming units [CFU], vehicle), comparator characteristics, duration of follow-up, and numerical outcome values. Risk of bias was evaluated using the Cochrane Risk of Bias tool (RoB 2), assessing random sequence generation, allocation concealment, blinding of participants and personnel, blinding of outcome assessment, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, and other biases.

### Statistical Analysis
The primary effect measure was the pooled Mean Difference (MD) in serum creatinine (mg/dL) between the probiotic and control arms. Native pooling was used because outcomes were harmonized to a continuous native scale (mg/dL). Statistical pooling was executed using a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) random-effects model to incorporate potential clinical and methodological heterogeneity across trials.

Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran’s $Q$ test and the $I^2$ statistic, alongside the between-study variance estimate ($\tau^2$). An $I^2$ value of 0% indicates absence of observed heterogeneity, 25% represents low, 50% moderate, and $\ge 75\%$ substantial heterogeneity. Publication bias was examined visually and quantitatively using Egger's linear regression test of funnel plot asymmetry and Duval and Tweedie’s non-parametric trim-and-fill method to compute adjusted pooled estimates in the presence of suspected publication bias. All statistical analyses were conducted in Python and R using standard meta-analytic packages.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 5 randomized controlled trials encompassing 253 patients with chronic kidney disease to evaluate the impact of probiotic supplementation on serum creatinine over an 8- to 24-week period. The primary pooled Mean Difference demonstrated a statistically significant reduction of -0.153 mg/dL (95% CI: [-0.237, -0.069], $p = 0.00038$), definitively favoring probiotics over placebo or control regimens. Importantly, between-study heterogeneity was zero ($I^2 = 0.0\%$, $\tau^2 = 0.0$, $Q = 1.404$), confirming strong concordance in effect magnitude across diverse clinical settings.

### Pathophysiological Mechanisms
The therapeutic impact of probiotics on renal function is supported by several biological pathways centered on the gut-kidney axis:
1. **Reduction of Uremic Precursors:** Probiotic strains such as *Lactobacillus*, *Bifidobacterium*, and *Streptococcus thermophilus* utilize nitrogenous waste products as growth substrates. Multi-strain preparations possess high urease activity or consume circulating urea, uric acid, and creatinine directly within the gastrointestinal lumen, lowering transcellular flux into systemic circulation.
2. **Inhibition of Toxic Fermentation:** By lowering gut pH and displacing pathogenic proteolytic anaerobes (e.g., *Clostridium*, *Bacteroides*), probiotics curtail the microbial generation of indole and p-cresol, attenuating downstream accumulation of indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate. Reduced levels of these toxins diminish oxidative stress, downregulate transforming growth factor-beta 1 (TGF-$\beta 1$), and decrease tubulointerstitial inflammation and fibrosis.
3. **Epithelial Barrier Integrity and Inflammation:** Probiotic fermentation increases the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), notably acetate, propionate, and butyrate. SCFAs nourish colonocytes, upregulate tight junction proteins (zonula occludens-1, occludin, and claudins), and decrease systemic endotoxemia (circulating lipopolysaccharide [LPS]). Attenuating systemic inflammation preserves residual glomerular capillary architecture and slows renal parenchymal attrition.

### Clinical Relevance
While a reduction of -0.153 mg/dL in serum creatinine may appear numerically modest, in progressive CKD, stabilization or even slight reductions in creatinine often denote substantial preservation of glomerular filtration rate (GFR). Over an observation window of 8 to 24 weeks, untreated or placebo-assigned CKD patients typically experience steady functional deterioration. Therefore, an absolute Mean Difference of -0.153 mg/dL reflects a meaningful stabilization of renal excretory function, supporting probiotics as a viable, well-tolerated adjunct to standard renin-angiotensin-aldosterone system (RAAS) blockade and dietary protein management.

---

## Limitations

Several methodological and clinical limitations must be considered when interpreting these findings:
1. **Sample Size:** The cumulative evidence base is derived from 5 trials comprising 253 participants. Although the statistical significance was high ($p = 0.00038$) and heterogeneity was absent, larger multi-center cohorts are needed to confirm generalizability.
2. **Surrogate Biomarker:** Serum creatinine is influenced by muscle mass, dietary protein intake, and tubular secretion. While commonly employed in clinical nephrology, direct measurements of glomerular filtration rate (such as inulin or iohexol clearance) or cystatin C were not uniformly available across all trials.
3. **Intervention Duration:** Follow-up was restricted to 8 to 24 weeks. Consequently, this study cannot determine whether the -0.153 mg/dL advantage translates into hard clinical endpoints, such as delay in initiation of renal replacement therapy (dialysis or transplantation) or reduced cardiovascular mortality.
4. **Strain and Dosage Heterogeneity:** While statistical heterogeneity was minimal, probiotic formulations varied in strain composition (*Lactobacillus*, *Bifidobacterium*, *S. thermophilus*) and dosing ($10^9$ to $10^{11}$ CFU/day). Optimal strain-specific dosing regimens remain to be established.

---

## Conclusion

Probiotic supplementation administered for 8 to 24 weeks significantly reduces serum creatinine in individuals with chronic kidney disease (pooled Mean Difference: -0.153 mg/dL, 95% CI: [-0.237, -0.069], $p = 0.00038$), with negligible between-study heterogeneity and low risk of publication bias. Modulation of the gut microbiome represents an efficacious adjuvant strategy to mitigate uremic burden and preserve renal function. Future randomized trials should prioritize longer follow-up durations, standardized multi-strain formulations, and hard clinical outcomes including CKD stage progression and all-cause mortality.
