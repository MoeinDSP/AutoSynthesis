# Zinc Treatment and the Duration of the Common Cold: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Mean Duration in Days

## Abstract

**Background.** Zinc has been proposed as a therapeutic agent for naturally acquired common colds on the basis of its putative inhibition of rhinoviral replication and of ICAM-1-mediated viral attachment. Trials of oral lozenges, tablets/syrup and intranasal gels/sprays have produced conflicting results, and pooled estimates have been sensitive to the choice of outcome and effect scale. This review addresses a single, narrowly defined question: in people with an active, naturally acquired common cold, does zinc treatment compared with placebo reduce the **mean duration of the cold in days**?

**Methods.** We systematically identified randomised, placebo-controlled trials of zinc given therapeutically for an active cold that reported mean cold duration in days (group means with SDs, a reported mean difference with its variance, or convertible t/F/confidence-interval data). Outcomes excluded by protocol were the proportion with ongoing symptoms at a fixed endpoint, symptom severity scores, duration of individual symptoms, adverse events, and prevention/incidence outcomes. Eight trial reports entered data extraction; effect sizes were computable for six. The pre-specified effect measure was the **mean difference (MD) in days**, pooled with a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Native mean-difference pooling was possible for **k = 4 trials (n = 412 participants)**; two further trials (study_3, study_6) could not contribute to native MD pooling because the raw inputs required for the day-scale computation were unavailable. The pooled **mean difference was −3.86 days (95% CI −5.905 to −1.814; p = 0.000217)**, a result that **favours zinc treatment** — i.e. colds resolved on average approximately 3.9 days sooner in zinc-treated participants than in placebo-treated participants. Heterogeneity was extreme: Q = 103.403 (df = 3), p < 0.001, I² = 96.39%, τ² = 4.130 days² (τ ≈ 2.03 days). Direction of effect was not uniform across the wider evidence base: five of six computable trials pointed towards shorter colds with zinc, whereas one (study_3) pointed towards longer colds with zinc. Egger's regression showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = 0.5654, p = 0.3864) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), leaving the estimate unchanged; overall risk of publication bias was graded **low**, with the caveat that these tests are underpowered at k < 10.

**Conclusions.** Pooled evidence from four placebo-controlled trials indicates a statistically significant reduction in the mean duration of naturally acquired common colds with zinc treatment, of the order of **3.9 days**. However, the certainty of this estimate is substantially limited by near-maximal statistical heterogeneity (I² = 96.4%), by a very small number of poolable trials, by the exclusion of two trials from native-scale pooling, and by the resulting instability of the pooled point estimate. The magnitude of benefit should be regarded as highly uncertain even though its existence is statistically supported; a difference approaching four days is larger than the effect typically reported in the clinical literature and may reflect clinical and methodological diversity (zinc salt, dose, formulation, route and duration definition) rather than a single underlying treatment effect.


## 1. Introduction

The common cold is the most frequent acute illness in human populations, with adults typically experiencing two to four episodes per year and children considerably more. Although self-limiting, the aggregate burden is substantial: colds are a leading cause of absenteeism from work and school, of unnecessary antibiotic prescribing, and of over-the-counter medication expenditure. No treatment has been established that reliably shortens the natural history of the illness, and symptomatic therapies (decongestants, antihistamines, analgesics) do not alter duration.

Zinc has been investigated as a disease-modifying therapy for colds for more than four decades. Two mechanisms are commonly cited: direct inhibition of rhinoviral 3C protease activity and capsid assembly by free Zn²⁺ ions in the nasopharyngeal mucosa, and blockade of ICAM-1, the receptor used by the majority of rhinovirus serotypes for cell entry. Both mechanisms imply that zinc must be delivered to the upper respiratory mucosa in ionisable form and begun early in the illness — which is why formulation (lozenge versus tablet/syrup versus intranasal gel or spray), the zinc salt used (acetate, gluconate, sulphate), the presence of chelating excipients (citric acid, mannitol, sorbitol), and the total daily elemental zinc dose are all plausible determinants of efficacy.

The trial literature is heterogeneous and the published syntheses have not converged. Part of the difficulty is that "duration" has been operationalised inconsistently: as time from randomisation to complete symptom resolution, as time from symptom onset to resolution, as the proportion of participants still symptomatic at a fixed day, or as the duration of individual symptoms such as cough or nasal discharge. Pooling across these definitions produces estimates that are difficult to interpret clinically. The present review therefore restricts itself deliberately to one outcome construct — **the mean duration of the cold in days** — and to one effect measure — **the mean difference in days** — so that the pooled result can be read directly on the clinical scale on which the question is asked.

**Review question.** In people with a naturally acquired common cold, does zinc treatment (oral lozenges, tablets/syrup, or intranasal preparations) compared with placebo reduce the mean duration of the cold in days?

**Direction convention.** Throughout this report, the raw statistical sign convention is treatment minus control, so a *negative* mean difference in days denotes a *shorter* cold in the zinc arm. To avoid ambiguity we additionally state, for every pooled result, which arm the result favours, using the direction field carried by the analysis itself rather than inferring it from the sign.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Information sources and eligibility

Records were assembled from a structured search of the biomedical trial literature for randomised, placebo-controlled trials of zinc administered **therapeutically** (i.e. started after the onset of a naturally acquired cold, not prophylactically) in which cold duration in days was reported as a group-level outcome.

**Inclusion criteria**

- **Population:** people of any age with a naturally acquired (not experimentally inoculated) common cold, treatment commenced during the active illness.
- **Intervention:** any zinc preparation intended to shorten the illness — oral lozenges, oral tablets or syrup, or intranasal gel/spray — of any zinc salt and dose.
- **Comparator:** placebo (matched lozenge, tablet, syrup, gel or spray).
- **Outcome:** mean duration of the common cold in days, measured from the start of treatment or from symptom onset (as defined by each trial) until resolution of the cold / complete symptom clearance.
- **Design:** randomised controlled trial with a parallel or equivalent placebo comparison.
- **Data:** group means with standard deviations, or a reported mean difference with its variance, or t/F/confidence-interval statistics convertible to these quantities.

**Exclusion criteria (outcome-level)**

By protocol the following outcomes were *not* eligible and could not be used as a substitute for mean duration in days: the proportion of participants with ongoing symptoms at a fixed endpoint; symptom severity or symptom-score outcomes; the duration of individual symptoms (e.g. cough, rhinorrhoea, sore throat) considered separately; adverse events and tolerability; and prevention/incidence outcomes (episodes averted, incidence rate ratios).

**Exclusion criteria (record-level)**

Narrative and systematic reviews, editorials, and secondary reports of a trial already represented in the dataset were excluded; where multiple publications described the same randomised cohort, a single report was retained to avoid double-counting participants.

### 2.2 Screening outcome

Screening proceeded from record identification through title/abstract triage to full-text assessment against the criteria above. **Eight trial reports** satisfied all eligibility criteria and proceeded to data extraction. Of these, quantitative effect sizes could be computed for **six** reports; two reports were eligible on substantive grounds but did not supply, and did not permit reconstruction of, the statistics needed for any effect-size computation, and therefore contribute to the narrative synthesis only.

Of the six computable trials, **four** supplied the raw inputs required for pooling on the native mean-difference (day) scale and constitute the primary meta-analytic set (k = 4, n = 412 participants). The remaining two computable trials (labelled **study_3** and **study_6**) could be represented as standardised effect sizes but lacked the raw inputs necessary for native MD pooling and were therefore excluded from the primary quantitative synthesis. This is a material limitation and is reported explicitly in §9 (Data Quality & Validation Notes).

A PRISMA-style accounting of the flow is therefore: 8 reports eligible and extracted → 6 with computable effect sizes → 4 contributing to the native mean-difference meta-analysis.

## 3. Included Studies

Eight trials of therapeutic zinc for naturally acquired colds met the eligibility criteria. The six with computable effect sizes ranged in size from 48 to 213 participants, with a median of approximately 76 and a combined enrolment across the six of 563 participants; the four trials contributing to the primary pooled analysis together enrolled **412 participants**.

The trials are clinically and methodologically diverse in ways that matter for the outcome under study:

- **Route and formulation.** The eligible interventions span oral zinc lozenges, oral zinc tablets/syrup and intranasal zinc gel/spray. These are not pharmacologically interchangeable: lozenges and intranasal preparations deliver ionic zinc directly to the upper respiratory mucosa, whereas swallowed tablets or syrup act systemically, and the two routes are therefore expected to differ in the magnitude of any antiviral effect.
- **Zinc salt and excipients.** Acetate, gluconate and sulphate salts differ in the proportion of zinc released as free Zn²⁺ at nasopharyngeal pH, and formulations containing chelating sweeteners or flavourings can render a nominally adequate dose biologically inert.
- **Dose and schedule.** Total daily elemental zinc and dosing frequency vary substantially across trials.
- **Population.** Both adult and paediatric populations are represented, with corresponding differences in natural cold duration and in the reliability of symptom reporting (self-report versus parental proxy report).
- **Definition of duration.** Some trials measured duration from randomisation (start of treatment), others from symptom onset, and the operational definition of "resolution" (complete absence of all symptoms versus return to baseline) differed. This definitional heterogeneity is a direct contributor to the between-study variance reported in §6.
- **Time from symptom onset to first dose.** Because the proposed mechanism is antiviral, delay in initiating treatment is a plausible effect modifier, and trials differ in how strictly early initiation was enforced.

Risk-of-bias considerations relevant to this outcome deserve emphasis. Mean duration of a self-limiting, subjectively assessed illness is an outcome highly susceptible to **unblinding**. Zinc lozenges — particularly zinc acetate and zinc gluconate at therapeutic doses — have a distinctive astringent, metallic taste that is difficult to mask; intranasal zinc can cause burning and transient anosmia. Where blinding fails, differential expectation effects can inflate the apparent treatment benefit on a patient-reported endpoint. Additional domains of concern across this literature include attrition (participants who stop taking an unpalatable lozenge may be differentially lost), the handling of participants whose colds had not resolved by the end of follow-up (censoring treated as resolution would bias duration estimates), and selective reporting of the duration definition most favourable to the intervention.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure and computation scale

The pre-specified and analysed effect measure is the **mean difference (MD) in days** — the metric in which the review question is posed. The effect measure was set by explicit specification of the review question (`effect_measure_source: explicit_user_request`), and **native-scale pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used: true`): the pooled estimate reported in §5 is a genuine mean difference in days, not a standardised quantity back-transformed onto a day scale. The measure label for the primary analysis is therefore **"Mean Difference (MD)"**, and it should be named as such; it is not a Hedges' *g*.

For descriptive and diagnostic purposes, each computable trial was additionally expressed as a standardised mean difference (Hedges' *g*), which permits visual comparison of trials on a common dimensionless scale and underpins the small-study-effect diagnostics in §8. **These standardised values are on a different scale from the pooled mean difference and must not be compared with it numerically.** A *g* of −2.08 and a pooled MD of −3.86 days are not contradictory or redundant quantities; they are different statistics.

Several features of this table are analytically important.

First, **five of six trials point in the same direction** (shorter colds with zinc) and in each of those five the 95% confidence interval excludes zero. The single discordant trial, **study_3**, favours placebo with *g* = +0.466 (95% CI 0.001 to 0.931); its lower bound sits essentially at the null, so while nominally "significant" this trial is better read as weakly discordant than as strong evidence of harm.

Second, the **magnitude** of the concordant effects varies enormously, from *g* = −0.499 to *g* = −3.573 — a seven-fold range on a standardised scale. Standardised effects of −2.0 to −3.6 are extraordinarily large by the conventions of clinical trial research, where *g* ≈ 0.8 is already described as a large effect. Effects of this size on a patient-reported duration endpoint are more commonly produced by incomplete blinding, by very small samples with unusually low within-group variance, or by duration definitions that interact with the treatment, than by pharmacological action alone. The largest single effect (study_4_R4, *g* = −3.573) is also the largest trial (n = 213), so it cannot be dismissed as a small-sample artefact, but its precision means it dominates any pooled estimate.

Third, **study_3 and study_6 do not contribute to the primary pooled analysis.** Both are shown here because standardised effect sizes were computable for them, but neither supplied the raw group means, standard deviations or equivalent statistics needed for pooling on the native day scale. The primary synthesis therefore rests on **study_1, study_2, study_4 and study_8**. Notably, the one trial favouring placebo (study_3) is among those excluded from native pooling, which means the pooled MD in §5 is derived exclusively from trials favouring zinc. This is an artefact of data availability rather than of eligibility, and it is a genuine threat to the validity of the pooled point estimate.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A single outcome group was analysed; the review question specifies one outcome construct (mean cold duration in days) at one conceptual timepoint (resolution), so no separate outcome/timepoint subgroups were required and no analysis was declared insufficient for pooling on those grounds.

Random-effects pooling used **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of the between-study variance, with Wald-type 95% confidence intervals.

**Table 2. Primary random-effects meta-analysis — mean duration of the common cold (days)**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Mean Difference (MD)**, days |
| Native-scale pooling | Yes (confirmed native MD) |
| Studies pooled (k) | 4 |
| Participants (n) | 412 |
| **Pooled mean difference** | **−3.86 days** |
| 95% confidence interval | **−5.905 to −1.814 days** |
| p-value | **0.000217** |
| Favours | **Zinc treatment** |
| τ² | 4.130 days² (τ ≈ 2.03 days) |
| Q (df = 3) | 103.403 |
| p (Q) | < 0.001 |
| I² | 96.39% |
| Estimator | REML |

The pooled **mean difference was −3.86 days (95% CI −5.905 to −1.814; p = 0.000217)**. Applying the direction field carried by the analysis, this result **favours zinc treatment**: participants randomised to zinc recovered, on average, approximately 3.9 days sooner than participants randomised to placebo, with the confidence interval spanning a reduction of roughly 1.8 to 5.9 days. The confidence interval excludes zero by a comfortable margin and the p-value is well below conventional thresholds, so the *existence* of a duration-shortening effect is statistically supported within this set of four trials.

The *magnitude*, however, must be interpreted with great caution. A typical uncomplicated cold lasts approximately 7–10 days; a mean reduction of 3.9 days would imply that zinc roughly halves the illness. This is considerably larger than the one-to-two-day reduction most commonly cited in the broader zinc literature. The lower confidence bound (−1.814 days) is closer to that conventional figure than the point estimate is, and given the heterogeneity documented in §6 the point estimate should be regarded as an upper-leaning summary of a very dispersed set of trial results rather than as a precise estimate of a common effect.

The forest plot for this analysis is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. A cumulative meta-analysis, showing how the pooled estimate evolves as studies are added, is at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; with only four contributing trials this plot is best read as a sensitivity display — it shows how heavily the summary depends on individual trials — rather than as evidence about temporal trends in the literature.

**Narrative synthesis of non-pooled trials.** Two computable trials could not be pooled on the native scale. Of these, **study_6** (*g* = −0.880, 95% CI −1.345 to −0.415, n = 78) is directionally concordant with the pooled result but of substantially smaller standardised magnitude, while **study_3** (*g* = +0.466, 95% CI 0.001 to 0.931, n = 73) is directionally discordant, favouring placebo. Had these two trials been poolable on the day scale, the pooled mean difference would in all likelihood have been attenuated towards the null. Two further eligible trials yielded no computable effect size at all and contribute nothing beyond their existence to the quantitative picture. Taken together, the narrative evidence is therefore less uniformly favourable to zinc than the pooled estimate alone suggests.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **extreme**:

- **Cochran's Q = 103.403 on 3 degrees of freedom, p < 0.001.** With only four studies, Q exceeds its expected value under homogeneity (which is simply df = 3) by a factor of roughly 34. The null hypothesis of a common underlying effect is decisively rejected.
- **I² = 96.39%.** Approximately 96% of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to genuine between-study differences rather than to within-study sampling error. By the usual interpretive thresholds this is at the very top of the "considerable heterogeneity" band.
- **τ² = 4.130 days²**, i.e. an estimated between-study standard deviation of τ ≈ 2.03 days. The true treatment effect is therefore estimated to vary across trial settings by about ±2 days (1 SD) around the mean of −3.86 days.

The practical consequence of τ ≈ 2.03 days is that a 95% **prediction interval** for the effect in a new, exchangeable trial would be extremely wide. With k = 4 (and hence only 2 degrees of freedom for the t-multiplier), an approximate prediction interval spans roughly **−13.7 to +6.0 days** — that is, it includes both very large benefit and appreciable harm. This interval should not be over-interpreted (it is barely estimable at k = 4), but it conveys the essential point: the data are consistent with a wide range of true effects, including the possibility that in some settings zinc does not shorten colds at all. This is reinforced by study_3, which, although excluded from native pooling, estimated a *longer* illness with zinc.

Sources of heterogeneity are not mysterious in this literature. The plausible contributors are (i) route and formulation (mucosal lozenge/intranasal versus systemic tablet/syrup), (ii) zinc salt and the presence of chelating excipients that neutralise free Zn²⁺, (iii) total daily elemental zinc dose, (iv) age of the population, (v) delay between symptom onset and first dose, (vi) the operational definition of "duration" and of "resolution", and (vii) variation in blinding integrity given the distinctive taste of zinc preparations. Any one of these could generate differences of the observed magnitude.

Given I² = 96.39%, a fixed-effect model would be indefensible, and even the random-effects model should be understood as describing the *average* of a heterogeneous distribution of effects rather than estimating a single shared parameter. A graphical summary of the heterogeneity diagnostics is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 7. Moderators

No formal moderator analysis — meta-regression or subgroup analysis — was undertaken, and none would be defensible with the available data.

With **k = 4** trials contributing to the native mean-difference synthesis, there are insufficient degrees of freedom to estimate even a single covariate reliably; conventional guidance suggests roughly ten studies per covariate for meta-regression, and subgroup comparisons at k = 4 would be dominated by sampling noise and highly vulnerable to aggregation bias. The clinically obvious moderators — **route/formulation** (lozenge versus tablet/syrup versus intranasal), **zinc salt and dose**, **adult versus paediatric population**, **time from symptom onset to first dose**, and **definition of duration** — are precisely the variables most likely to explain the 96.4% of variance attributable to heterogeneity, but they cannot be tested here. Their non-estimation is a limitation of the evidence base, not a finding of no moderation.

The bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png` displays effect magnitude against study precision across the six computable trials and may be inspected descriptively. It should not be read as a fitted meta-regression: no moderator coefficient, slope test or R² analogue was estimated, and any apparent trend is observational. In particular, the co-occurrence of the largest effect with the largest sample (study_4_R4) means that precision-based patterns in this plot do not follow the classic small-study-effect signature.

## 8. Publication Bias

Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed using two complementary approaches, both conducted on the standardised (Hedges' *g*) scale.

- **Egger's regression test** produced an intercept of **0.5654** with **p = 0.3864**. There is thus no statistically significant evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry: the relationship between effect size and precision does not depart detectably from what would be expected in the absence of small-study effects.
- **Trim-and-fill** imputed **k₀ = 0** missing studies. Because no studies were trimmed or filled, the adjusted estimate is identical to the unadjusted one; the adjusted value on the standardised diagnostic scale was **g = −1.5275**, which is simply the unadjusted random-effects estimate on that scale (note that this standardised figure is a bias-diagnostic quantity and is *not* the primary result — the primary result is the native mean difference of −3.86 days reported in §5).

The overall risk of publication bias was accordingly graded **low**. As the accompanying interpretation states: Egger's test shows no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.3864), trim-and-fill imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0) so the pooled estimate is unchanged after adjustment, and together these converge on a low risk of publication bias, suggesting the meta-analytic effect is unlikely to be an artefact of selective reporting or small-study effects.

One important caveat must be attached to this grading. Both Egger's regression and trim-and-fill have **very limited statistical power when fewer than about ten studies are available**. With four trials in the primary synthesis (and six with computable effect sizes), these tests are close to uninformative: a null result should be read as *an absence of detectable asymmetry*, not as positive proof that no bias exists. Funnel-plot-based methods also conflate publication bias with other causes of small-study effects, and in a literature as heterogeneous as this one (I² = 96.39%) that conflation is particularly problematic. A further and arguably more consequential form of reporting bias in this dataset is not captured by these tests at all: **two eligible trials yielded no computable effect size, and two further trials could not be pooled on the native day scale because their raw inputs were unreported.** Incomplete outcome reporting of this kind can distort a pooled estimate in exactly the same way as outright non-publication, and here it removed from the primary analysis the one trial that favoured placebo.

## 9. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-handling issues arose during this review and are reported plainly because they materially affect how the pooled result should be read.

1. **Two trials were excluded from native mean-difference pooling for lacking the required raw inputs.** The analysis explicitly records: *"2 row(s) excluded from native MD pooling for lacking raw inputs: ['study_3', 'study_6']."* Both trials were eligible and both yielded computable standardised effect sizes, but neither supplied group means with standard deviations, a mean difference with its variance, or convertible statistics sufficient to place them on the native day scale. Consequently the primary synthesis rests on **4 of the 6 computable trials**, and the excluded pair includes **study_3, the only trial whose point estimate favoured placebo**. The pooled mean difference of −3.86 days is therefore computed from a set of trials that all favour zinc, and is likely to be less conservative than a synthesis of the full computable evidence base would have been.

2. **Attrition between extraction and analysis.** Eight trial reports were eligible and extracted; effect sizes were computable for six; four entered the pooled analysis. Two eligible trials thus contribute no quantitative information whatsoever, and a further two contribute only descriptively. Readers should note that the headline k = 4 reflects **data availability**, not the size of the eligible literature.

3. **Effect measure confirmed and native.** The effect measure for the primary analysis is the Mean Difference (MD) in days, specified by the review question and pooled natively on that scale (`native_pooling_used: true`). The result in §5 is a confirmed native-scale mean difference and should be named "pooled mean difference", not "Hedges' *g*". Where standardised values appear in this report (Table 1, §8), they are explicitly labelled as descriptive or diagnostic quantities on a different scale.

4. **Mixed scales within the report.** Because study-level effects are tabulated as Hedges' *g* while the pooled result is a mean difference in days, the study-level numbers cannot be compared arithmetically with the pooled number, and the forest plot should be read with attention to the scale on which each element is plotted.

5. **Record-level exclusions.** Reviews, editorials and duplicate/secondary reports of trials already represented were excluded at screening; where more than one publication described the same randomised cohort, a single report was retained so that no cohort contributes twice to the pooled estimate.

6. **Heterogeneity flag.** I² = 96.39% with τ² = 4.130 days² constitutes near-maximal statistical heterogeneity. The pooled estimate is reported because it was pre-specified, but it summarises a distribution of effects so dispersed that the notion of a single common effect is not supported by the data.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis of four placebo-controlled randomised trials (n = 412) found that zinc treatment for a naturally acquired common cold was associated with a **mean reduction in cold duration of 3.86 days (95% CI −5.905 to −1.814; p = 0.000217)**, a result favouring zinc. Five of the six trials for which any effect size could be computed pointed in the same direction.

**Is the effect real?** The statistical evidence for a non-null effect within this dataset is reasonably strong: the confidence interval excludes zero by a wide margin, the p-value is small, the direction is consistent across most trials, and small-study-effect diagnostics are null (Egger p = 0.3864; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0). The mechanistic rationale — mucosal delivery of ionic zinc inhibiting rhinoviral replication and attachment — is biologically coherent and route-dependent in a way that fits the clinical observation that lozenges and intranasal preparations are more plausible candidates than swallowed systemic formulations.

**Is the effect this large?** Almost certainly not. Three considerations argue for substantial downward revision of the point estimate. First, **heterogeneity is near-maximal** (I² = 96.39%): the pooled value is the average of a widely scattered set of estimates, and averages of scattered sets are poor guides to any individual setting. Second, **the trials excluded from native pooling are systematically less favourable** — study_6 is concordant but smaller in magnitude, and study_3 actually favours placebo — so the available pooled set is directionally selected by data availability. Third, **blinding integrity is a serious concern** for a patient-reported duration endpoint when the active preparation has a distinctive metallic taste or causes nasal burning; differential expectancy is a well-documented source of inflation in exactly this literature, and it is the most parsimonious explanation for standardised effects in the −2 to −3.6 range. A reduction of around four days — roughly halving a typical cold — exceeds what most published syntheses of this question report, and the lower confidence bound of 1.8 days sits closer to the conventionally reported one-to-two-day benefit.

**Clinical interpretation.** If a reduction of even one to two days in mean cold duration were real and reproducible, it would be clinically meaningful given the aggregate burden of colds, the absence of alternative disease-modifying treatments, and the low cost of zinc. The practical question for clinicians, however, is not whether "zinc works" in the abstract but *which* zinc, *at what dose*, *by what route*, and *started how early* — and these are precisely the questions this evidence base cannot answer. Zinc is also not free of harm: lozenges commonly cause nausea and taste disturbance, and intranasal zinc has been associated with anosmia, which is why intranasal products were withdrawn in several jurisdictions. Adverse events were outside the scope of this outcome-specific review, but they are material to any recommendation and should be considered alongside the duration estimate.

**Relation to the wider literature.** The broad pattern reported here — a statistically significant shortening of colds with zinc, accompanied by heterogeneity so large that the magnitude is unstable — is consistent with the long-standing state of this field. Previous syntheses have likewise found directionally positive but highly dispersed effects, with results sensitive to the inclusion or exclusion of individual trials, to formulation, and to the chosen duration definition. The present analysis adds a clean, outcome-restricted, native-scale estimate, but it does not resolve the underlying instability.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small number of poolable trials.** The primary analysis rests on **k = 4** trials and 412 participants. At this k, the between-study variance τ² is poorly estimated, the confidence interval is likely too narrow (Wald intervals with REML are known to be anti-conservative at small k), and the prediction interval is barely estimable.
2. **Incomplete use of the eligible evidence.** Eight trials were eligible, six yielded computable effect sizes, and only four could be pooled natively. The two trials excluded from native pooling (study_3, study_6) include the only trial favouring placebo, so the pooled estimate is plausibly optimistic.
3. **Near-maximal heterogeneity.** I² = 96.39%, τ² = 4.130 days², Q = 103.403 (df = 3, p < 0.001). The assumption of a single, transportable treatment effect is not supported; the pooled value is an average over a heterogeneous effect distribution.
4. **No moderator analysis possible.** Route/formulation, zinc salt, dose, age group, time-to-first-dose and duration definition could not be tested as moderators at k = 4, leaving the dominant source of variance unexplained.
5. **Underpowered bias diagnostics.** Egger's test and trim-and-fill are near-uninformative below ten studies. The "low risk" grading means *no detectable asymmetry*, not *no bias*. Outcome-reporting bias — evident here in the four trials that could not be fully used — is not captured by these tests.
6. **Blinding and outcome ascertainment.** Mean cold duration is patient-reported and the active preparations are organoleptically distinctive. Imperfect blinding, differential attrition, and inconsistent handling of unresolved colds at end of follow-up could each inflate the apparent benefit.
7. **Definitional heterogeneity in the outcome itself.** Duration measured from randomisation and duration measured from symptom onset are not the same quantity, and "resolution" was not uniformly defined. Pooling across these definitions introduces variance that no statistical model can remove.
8. **Scale mixing in reporting.** Study-level effects are presented as standardised mean differences while the pooled result is a native mean difference in days; the two scales are not numerically comparable, and readers must keep them distinct.
9. **No formal certainty rating.** A GRADE assessment was not performed, but on the evidence above — imprecision from k = 4, serious inconsistency (I² = 96.4%), and risk of bias from blinding and incomplete reporting — the certainty of the *magnitude* of effect would be low at best, with somewhat greater confidence attaching to the *direction*.

---

## 12. Conclusion

Pooling four randomised placebo-controlled trials (n = 412) on the native day scale, zinc treatment for a naturally acquired common cold was associated with a **pooled mean difference of −3.86 days (95% CI −5.905 to −1.814; p = 0.000217)**, a result that **favours zinc treatment** — colds resolved on average approximately 3.9 days sooner with zinc than with placebo. Five of the six trials with computable effect sizes were directionally concordant, and formal tests gave no evidence of publication bias (Egger intercept = 0.5654, p = 0.3864; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0), although these tests are underpowered at this number of studies.

The direction of effect is therefore reasonably well supported; the magnitude is not. Heterogeneity was near-maximal (I² = 96.39%, τ² = 4.130 days², Q = 103.403, df = 3, p < 0.001), only four of eight eligible trials could be pooled, and the trials excluded from native pooling — including the single trial favouring placebo — would probably have attenuated the estimate. A reduction approaching four days should be read as the upper end of a wide and unstable range rather than as a dependable clinical expectation; the lower confidence bound of about 1.8 days is closer to the benefit reported elsewhere in this literature.

For practice, this analysis is compatible with a genuine but uncertain shortening of colds by zinc, most plausibly with mucosally delivered formulations started early, and it does not identify an optimal salt, dose or route. For research, the priority is clear: adequately powered, rigorously blinded trials using a pre-specified and uniform definition of cold duration, with complete reporting of group means and standard deviations so that future syntheses are not forced to discard half the eligible evidence for want of extractable numbers.
