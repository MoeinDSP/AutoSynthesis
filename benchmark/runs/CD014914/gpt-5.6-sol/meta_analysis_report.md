# Zinc Initiated After Symptom Onset for the Duration of Naturally Acquired Common Cold or Acute Upper Respiratory Tract Infection: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Zinc has been proposed as a treatment that may shorten naturally acquired common colds when started after symptoms begin.

### Objective
To determine whether zinc initiated after symptom onset, compared with matching placebo, reduces overall illness duration among adults and children in randomized placebo-controlled trials.

### Methods
The prespecified outcome was overall cold duration in days, measured from treatment initiation until complete symptom resolution or until the participant was considered well according to each trial’s primary definition. The requested effect was the unscaled mean difference (MD), calculated as zinc minus placebo; negative MDs therefore indicate shorter duration with zinc. The supplied dataset contained eight studies and five computed study-level effect sizes. Four trials had the raw inputs required for native-scale MD pooling and contributed 412 randomized participants. A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. One additional effect-size row lacked the raw data needed for MD pooling, and three of the eight studies had no usable effect-size row in the supplied results.

### Results
Across four trials (N = 412), the pooled Mean Difference (MD) was −3.859501706282759 days (95% CI −5.905 to −1.814; p = 0.00021749587062375202), favoring zinc initiated after symptom onset. Heterogeneity was considerable: Q = 103.403 with 3 degrees of freedom (reported p = 0.0), I² = 96.39%, and τ² = 4.130279 days². Egger’s intercept was 0.3344 (p = 0.4924), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀ = 0); however, publication-bias procedures were based on only four pooled trials and consequently have very low power.

### Conclusions
The available native-scale analysis indicates that zinc started after symptom onset shortened overall cold duration by an average of approximately 3.86 days compared with placebo. The estimate is statistically significant but should be interpreted cautiously because between-trial heterogeneity was extreme, only four trials entered the requested MD analysis, study-level means and standard deviations were not supplied for independent verification, and the available information was insufficient to assess clinical or methodological moderators.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects meta-analysis using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) combined the four native-scale trial estimates.

The **pooled Mean Difference (MD)** was **−3.859501706282759 days** (95% CI **−5.905 to −1.814**; **p = 0.00021749587062375202**; k = 4; N = 412). According to the supplied `favors` field, the result favors **zinc initiated after symptom onset**.

In clinical units, the model estimates that zinc reduced overall illness duration by an average of approximately **3.86 days** relative to matching placebo. The confidence interval corresponds to an average reduction ranging from **1.814 to 5.905 days**. This is an average random-effects estimate across the included trial effects and should not be interpreted as a uniform treatment benefit in every setting, particularly given the very high heterogeneity.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study inconsistency was extreme:

- Cochran’s Q = **103.403** with **3 degrees of freedom**
- Reported Q-test p value = **0.0**
- I² = **96.39%**
- τ² = **4.130279 days²**
- Estimator: **REML**

The reported Q-test p value of 0.0 reflects the numerical output supplied by the analysis pipeline rather than a literally zero probability. The Q statistic and I² indicate that nearly all observed variation beyond sampling error was attributed to between-study differences. The estimated between-study variance, τ² = 4.130279 days², corresponds to a between-study SD of approximately 2.0323 days.

This heterogeneity materially limits the interpretation of the pooled average. Plausible sources include zinc formulation and route, elemental zinc dose, zinc salt, dosing frequency, delay between symptom onset and treatment initiation, participant age, case definition, outcome definition, follow-up procedures, and trial conduct. None of these variables was available in analyzable form.

Egger’s test was not statistically significant, and trim-and-fill did not impute missing studies. These diagnostics therefore did not detect funnel-plot asymmetry or putatively missing effects. Nevertheless, absence of statistical evidence is not evidence that reporting bias is absent. With only four trials in the native MD synthesis, Egger’s test is severely underpowered, funnel-shape assessment is unstable, and trim-and-fill may fail to detect bias.

The supplied trim-and-fill value is labeled *g* and is not on the confirmed native MD-in-days scale. It should not be interpreted as an adjusted illness-duration difference or compared directly with the pooled MD of −3.859501706282759 days. No funnel-plot path was provided.

## Discussion

This random-effects synthesis suggests that zinc treatment initiated after common-cold symptoms began reduced overall illness duration relative to matching placebo. The pooled Mean Difference was −3.859501706282759 days, with a 95% CI excluding no difference and a p value of 0.00021749587062375202. Because the analysis used days rather than standardized units, the pooled estimate is readily interpretable.

The apparent magnitude of benefit is substantial, but its generalizability is uncertain. Heterogeneity was exceptionally high (I² = 96.39%; τ² = 4.130279 days²), indicating that the average effect does not adequately describe the dispersion of trial results. Differences in formulation, dose, administration route, timing, population, illness definition, placebo composition, and recovery criteria could all plausibly modify treatment effects. The supplied data did not permit these explanations to be investigated.

Only four studies contributed to the primary synthesis despite eight studies being represented in the pipeline. One additional study had a computed standardized effect but could not enter native MD pooling because the raw inputs were lacking; three further studies had no supplied effect-size row. Excluding these studies may reduce precision and could introduce availability bias if the presence of analyzable mean-and-SD data is related to study findings.

The nonsignificant Egger test and absence of trim-and-fill imputations are weakly reassuring but do not resolve concerns about dissemination or selective-outcome bias. Publication-bias tests are not reliable with so few trials. Likewise, without risk-of-bias judgments, the certainty of the evidence cannot be graded rigorously.

The findings apply specifically to treatment started after symptom onset and to overall duration as defined by each trial. They do not establish efficacy for prevention, individual symptom resolution, symptom-severity scores, biological markers, or median-only duration outcomes.

## Data Quality and Validation Notes

- The pipeline listed **8 studies**, but only **5 effect-size rows** were supplied.
- Only **4 studies** (N = 412) had sufficient raw information for confirmed native-scale MD pooling.
- `study_3_R3` was excluded from the MD synthesis because it lacked the raw inputs required to calculate an unscaled mean difference in days.
- Three pipeline studies had no effect-size row in the supplied context; their exclusion reasons were not reported.
- The five displayed study-level effects were supplied as Hedges’ g values, whereas the requested and pooled primary effect was the native **Mean Difference (MD)** in days. These measures were kept separate.
- The arm-level means and SDs were unavailable, preventing independent verification of individual MDs and any SE- or CI-to-SD conversion.
- The study-level Hedges’ g signs are not documented as direction-harmonized and therefore were not used to infer which arm they favor.
- The supplied trim-and-fill estimate was labeled *g* and cannot be interpreted as an adjusted effect in days.
- Search methods, screening counts, trial citations, risk-of-bias assessments, and study characteristics were not supplied.

## Limitations

First, the synthesis included only four trials, limiting precision in estimating between-study variance and making publication-bias diagnostics unreliable. Second, heterogeneity was extreme, so the pooled mean may not represent any particular formulation, population, or treatment protocol. Third, the absence of arm-level data prevented independent reconstruction and validation of the individual mean differences. Fourth, one computed effect was excluded for lack of native-scale data, while three other pipeline studies had no supplied effect size; the impact of their omission is unknown.

Fifth, no trial-level risk-of-bias information was available. Randomization methods, allocation concealment, masking integrity, attrition, adherence, selective reporting, and placebo adequacy could not be assessed. This is especially relevant in zinc trials because taste or local adverse effects may compromise blinding. Sixth, no moderator data were available to explain heterogeneity. Seventh, search methods and study-selection details were absent, preventing assessment of search completeness and full PRISMA flow reporting. Finally, the trial bibliographic details were not provided, so the evidence base cannot be independently identified from this dataset alone.

## Conclusion

In four randomized placebo-controlled trials with 412 participants, zinc initiated after symptom onset produced a statistically significant reduction in overall cold duration. The pooled Mean Difference was **−3.859501706282759 days** (95% CI **−5.905 to −1.814**; p = **0.00021749587062375202**), favoring zinc.

The result should be interpreted as evidence of a potentially important average benefit rather than a universally applicable treatment effect. Extreme heterogeneity (I² = 96.39%) and incomplete study-level information substantially reduce confidence in the estimate’s consistency and generalizability. Future evidence syntheses should obtain full arm-level means, SDs, and randomized denominators; distinguish formulations and doses; evaluate timing of treatment initiation; assess trial-level risk of bias; and report enough trials to support credible moderator and reporting-bias analyses.
