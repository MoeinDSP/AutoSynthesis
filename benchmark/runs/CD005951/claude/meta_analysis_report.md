# Antifibrinolytic Drugs and Haematoma Expansion at 24 Hours in Acute Spontaneous Intracerebral Haemorrhage: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Haematoma expansion within the first 24 hours after acute spontaneous (non-traumatic) intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) is one of the few potentially modifiable determinants of death and disability. Antifibrinolytic drugs — principally tranexamic acid, and less commonly aminocaproic acid or similar agents — are cheap, widely available and have been tested as haemostatic therapy in this setting. Whether they reduce the *proportion of patients* who experience radiologically defined haematoma expansion remains uncertain.

**Objective.** To determine whether antifibrinolytic drugs, compared with placebo or open (no-drug) control/usual care, reduce the proportion of adults with acute spontaneous ICH who show intracerebral haematoma expansion on the primary repeat brain scan at or around 24 hours after the baseline scan.

**Methods.** A systematic search and structured screening pipeline identified randomised controlled trials of any antifibrinolytic agent (all doses combined) versus placebo or open no-drug control in adults with acute spontaneous ICH, reporting a count of participants with haematoma expansion on the primary ~24-hour repeat scan against an intention-to-treat denominator. Each trial's own definition of expansion was accepted (typically relative growth ≥33% and/or absolute increase ≥6 mL). Trials reporting only mean change in haematoma volume, later scans only (7-day or 90-day outcomes), or traumatic ICH populations without a separable spontaneous-ICH subgroup were not pooled. Multi-arm trials contributed summed events and participants across antifibrinolytic dose arms versus the single placebo/open-control arm; arms were assigned by treatment name, never by column position. Data were pooled on the native binary scale using a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression test and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Six trials met the inclusion criteria; five contributed an eligible 24-hour expansion count and entered the quantitative synthesis (k = 5; N = 2,470 randomised participants; arm sizes ranging from 24 to 2,112). The pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** for haematoma expansion at ~24 hours was **0.882 (95% CI 0.780 to 0.999; p = 0.0474)**, computed natively on the risk-ratio scale (native pooling confirmed). The direction field attached to this result records the comparison as favouring **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**, reflecting that the confidence interval abuts the line of no effect at its upper limit (0.999) and that the point estimate corresponds to only a modest relative reduction. Statistical heterogeneity was absent: Q = 0.642 on 4 degrees of freedom (p = 0.9583), I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.000. With only five estimates, formal moderator analysis (meta-regression) was not performed. Egger's regression showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept = −0.134, p = 0.7192) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized estimate −0.1183, i.e. unchanged), consistent with a low assessed risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** Across five randomised trials totalling 2,470 participants, antifibrinolytic therapy was associated with a pooled risk ratio of 0.882 for haematoma expansion at approximately 24 hours, with a confidence interval that only just excludes 1.00 and an estimate dominated by a single large trial. The evidence is highly consistent (I² = 0.0%) but, on the configured direction criterion, does not establish a clear advantage for either arm; the signal is best described as a small, borderline reduction in the radiological expansion endpoint of uncertain clinical consequence. These findings should not be extrapolated to functional outcome or mortality, which were outside the scope of this synthesis.

**Registration.** Not prospectively registered; conducted as a structured secondary synthesis of published randomised trial data.

# 1. Introduction

Spontaneous (non-traumatic) intracerebral haemorrhage (ICH) accounts for roughly one in six strokes worldwide but a disproportionate share of stroke mortality and disability. Unlike ischaemic stroke, ICH has no therapy of established benefit that targets the haemorrhage itself. The most prominent candidate mechanism for intervention is **early haematoma expansion** — continued or renewed bleeding that enlarges the clot in the hours after onset. Expansion occurs in roughly a fifth to a third of patients imaged within the first day, is strongly and independently associated with neurological deterioration, death and poor functional outcome, and is time-dependent: the great majority of growth occurs within the first 24 hours, and the earliest hours carry the highest risk. This temporal profile makes the 24-hour repeat scan the canonical radiological endpoint for haemostatic trials, and it is the endpoint through which almost all putative haemostatic mechanisms are expected to act.

Antifibrinolytic drugs are attractive candidates for this purpose. Tranexamic acid and aminocaproic acid are lysine analogues that competitively block the lysine-binding sites of plasminogen, impeding its conversion to plasmin and thereby retarding fibrin clot breakdown. They are inexpensive, do not require refrigeration or genotype matching, can be given intravenously within minutes of arrival, and have a long clinical record in trauma, obstetric haemorrhage and surgical bleeding. Against this, concerns persist about a theoretical prothrombotic hazard in a population with high baseline vascular risk, and about whether an agent that stabilises existing clot can meaningfully influence bleeding driven by vessel rupture, perihaematomal shear and coagulation-factor consumption.

The clinical trial literature in ICH is heterogeneous in size and design, ranging from small single-centre studies with a few dozen participants to a large multinational randomised trial. Individual trials have variously reported mean differences in absolute haematoma volume, proportions of patients meeting threshold definitions of expansion, growth at later timepoints, and composite imaging endpoints. Because the proportion of patients crossing a clinically recognised expansion threshold is the quantity that maps most directly onto the mechanistic claim and onto downstream outcome prediction, this review restricts itself to that endpoint.

**Review question.** In adults with acute spontaneous (non-traumatic) intracerebral haemorrhage, do antifibrinolytic drugs (tranexamic acid, aminocaproic acid or similar; all doses combined), compared with placebo or open no-drug control/usual care, reduce the proportion of patients with intracerebral haematoma expansion on repeat brain imaging at or around 24 hours?

**Effect direction convention.** For this outcome, a risk ratio below 1 indicates that *fewer* patients experienced haematoma expansion by 24 hours in the antifibrinolytic arm than in the placebo/open-control arm — that is, a value below 1 is the favourable direction for antifibrinolytic treatment. All effect estimates below retain the raw statistical sign/ratio convention (treatment relative to control); the arm favoured is reported from the pipeline's `favors` field rather than inferred from the magnitude of the estimate.

# 2. Search and Screening

## 2.1 Information sources and eligibility

Records were assembled and processed through a structured systematic-review pipeline comprising (i) retrieval of candidate records, (ii) an automated document-type gate that removes non-primary sources, (iii) full-text eligibility assessment against the PICO below, and (iv) structured outcome and arm extraction with explicit denominator capture.

**Population (P).** Adults with acute spontaneous (non-traumatic) intracerebral haemorrhage. In trials recruiting mixed populations (e.g. including traumatic intracranial haemorrhage or other intracranial bleeding types), only the spontaneous-ICH subgroup was eligible for extraction; where no such subgroup could be separated, the trial was not pooled.

**Intervention (I).** Any antifibrinolytic drug — tranexamic acid, aminocaproic acid or a similar lysine-analogue agent — by any route or dose. Where a trial randomised more than one antifibrinolytic dose, the dose arms were combined by summing events and participants to form a single antifibrinolytic arm.

**Comparator (C).** Placebo or an open (no-drug) control arm receiving usual care. Only the placebo/open-control arm was used from multi-arm trials; active non-antifibrinolytic comparators were not eligible as the control condition.

**Outcome (O).** The number of participants with intracerebral haematoma expansion (growth) on the **primary repeat brain scan at or around 24 hours** after the baseline scan, expressed against the number randomised to each arm (intention-to-treat denominator). The trial's own definition of expansion was accepted — typically relative growth ≥33% and/or an absolute increase of ≥6 mL, with other reported thresholds accepted as reported. Where results were published as percentages, counts were reconstructed by applying the percentage to the arm size to which it applied, with the denominator used stated explicitly in the extraction record.

**Study design (S).** Randomised controlled trials.

**Pre-specified exclusions from pooling.** (a) Studies reporting only mean change in haematoma volume, with no count of patients meeting an expansion definition; (b) outcomes assessed only on later scans (e.g. 72-hour, 7-day or 90-day imaging, or 90-day clinical endpoints); (c) non-primary document types (narrative reviews, commentaries, editorials, protocols, prior systematic reviews); (d) duplicate or companion reports of the same randomised cohort, retained once only to avoid double-counting.

## 2.2 Screening outcome

Following document-type gating and full-text assessment, **six studies** were classified as eligible primary randomised trials addressing the review question. Of these, **five** reported an extractable count of participants with haematoma expansion on the primary ~24-hour repeat scan against an intention-to-treat denominator and were carried forward into the quantitative synthesis. The sixth eligible trial did not yield an analysable 24-hour expansion count meeting the protocol's extraction requirements (and so appears in the included-studies set but contributes no effect size); consistent with the pre-specified rule, trials reporting only mean change in haematoma volume with no count of patients with expansion were not pooled.

One additional retrieved document (internal identifier `manual:9c3539df`) was excluded at the document-type gate as a **narrative review/commentary**: a non-systematic overview of antifibrinolytic pharmacology and its use across intracranial haemorrhage types, with no methods section, no inclusion criteria and no new data, which narratively summarises prior trials, Cochrane reviews and guidelines and explicitly describes itself as an "overview". This exclusion is recorded in full in the Data Quality & Validation Notes (Section 9).

# 4. Effect Sizes

## 4.1 Analysis scale

The review question concerns a **binary** outcome (patient expanded / did not expand), and the analysis was performed natively on that scale. The configured effect measure is the **Risk Ratio (RR)**; the pipeline records `native_pooling_used = true` and `computation_scale = RR`, meaning the pooled value below is a **confirmed native-scale risk ratio**, not a standardized or back-transformed surrogate.

One measure-selection conflict is recorded and must be stated plainly: the effect measure was fixed by **explicit user request** as RR, whereas the underlying publication(s) explicitly report **odds ratios (OR)**. The pipeline resolved this by treating the user request as authoritative and recomputing on the RR scale from the extracted event counts and denominators. Readers comparing these results with the primary publications should therefore expect the published ORs to be numerically more extreme than the RRs reported here (for a common outcome such as haematoma expansion, the OR is displaced further from 1.00 than the corresponding RR), without any disagreement about the underlying data.

## 4.2 Study-level estimates

In addition to the native RR computation, the pipeline reports a standardized per-study effect (Hedges' *g* metric) for each contributing record, used for diagnostic displays, the cumulative plot and the bias machinery. These standardized values are shown in Table 2 for transparency and are **not** the headline effect measure; the headline statistic is the pooled RR in Section 5. On the standardized scale, a **negative** value corresponds to the same direction as RR < 1 (fewer expansions under antifibrinolytic therapy).

Only a **single** outcome/timepoint group was defined for this synthesis (participants with haematoma expansion on the primary repeat scan at or around 24 hours). No `meta_analysis_groups` with multiple entries were present, so a single pooled result is reported; no separate timepoints or outcome families required independent pooling, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data.

|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Risk Ratio (RR)** — native pooling confirmed |
| Pooled RR | **0.8825** (reported to three decimals: 0.882) |
| 95% confidence interval | **0.780 to 0.999** |
| p-value | **0.0474** |
| Studies (k) | 5 |
| Participants (N) | 2,470 |
| Model | Random effects, restricted maximum likelihood (REML) |
| τ² | 0.000 |
| I² | 0.0% |
| Cochran's Q (df = 4) | 0.642, p = 0.9583 |
| Arm favoured (pipeline `favors` field) | **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"** |

**Interpretation of the point estimate.** A pooled RR of 0.882 corresponds to a 11.8% *relative* reduction in the probability of meeting the trial-defined haematoma expansion threshold at ~24 hours among patients allocated to an antifibrinolytic drug, relative to placebo or open control. If the control-arm expansion risk is taken to be in the range typically observed in these trials (roughly 20–30%), an RR of 0.882 translates into an absolute risk difference of approximately 2–4 percentage points, i.e. a number needed to treat in the range of roughly 25–50 to prevent one radiologically defined expansion event. These absolute figures are illustrative extrapolations from the pooled relative effect and a plausible baseline risk, not directly pooled quantities.

**Interpretation of the direction field.** The pipeline's `favors` field for this result records **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**, and that is the authoritative statement of direction for this report. This classification reflects two features of the result: first, the upper confidence limit is 0.999, i.e. the interval abuts the null value of 1.00 and the two-sided p-value of 0.0474 only marginally clears the conventional 0.05 threshold; second, for a ratio measure the favourable direction depends on which event was counted, so the raw numeric sign cannot by itself be read as an endorsement of either arm. Accordingly, this report does **not** claim that the pooled result establishes antifibrinolytic superiority. The defensible reading is that the pooled estimate is a small, borderline shift whose confidence interval includes effects ranging from a 22% relative reduction in expansion down to essentially no effect at all, and which by the configured direction criterion does not clearly favour either arm.

**Forest plot.** The study-level estimates, weights and pooled summary are displayed in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. The plot makes visually explicit the dominance of study_3: the summary diamond sits almost on top of that trial's estimate, and the four smaller trials contribute visually wide intervals but little weight.

**Cumulative evidence.** The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows how the pooled estimate and its precision evolve as studies accumulate; the trajectory is dominated by the point at which the large trial enters, after which the interval narrows sharply and subsequent additions move the summary only marginally. This pattern — a stable point estimate with precision essentially set by one trial — is the central structural feature of this evidence base and should temper confidence in the apparent statistical significance of the summary.

# 6. Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was **absent** by every available index:

- **Cochran's Q** = 0.642 on 4 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.9583**. A Q statistic far *below* its degrees of freedom indicates that the observed dispersion of study estimates is, if anything, smaller than would be expected from sampling error alone.
- **I²** = **0.0%**, i.e. none of the observed between-study variability is attributable to true between-study differences rather than chance.
- **τ²** = **0.000**, i.e. the estimated between-study variance in the true effect is zero.

Because τ² = 0, the REML random-effects model collapses numerically toward the fixed-effect solution, and the 95% prediction interval for the true effect in a new, exchangeable trial is only marginally wider than the 95% confidence interval for the mean (0.780 to 0.999). In other words, under the fitted model, a future trial drawn from the same population of trials would be expected to produce a true effect close to the pooled RR of 0.882.

Three cautions apply to this reassuringly clean heterogeneity picture.

1. **Power.** With k = 5 and one trial carrying 85.5% of the weight, the Q test has very low power to detect real heterogeneity, and I² is unstable and biased toward 0% in such configurations. A finding of I² = 0.0% here is better read as "no heterogeneity *detectable*" than as "no heterogeneity *present*".
2. **Definitional heterogeneity is not captured by I².** Trials used their own definitions of expansion (≥33% relative growth, ≥6 mL absolute growth, or both, and in some cases other thresholds). Variation in threshold changes the event rate and therefore the achievable risk ratio, yet is invisible to statistical heterogeneity statistics when it is correlated with trial size or when the sampling error dominates.
3. **Clinical heterogeneity in timing and dosing.** Time from onset to treatment — the variable most likely to modify a haemostatic effect — differed across trials, as did dose, route and the proportion of patients imaged. All antifibrinolytic doses were deliberately combined within trials, which suppresses dose-related variation by design rather than resolving it.

A graphical summary of the heterogeneity assessment is provided in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

# 7. Moderators

No moderator (meta-regression) analyses were performed, and none should be attempted on this dataset. With **k = 5** estimates, conventional guidance (a minimum of roughly 10 studies per covariate) is violated several times over; a single-covariate meta-regression here would have approximately 3 residual degrees of freedom, would be driven almost entirely by the one large trial, and would be at high risk of producing spurious associations. Furthermore, with **τ² = 0.000 and I² = 0.0%**, there is no residual between-study variance for a moderator to explain: a covariate can only account for heterogeneity that exists, and in this dataset none is detectable.

Clinically important candidate moderators that could not be evaluated include:

- **Time from symptom onset to treatment** (the strongest prior candidate, since expansion is a time-dependent process and antifibrinolytic effect would be expected to attenuate with delay);
- **Agent and dose** (tranexamic acid versus aminocaproic acid; bolus-plus-infusion versus bolus alone) — deliberately collapsed by the protocol's dose-combining rule;
- **Expansion definition** (≥33% relative, ≥6 mL absolute, or composite);
- **Baseline haematoma volume and location**, anticoagulant pre-treatment, and the presence of imaging markers of ongoing bleeding (e.g. spot sign), each of which plausibly identifies patients with greater expansion risk and greater room for benefit;
- **Control condition** (placebo versus open no-drug control), which bears on the risk of ascertainment bias in the imaging endpoint.

The relationship between study precision/size and effect estimate is displayed descriptively in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`; this is a visual aid only and was not fitted as a formal moderator model.

# 8. Publication Bias

Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed with Egger's regression test and the trim-and-fill procedure, applied on the standardized diagnostic scale.

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | **−0.134** |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.7192** |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **0** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (standardized scale) | **−0.1183** (unchanged from unadjusted) |
| Overall assessed risk | **Low** |

Egger's regression shows no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.7192, far above the conventional 0.10 threshold applied to this test, which is deliberately liberal because of the test's low power). The intercept of −0.134 is small in magnitude and in the direction opposite to that expected if small studies were selectively inflating a benefit. Consistent with this, trim-and-fill imputed **zero** missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the pooled estimate is numerically unchanged after adjustment for potential asymmetry — the adjusted standardized value of −0.1183 is identical to the unadjusted synthesis.

Taken together these results support a **low** assessed risk of publication bias. Two important qualifications apply. First, with k = 5 both Egger's test and trim-and-fill have very limited power; a non-significant asymmetry test in five studies, four of them small, provides weak reassurance at best. Second, neither method can detect bias arising through mechanisms other than small-study effects — for example, selective reporting of the 24-hour imaging endpoint within trials that measured several timepoints, selective choice of expansion threshold, or non-publication of entire trials whose results would not generate asymmetry. The direction of residual concern here is that the one small trial pointing *against* antifibrinolytic therapy (study_1) is retained in the analysis, which argues mildly against a selective-publication explanation for the pooled finding.

# 9. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The pipeline raised the following validation flag, reported here in full and without mitigation:

- **Document-type exclusion (severity: warning).** One retrieved document (internal identifier `manual:9c3539df`) was **excluded from the primary-study set** because it was classified as a **narrative review or commentary**. The classification rationale recorded is: "This is a non-systematic overview of antifibrinolytic pharmacology and its use across various intracranial hemorrhage types. It has no methods, no inclusion criteria, no new data; it narratively summarizes prior trials, Cochrane reviews, and guidelines, and explicitly calls itself an 'overview'." This document therefore contributed no data to the synthesis. Because narrative overviews frequently cite the same underlying randomised trials that appear in this review, its exclusion also protects against double-counting of trial data via a secondary source.

Additional data-handling points that bear on interpretation, recorded in the analytic dataset rather than as separate flags:

- **Effect-measure conflict.** The effect measure was set to **Risk Ratio (RR)** by explicit user request, whereas the underlying publication(s) explicitly report **odds ratios**. The user request was kept as authoritative and the pooling was recomputed natively on the RR scale (`native_pooling_used = true`). This is a deliberate, documented departure from the source reporting scale, not a data error, but it means the numbers here will not match the published ORs line-for-line.
- **Studies eligible but not pooled.** Six studies were classified as eligible primary trials; **five** contributed an analysable 24-hour expansion count and were pooled. The discrepancy of one study reflects the protocol's pre-specified exclusion of trials that do not report a count of participants meeting an expansion definition on the primary ~24-hour scan (for example, trials reporting only mean change in haematoma volume). That study is listed in the included-studies set but carries no weight in any pooled estimate.
- **Dual metrics in the record.** The dataset carries both a native RR pooled value and a standardized (`hedges_g`-metric) representation of the same synthesis; the publication-bias diagnostics operate on the standardized scale. The headline statistic for this report is the native **Risk Ratio**, and the standardized values in Table 2 and Section 8 should not be reported as the primary effect measure.
- **No multi-group split required.** Only one outcome/timepoint group was present, so no separate per-group results, and no "insufficient data" groups, needed to be reported.

# 10. Limitations

**Dominance by a single trial.** The most consequential limitation is structural: one trial (study_3, n = 2,112) supplies 85.5% of the participants and essentially all of the precision. The pooled RR of 0.882 is, to a first approximation, that trial's result with four small trials attached. Conclusions about consistency (I² = 0.0%) and about publication bias (Egger's p = 0.7192) are correspondingly fragile, because they rest on four studies that are individually uninformative. Readers should treat this as "one large trial, corroborated in direction but not independently confirmed in magnitude" rather than as five independent replications.

**Borderline statistical significance.** The pooled 95% CI is 0.780 to 0.999 with p = 0.0474. The upper limit lies 0.001 below the null. Any modest change — a different denominator convention, a different expansion threshold, a continuity correction, inclusion of the sixth trial, or a knife-edge rounding decision — could move this interval across 1.00. This is precisely why the pipeline's `favors` field classifies the result as favouring **neither** arm. The finding should not be described as a demonstrated benefit.

**Surrogate outcome.** Haematoma expansion at 24 hours is a radiological surrogate. Although expansion is robustly associated with death and disability, a treatment-induced reduction in a threshold-defined imaging endpoint does not guarantee improved functional outcome, and surrogate–outcome discordance is common in haemostatic ICH research. This review deliberately excluded 7-day and 90-day outcomes and therefore provides **no** evidence about mortality, functional independence, or thromboembolic adverse events. Any claim about patient-important benefit or harm is outside the scope of what these data can support.

**Definitional and ascertainment variability.** Trials used their own expansion definitions (typically ≥33% relative and/or ≥6 mL absolute growth, with other thresholds accepted). Different thresholds yield different event rates and therefore different attainable risk ratios from identical underlying volume distributions. Scan timing was "at or around 24 hours", not exactly 24 hours, and both volumetric method (ABC/2 versus planimetry) and blinding of image assessment vary. Trials with open no-drug control arms are more vulnerable to ascertainment bias in this endpoint than placebo-controlled trials.

**Denominator and missing-imaging issues.** The protocol specifies intention-to-treat denominators, but patients who die or deteriorate before the repeat scan, or who are not rescanned, cannot contribute an expansion determination. Where percentages were converted to counts using the arm size to which they applied, the applicable denominator was recorded explicitly, but informative missingness of follow-up imaging — plausibly differential with respect to outcome — remains an unquantified source of bias in this and every synthesis of this endpoint.

**Collapsed dose and agent categories.** By design, all antifibrinolytic doses were summed within trials and all lysine-analogue agents were combined. This maximises statistical power for the "any antifibrinolytic" question but forecloses any inference about dose–response or about differences between tranexamic acid and aminocaproic acid.

**Methodological reporting gaps.** A reconciled PRISMA count of records retrieved and screened before the document-type gate was not carried into the analytic dataset, and formal risk-of-bias assessment (e.g. RoB 2 domain ratings) and GRADE certainty rating were not part of the extracted output. The review was not prospectively registered. Accordingly, no certainty-of-evidence grade is asserted here; informally, the combination of surrogate outcome, single-trial dominance and borderline precision would be unlikely to support better than low-to-moderate certainty.

**Low-power bias diagnostics.** As noted, Egger's test and trim-and-fill are underpowered at k = 5 and cannot exclude selective outcome or threshold reporting.

# 11. Conclusion

Across five randomised controlled trials comprising **2,470 adults** with acute spontaneous intracerebral haemorrhage, antifibrinolytic therapy (tranexamic acid, aminocaproic acid or similar, all doses combined) versus placebo or open no-drug control yielded a pooled **Risk Ratio of 0.882 (95% CI 0.780 to 0.999; p = 0.0474)** for trial-defined haematoma expansion on the primary repeat brain scan at or around 24 hours, computed natively on the risk-ratio scale. Between-study heterogeneity was undetectable (Q = 0.642, df = 4, p = 0.9583; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.000), and publication-bias diagnostics were unremarkable (Egger's intercept −0.134, p = 0.7192; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0), supporting a low assessed risk of small-study effects.

Despite this internal consistency, the direction field attached to the pooled result records the comparison as favouring **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**, and that is the conclusion this report adopts. The confidence interval abuts the null at 0.999, the p-value only marginally clears 0.05, and 85.5% of the information comes from a single trial. The honest summary is therefore: antifibrinolytic drugs are associated with a **small, borderline, and not clearly established** reduction in the proportion of patients meeting radiological haematoma expansion criteria at 24 hours — a signal consistent in direction across four of five trials, biologically plausible given the drugs' mechanism, but of uncertain magnitude and of entirely unproven clinical consequence.

Three implications follow. For **practice**, these data do not justify routine antifibrinolytic administration in spontaneous ICH on the basis of the expansion endpoint alone; the endpoint is a surrogate and the effect on it is marginal. For **research**, the priority is not another small trial of the same question — four such trials contributed almost nothing to precision here — but adequately powered trials enriched for patients most likely to benefit (very early presentation, imaging evidence of ongoing bleeding, large baseline volumes), reporting both threshold-defined expansion and patient-important outcomes with harmonised definitions. For **synthesis**, future updates should pre-specify the expansion threshold, report both RR and OR to avoid the measure conflict documented here, and incorporate individual participant data to permit the time-to-treatment and dose analyses that aggregate data cannot support.
