# Elective Immediate Versus Therapeutic Delayed Neck Dissection for Disease-Free Survival in Early-Stage, Clinically Node-Negative Oral Cavity Squamous Cell Carcinoma: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

## Abstract

**Background:** Management of the clinically node-negative neck in early-stage oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma may involve elective neck dissection at primary-tumour surgery or observation followed by therapeutic neck dissection if nodal disease emerges.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of elective immediate prophylactic neck dissection, compared with therapeutic delayed neck dissection after watchful waiting, on disease-free survival (DFS).

**Methods:** The supplied evidence pipeline contained five studies, of which two contributed effect estimates to the quantitative synthesis. These two studies included 746 participants. The prespecified effect measure was the hazard ratio (HR) for DFS, with synonymous event-free, relapse-free, or recurrence-free survival outcomes eligible when they represented time to new disease, recurrence, progression, or death from any cause. A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ².

**Results:** The meta-analysis of two studies produced a pooled Hazard Ratio (HR) of **0.5535081029609918** (95% CI **0.355–0.864**; p = **0.009183178099906139**; total N = **746**). Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = **71.78%**; τ² = **0.074639**; Q = **3.544**, p = **0.0598**). Although an HR below 1 would ordinarily indicate a lower DFS-event hazard under an elective-versus-therapeutic contrast, the supplied direction field classified the result as favoring **neither arm** and stated that favorability could not be determined. Accordingly, no arm-specific benefit is assigned here without verification of event coding and contrast orientation.

**Conclusions:** The pooled HR was statistically different from 1, but substantial heterogeneity, only two contributing studies, incomplete study-level reporting, and unresolved direction coding limit clinical interpretation. Verification of the original HR definitions and treatment-reference coding is essential before concluding that either management strategy improves DFS.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

Using a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), the pooled **Hazard Ratio (HR)** was **0.5535081029609918** (95% CI **0.355–0.864**; p = **0.009183178099906139**), based on **k = 2** studies and **N = 746** participants.

The confidence interval excluded the null value of 1, and the model-based test was statistically significant. Nevertheless, treatment direction requires caution. The prespecified contrast indicates that an HR below 1 would ordinarily correspond to fewer or later DFS events with elective neck dissection. In contrast, the supplied meta-analysis direction field explicitly states that the result favors **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).”** Consistent with the required direction field, this report does not assign the pooled result to either arm. The discrepancy should be resolved by checking each source report’s event definition, numerator/reference group, and HR coding before making a clinical claim of benefit.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- **I² = 71.78%**
- **τ² = 0.074639**
- **Q = 3.544** with 1 degree of freedom
- **Q-test p = 0.0598**

The I² value indicates that a considerable proportion of observed variation was attributed to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error. The Q test did not cross a conventional 0.05 threshold, but this should not be treated as evidence of homogeneity: with only two studies, Q has very low power to detect genuine between-study differences. The τ² estimate also remains imprecise when k = 2.

## Discussion

This synthesis addressed whether immediate elective neck dissection or observation followed by delayed therapeutic dissection affects DFS in early-stage, clinically node-negative oral cavity squamous cell carcinoma. Across two studies and 746 participants, the REML random-effects analysis yielded a pooled **Hazard Ratio (HR) of 0.5535081029609918** (95% CI **0.355–0.864**; p = **0.009183178099906139**). The estimate was statistically different from the null, but heterogeneity was substantial (I² = **71.78%**).

From a clinical perspective, elective dissection may eradicate occult nodal disease before it becomes clinically evident, improve pathological staging, and guide adjuvant treatment. Conversely, a surveillance strategy can spare neck surgery in patients who never develop nodal disease, provided that surveillance is sufficiently sensitive and salvage treatment is timely and effective. DFS captures both recurrence and death-related events and is therefore more comprehensive than a simple recurrence proportion.

The principal interpretive issue is the unresolved direction coding. Under the stated elective-versus-therapeutic contrast, an HR below 1 would conventionally favor elective treatment for an adverse-event DFS endpoint. However, the supplied direction field did not identify either arm as favored. The pooled estimate should therefore be treated as evidence of a time-to-event difference whose clinical direction requires source-level verification, rather than as definitive evidence for either management strategy. This caution is particularly important because reversing the reference group or coding survival rather than failure can reverse the interpretation of a ratio estimate.

The substantial heterogeneity indicates that the studies may not estimate a single common clinical effect. With only two studies, the random-effects mean and between-study variance are unstable, and the nominal p value does not resolve concerns about generalizability or consistency. Clinical decision-making should therefore consider tumour characteristics, patient preferences, operative morbidity, surveillance capacity, and the feasibility of prompt salvage treatment in addition to this quantitative estimate.

## Limitations

1. **Very small meta-analytic evidence base:** Only two studies contributed to pooling, limiting precision, robustness, and generalizability.
2. **Substantial heterogeneity:** I² was **71.78%**, and reliable exploration of its causes was impossible.
3. **Unresolved direction coding:** The numerical HR and supplied favorability field do not support an unambiguous arm-specific interpretation without source verification.
4. **Incomplete study-level data:** Individual HRs, follow-up durations, DFS definitions, intention-to-treat status, patient characteristics, and treatment details were unavailable.
5. **Unexplained attrition from qualitative to quantitative evidence:** Five studies were listed, but only two effect sizes were pooled.
6. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** The supplied context did not include domain-level or overall judgments, preventing evaluation of internal validity.
7. **No meaningful publication-bias assessment:** Statistical investigation of small-study effects is not reliable with two studies.
8. **Uncertain robustness of random-effects inference:** REML estimates of τ² and the pooled confidence interval can be unstable with k = 2; no Hartung–Knapp or sensitivity analysis was supplied.
9. **Incomplete review methods:** Search sources, dates, screening procedures, protocol registration, data-extraction methods, and certainty-of-evidence assessment were not reported in the context.
10. **Potential endpoint variation:** Although the target was DFS and synonymous composite time-to-event outcomes, study-specific endpoint definitions could not be verified.

## Conclusion

In two studies comprising **746 participants**, random-effects meta-analysis produced a pooled **Hazard Ratio (HR) of 0.5535081029609918** (95% CI **0.355–0.864**; p = **0.009183178099906139**) for DFS, with substantial heterogeneity (I² = **71.78%**; τ² = **0.074639**). The supplied direction field classified the finding as favoring neither treatment arm, so an arm-specific conclusion should not be drawn until the HR contrast, event coding, and reference groups are verified in the original reports. The limited number of studies, incomplete study-level information, and unresolved data inconsistencies substantially reduce confidence in the result. Additional well-reported comparative studies with consistent DFS definitions, intention-to-treat HRs, and adequate long-term follow-up are needed.
