# Atorvastatin Versus Placebo or No Treatment and Withdrawal Due to Adverse Effects in Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Withdrawal because of adverse effects is an important measure of treatment acceptability. This review evaluated whether atorvastatin, compared with placebo or no treatment, affects withdrawal or discontinuation due to adverse effects in adults.

### Methods
The supplied evidence pipeline contained six studies, but only two study-level effect sizes were available for quantitative synthesis. Together, the two analyzable studies included 118 participants. A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. Although the target outcome was binary, the native binary effect measure could not be confirmed; consequently, the analysis used Hedges’ g as an unresolved standardized fallback rather than a risk ratio, odds ratio, or risk difference.

### Results
The pooled Hedges’ g was −0.018983374012459638 (95% CI −0.380 to 0.342; p = 0.9178988094791287; k = 2; total N = 118), with the analysis direction designated as favoring atorvastatin. The estimate was negligible and its confidence interval crossed the null. There was no detected statistical heterogeneity (Q = 0.005, p = 0.9433; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). With only two effect sizes, moderator analysis and statistical assessment of publication bias were not informative.

### Conclusions
The available analysis found no statistically significant difference between atorvastatin and placebo or no treatment in withdrawal due to adverse effects. However, this conclusion is substantially limited by the small evidence base and by use of an unconfirmed standardized fallback measure for an inherently binary outcome. The result should not be interpreted as a pooled relative risk or as establishing equivalence or non-inferiority.

## Introduction

Withdrawal or discontinuation because of adverse effects is a clinically relevant outcome that combines aspects of tolerability, treatment burden, and patients’ willingness or ability to continue therapy. For preventive medicines such as statins, even modest differences in discontinuation may affect long-term adherence and potential clinical benefit.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: **In adults, what is the effect of atorvastatin, compared with placebo or no treatment, on withdrawal from the study or assigned treatment because of adverse effects?** The prespecified outcome was binary: the number of adults withdrawing due to adverse effects among the number randomized or otherwise included in each comparison group. A favorable effect was defined as fewer withdrawals with atorvastatin.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across two studies and 118 participants, atorvastatin had a negligible standardized fallback effect on withdrawal due to adverse effects compared with placebo or no treatment. The pooled Hedges’ g was **−0.018983374012459638** (95% CI **−0.380 to 0.342**; p = **0.9178988094791287**), and the supplied direction field identified the result as favoring atorvastatin. Nevertheless, the confidence interval crossed the null, and the analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant treatment difference.

The individual estimates were also close to zero: **0.0** in study_3_R3 and **−0.028007** in study_4_R4. Observed statistical heterogeneity was absent (I² = **0.0%**, τ² = **0.0**), but this finding is not conclusive given the very small number of studies.

### Clinical interpretation

The result does not provide direct information about how many withdrawals would be prevented or caused per 100 or 1,000 treated adults. Such interpretation requires arm-level event counts and denominators and, ideally, pooling on a native binary scale. In their absence, the standardized fallback estimate cannot be translated reliably into a risk ratio, odds ratio, risk difference, or number needed to treat or harm.

The broad practical conclusion is therefore limited: the analyzed data did not reveal a detectable difference in adverse-effect withdrawals, but they also do not rule out potentially meaningful benefit or harm. A nonsignificant result from two small studies should not be taken as evidence that atorvastatin and control are equivalent in tolerability.

### Certainty and applicability

A formal certainty-of-evidence assessment could not be completed because risk-of-bias information, direct event data, study characteristics, and reporting details were absent. The certainty of the quantitative conclusion is constrained by the small sample, incomplete contribution of studies to the outcome, unresolved effect-measure classification, and inability to evaluate publication bias. Applicability to specific adult populations, atorvastatin doses, or treatment durations cannot be determined from the supplied information.

## Limitations

1. **Very small meta-analytic evidence base:** Only two studies and 118 participants contributed effect sizes, limiting precision, heterogeneity assessment, and robustness.
2. **Unconfirmed effect measure:** The target outcome was binary, but the native effect measure was unresolved. Hedges’ g was used as a standardized fallback, not as a confirmed native-scale measure.
3. **Missing arm-level data:** Withdrawal event counts and group-specific denominators were unavailable, preventing verification and calculation of clinically interpretable binary effects.
4. **Incomplete study contribution:** Six studies appeared in the pipeline, but only two contributed effect sizes. Reasons for the absence of quantitative data from the other four studies were not reported.
5. **Insufficient study descriptions:** Trial design, population, atorvastatin dose, control condition, follow-up, and setting were unavailable, precluding assessment of clinical comparability and applicability.
6. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** The internal validity of the contributing studies could not be evaluated.
7. **No informative moderator or publication-bias analysis:** With k = 2, such analyses would be underpowered or invalid.
8. **Incomplete review-process reporting:** Search methods, screening procedures, protocol information, and study-selection reasons were not available, preventing full verification of PRISMA compliance.

## Conclusion

Based on two studies involving 118 participants, atorvastatin did not significantly differ from placebo or no treatment in withdrawal due to adverse effects. The pooled Hedges’ g was **−0.018983374012459638** (95% CI **−0.380 to 0.342**; p = **0.9178988094791287**), with the supplied direction designation favoring atorvastatin. No statistical heterogeneity was detected (Q = **0.005**, p = **0.9433**; I² = **0.0%**; τ² = **0.0**).

This finding remains uncertain because Hedges’ g was an unresolved standardized fallback for a binary outcome, only two of six pipeline studies contributed effect sizes, and arm-level events were unavailable. Future evidence syntheses should obtain the original numbers of withdrawals and participants in each treatment arm, pool an explicitly verified binary measure, document why each eligible study did or did not contribute data, and assess study-level risk of bias.
