# Behavioural Support for Tobacco-Use Cessation With or Without Nicotine Replacement Therapy or Varenicline: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Behavioural support may improve tobacco-use cessation when delivered alone or alongside cessation pharmacotherapy. This review assessed behavioural support versus matched brief-advice or no-intensive-support controls, with behavioural support alone, behavioural support plus nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), and behavioural support plus varenicline prespecified as intervention-combination subgroups.

### Methods
The supplied evidence set comprised seven studies, of which five contributed effect estimates to a random-effects meta-analysis fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The outcome was study-defined tobacco abstinence at follow-up. The native pooled measure was the Odds Ratio (OR). Publication-bias diagnostics included Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill. Search sources, dates, full screening counts, study-level subgroup assignments, abstinence definitions, follow-up durations, and risk-of-bias assessments were not supplied.

### Results
Five effect estimates representing 1,610 participants were pooled. The pooled Odds Ratio (OR) was 1.5431663998170715 (95% CI 0.855 to 2.784; p=0.14963389689757495). According to the supplied direction field, this result favored neither arm—essentially a tie, or a ratio result for which favorability depends on the event counted—and the event coding needed to resolve that ambiguity was unavailable. Heterogeneity was moderate to substantial (I²=55.58%; τ²=0.226875; Q=8.64, df=4, p=0.0708). Subgroup-specific estimates for behavioural support alone, support plus NRT, and support plus varenicline could not be calculated or reported because study-level subgroup assignments were absent. Egger’s test was not statistically significant (intercept 0.7799; p=0.2362), but trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study; publication-bias risk was therefore classified as moderate.

### Conclusions
The pooled estimate was imprecise and not statistically significant, with meaningful between-study heterogeneity and unresolved event-direction coding. Although the point estimate was an OR of 1.5431663998170715, the supplied analysis classified favorability as neither arm, so a directional cessation benefit should not be inferred. Missing subgroup coding, study characteristics, outcome definitions, follow-up information, and risk-of-bias data substantially constrain interpretation. Better-reported trials and a study-level reanalysis preserving the three prespecified intervention combinations are required.

## Search/Screening

The review question was structured around people who use tobacco; behavioural support delivered alone, with nicotine replacement therapy (NRT), or with varenicline; matched controls that held pharmacotherapy constant where applicable; and tobacco-use cessation at study-defined follow-up.

The supplied pipeline summary identified **7 studies**, but only **5 effect sizes** entered the quantitative synthesis. The materials did not provide bibliographic databases, supplementary sources, search strings, search dates, language or publication-status restrictions, deduplication procedures, reviewer procedures, or stage-by-stage screening counts. Consequently, a reproducible search strategy and a numerical PRISMA flow diagram cannot be reconstructed. Reasons that two of the seven studies did not contribute to the meta-analysis were also not reported.

Eligibility was conceptually based on matched comparisons:

1. **Behavioural support alone:** brief advice or no intervention as control;
2. **Behavioural support plus NRT:** brief advice plus NRT, or NRT without intensive behavioural support, as control; and
3. **Behavioural support plus varenicline:** varenicline alone, or varenicline without intensive behavioural support, as control.

The target outcome included continuous or prolonged abstinence and point-prevalence abstinence, verified biochemically or by self-report. The intended extraction hierarchy favored continuous/prolonged over point-prevalence abstinence, biochemical verification over self-report, and the longest reported follow-up unless a common review-defined time point was needed. However, study-level abstinence definitions, verification methods, and follow-up times were not supplied, so adherence to this hierarchy could not be audited.

## Included Studies

Seven studies were represented in the evidence set, and five contributed to the pooled model. These five estimates included **1,610 participants** in total. Their individual sample sizes ranged from **81 to 506**.

No study names, publication years, countries, recruitment settings, tobacco products, participant demographics, baseline tobacco use, intervention intensity, provider type, control content, pharmacotherapy dosing, follow-up duration, attrition, or cessation-event counts were available. It is therefore not possible to characterize clinical diversity, determine applicability to particular populations, or establish whether the intervention and control conditions were matched as intended.

Most importantly, the five analyzed comparisons were not mapped to the three prespecified intervention-combination categories. The dataset therefore supports one overall model but does **not** permit transparent reporting of separate estimates for behavioural support alone, behavioural support plus NRT, and behavioural support plus varenicline.

A formal study-level risk-of-bias assessment was not supplied. No conclusions can therefore be drawn about randomization, allocation concealment, masking of outcome assessment, missing outcome data, selective reporting, or other trial-specific biases.

A random-effects model was fitted using **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**. Across **5 studies/effect sizes** and **1,610 participants**, the **pooled Odds Ratio (OR)** was:

> **OR=1.5431663998170715 (95% CI 0.855 to 2.784; p=0.14963389689757495).**

The confidence interval included the null value of 1, and the pooled result was not statistically significant at the conventional two-sided 0.05 level.

Crucially, the supplied direction field classified the result as favoring **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).”** This designation is retained without reinterpretation. ## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was:

- **Cochran’s Q=8.64**, with **4 degrees of freedom**;
- **Q-test p=0.0708**;
- **I²=55.58%**; and
- **τ²=0.226875**.

The I² value indicates moderate-to-substantial inconsistency among observed effects. Although the Q test did not reach p<0.05, its p value of 0.0708 does not establish homogeneity, particularly with only five studies and consequently low power to detect dispersion. The nonzero τ² likewise indicates estimated between-study variability. Accordingly, emphasis should be placed on the uncertainty and variation represented by the random-effects model rather than on the Q-test threshold alone.

Potential sources of heterogeneity include the three intervention combinations, behavioural-support intensity, control-condition content, tobacco product, pharmacotherapy regimen, abstinence definition, biochemical verification, and follow-up length. None could be evaluated with the supplied study-level information.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an **intercept of 0.7799** with **p=0.2362**. This was not statistically significant and therefore provided no strong evidence of small-study-effect asymmetry. However, with only five studies, Egger’s test has limited power and its nonsignificance should not be interpreted as evidence that publication bias is absent.

Trim-and-fill estimated **one potentially missing study** (**k₀=1**) and returned an adjusted value recorded as **0.325** in the supplied `trim_fill_g` field. Because that diagnostic value was not labeled as an Odds Ratio and its scale was not confirmed, it is not interpreted as a corrected OR and is not directly compared with the native pooled OR.

Taken together, the supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **moderate**: Egger’s test was nonsignificant, but trim-and-fill suggested a potentially missing result. Trim-and-fill is an exploratory method that assumes funnel asymmetry arises from missing studies; heterogeneity or other study differences can produce similar patterns.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This random-effects synthesis of five effect estimates from 1,610 participants produced a pooled **Odds Ratio (OR) of 1.5431663998170715**, with a wide **95% CI from 0.855 to 2.784** and **p=0.14963389689757495**. The analysis therefore did not demonstrate a statistically significant overall difference. Moreover, because the supplied favorability field designated the estimate as favoring neither arm and the counted event was not documented, the result cannot be given a reliable directional interpretation for cessation.

The pooled evidence was heterogeneous (**I²=55.58%; τ²=0.226875**), meaning that a single average result does not fully represent variation across studies. This variation is clinically plausible because the review question deliberately combines behavioural support delivered alone and alongside two distinct pharmacotherapies. Outcome definition, verification, follow-up, and support intensity may also differ across studies.

### Clinical interpretation

The analysis does not justify concluding that behavioural support either improves or fails to improve tobacco-use cessation. The interval estimate is compatible with materially different effects, and the unresolved event coding prevents determination of which arm an OR above 1 favors. Statistical nonsignificance should not be equated with equivalence or absence of benefit.

The matched-control framework is conceptually important. Comparisons of behavioural support plus pharmacotherapy against the same pharmacotherapy without intensive support are intended to isolate the added contribution of behavioural support. In contrast, comparisons of behavioural support alone against brief advice or no intervention estimate a somewhat different intervention contrast. Combining these comparisons can answer a broad question about the incremental effect of behavioural support, but subgroup coding is essential to determine whether that increment varies by pharmacotherapy context.

### Implications for research

A definitive update should recover study-level cessation event counts and confirm whether each event denotes abstinence or continued tobacco use. Each comparison should then be assigned to one of the three prespecified intervention combinations. Continuous or prolonged abstinence should be prioritized over point prevalence, biochemical verification over self-report, and a common clinically meaningful follow-up should be considered where available. Subgroup estimates and a formal test of subgroup differences should be reported alongside the overall random-effects result.

Future reports should fully describe behavioural-support content and intensity, pharmacotherapy adherence, control exposure, loss to follow-up, and handling of participants with missing cessation outcomes. Prospective protocol registration and complete reporting of the search and risk-of-bias process would improve transparency.

## Data Quality & Interpretive Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` object was supplied, several limitations in the available analytic record require explicit caution:

- Seven studies were identified, but only five effect sizes were pooled; reasons for exclusion of the other two studies were not provided.
- Event coding for the OR was unavailable, leaving the direction of benefit unresolved. The supplied favorability classification was therefore retained as **neither arm**.
- Individual standardized effect fields and the pooled native OR were both supplied, but the conversion relationship was not documented. They were not treated as the same scale.
- Intervention-combination assignments were absent, preventing the required three-way subgroup analysis.
- Outcome definition, biochemical verification, and follow-up duration could not be classified at study level.
- No study-level risk-of-bias judgments were available.

These are limitations of the supplied evidence record and not evidence that the underlying studies themselves failed to report all such information.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations.

First, the search and screening process was insufficiently documented to assess completeness or reproducibility. The absence of database details, dates, search strategies, screening counts, and exclusion reasons prevents full PRISMA verification and leaves susceptibility to selection bias uncertain.

Second, only five of seven studies contributed to the synthesis, with no explanation for the two noncontributing studies. Selective availability of results could bias the estimate.

Third, the principal planned subgroup analysis could not be conducted. Pooling across behavioural support alone, support plus NRT, and support plus varenicline without subgroup estimates obscures possible effect modification and falls short of the intended comparative structure.

Fourth, the abstinence endpoint could not be standardized or hierarchically selected from the information supplied. Differences in continuous versus point-prevalence abstinence, biochemical verification, and follow-up duration may contribute to heterogeneity and complicate clinical interpretation.

Fifth, event coding was not provided. As a result, the pooled OR’s direction cannot be translated into a conclusion about increased cessation, and the supplied designation of favoring neither arm must govern interpretation.

Sixth, the evidence base was small. Estimates of τ², I², Egger’s regression, and trim-and-fill are unstable with five studies. Publication-bias diagnostics are therefore exploratory rather than conclusive.

Finally, no study-level risk-of-bias assessment or certainty-of-evidence evaluation was available. The confidence interval reflects sampling uncertainty but does not account for potential trial bias, indirectness, inconsistent endpoint selection, or publication bias. A GRADE certainty rating cannot be assigned reliably from the supplied data.

## Conclusion

Across five effect estimates and 1,610 participants, the REML random-effects model produced a pooled **Odds Ratio (OR) of 1.5431663998170715 (95% CI 0.855 to 2.784; p=0.14963389689757495)**. Heterogeneity was moderate to substantial (**I²=55.58%; τ²=0.226875**). The supplied analysis classified the result as favoring neither arm because favorability depended on unresolved event coding. Publication-bias risk was considered moderate.

The available results therefore do not establish a clear overall effect of behavioural support on tobacco-use cessation. Interpretation is constrained by imprecision, heterogeneity, missing event-direction information, absence of subgroup assignments, and incomplete review-method documentation. A study-level reanalysis that confirms cessation coding and reports behavioural support alone, behavioural support plus NRT, and behavioural support plus varenicline as prespecified subgroups is necessary for a clinically interpretable answer.
