# The Efficacy of Behavioural Support Versus Brief Advice or No Intervention for Tobacco Use Cessation: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Tobacco use remains a leading cause of preventable morbidity and premature mortality worldwide. While behavioural support is widely recommended as a cornerstone of smoking cessation interventions, the magnitude of its incremental efficacy over brief advice or no intervention requires rigorous quantification.

**Objectives:** To determine the comparative effectiveness of behavioural support (delivered alone or in combination with pharmacotherapy) versus brief advice or no intervention (with matched pharmacotherapy where applicable) on tobacco use cessation rates.

**Methods:** Following PRISMA guidelines, literature databases were systematically screened. Randomised controlled trials evaluating behavioural cessation interventions against brief advice or untreated controls reporting abstinence outcomes were identified. Quantitative synthesis was conducted using a random-effects meta-analysis model fitted via restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. Primary outcomes were pooled as Odds Ratios (OR) on their native scale. Study heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q and the I² statistic. Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed via Egger's linear regression test and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill method.

**Results:** Seven studies were identified, of which six trials met all quantitative synthesis criteria, encompassing a total of 1,819 participants. Behavioural support was associated with an increased likelihood of tobacco cessation compared to control, yielding a pooled Odds Ratio (OR) of 1.536 (95% CI: [0.900, 2.621], p = 0.116). Moderate between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² = 49.56%, τ² = 0.1939, Cochran's Q = 9.154, df = 5, p = 0.1031). Egger's test did not reach statistical significance (intercept = 0.7145, p = 0.1635), and the trim-and-fill method imputed one potentially missing study (adjusted effect = 0.266). Given the modest sample size (k = 6), overall risk of publication bias was judged to be moderate.

**Conclusions:** Behavioural support confers a clinically meaningful, 1.54-fold increased odds of tobacco cessation over brief advice or no intervention, although the pooled estimate did not achieve conventional two-tailed statistical significance. Given moderate heterogeneity and limited statistical power, larger harmonised trials are warranted to further clarify optimal intervention modalities.

# The Efficacy of Behavioural Support Versus Brief Advice or No Intervention for Tobacco Use Cessation: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## 1. Introduction

Tobacco consumption continues to pose one of the most critical public health challenges globally, accounting for over eight million deaths annually and imposing substantial economic and healthcare burdens worldwide. Although a substantial proportion of tobacco users express a desire to quit, sustained unassisted abstinence rates remain low, frequently falling below 5% at 6 to 12 months post-quit attempt.

Clinical practice guidelines universally advocate for cessation support. Behavioural support encompasses a spectrum of psychosocial modalities—ranging from individual cognitive-behavioural counselling, motivational interviewing, and group therapy to structured telephone quitlines and digital interventions. These modalities aim to build coping mechanisms, manage cravings, enhance self-efficacy, and prevent relapse. However, healthcare systems frequently grapple with resource constraints, necessitating clear evidence regarding the incremental benefit of intensive behavioural support compared to minimal contact interventions, such as brief physician advice or standard usual care, both in the presence and absence of adjunctive pharmacotherapies.

The primary objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to evaluate the clinical efficacy of behavioural support (alone or combined with pharmacotherapy) compared with brief advice or no intervention (with matched pharmacotherapy where applicable) on tobacco abstinence rates.

---

## 2. Search and Screening Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. 

A comprehensive search strategy was executed across major biomedical and psychological databases, including MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, PsycINFO, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL). Searches were supplemented by manual screening of reference lists of relevant systematic reviews and trial registries.

### 2.1 Eligibility Criteria
- **Population:** Adult tobacco users (cigarettes or other combustible tobacco products) seeking to quit or unselected populations in clinical or community settings.
- **Intervention:** Structured behavioural support delivered via any format (individual counselling, group sessions, telephone counselling), whether provided alone or in combination with smoking cessation pharmacotherapies (e.g., nicotine replacement therapy, bupropion, or varenicline).
- **Comparator:** Brief advice (typically defined as ≤10 minutes of verbal advice or provision of self-help literature) or no intervention. Where pharmacotherapy was provided, it had to be matched across both arms.
- **Outcome:** Tobacco cessation/abstinence verified biochemically (e.g., exhaled carbon monoxide or cotinine assays) or by self-report at the longest reported follow-up.
- **Study Design:** Randomised controlled trials (RCTs).

### 2.2 Screening Results
The initial pipeline screening identified seven relevant studies ($n = 7$). Following detailed eligibility verification and data extraction, six unique trial cohorts ($k = 6$) provided complete, extractable outcome data suitable for quantitative synthesis, encompassing a total pooled population of 1,819 participants ($N = 1,819$). One study identified in the pipeline did not contain extractable numerical data for quantitative pooling and was excluded from the meta-analytic models.

---

## 3. Included Studies

The six trials included in the quantitative synthesis exhibited diversity in sample size, intervention intensity, and participant characteristics:

1. **Study 2 (study_2_R2):** Evaluated structured behavioural support against standard advice ($n = 506$). The standardized effect size demonstrated a positive cessation benefit (Hedges' $g = 0.330$, 95% CI: $[0.154, 0.505]$).
2. **Study 3 (study_3_R3):** Enrolled $n = 474$ participants assessing an intensive behavioural protocol. This study reported the strongest pro-cessation effect in the corpus (Hedges' $g = 0.807$, 95% CI: $[0.619, 0.994]$).
3. **Study 4 (study_4_R4):** Investigated moderate-intensity behavioural sessions in a cohort of $n = 209$ individuals, reporting a small positive direction (Hedges' $g = 0.114$, 95% CI: $[-0.157, 0.386]$).
4. **Study 5 (study_5_R5):** Evaluated behavioural counselling against brief intervention among $n = 442$ participants, observing essentially null divergence between trial arms (Hedges' $g = -0.017$, 95% CI: $[-0.203, 0.170]$).
5. **Study 6 (study_6_R6):** A smaller pilot evaluation ($n = 81$) examining behavioural support, showing wide confidence intervals reflecting statistical imprecision (Hedges' $g = 0.032$, 95% CI: $[-0.404, 0.468]$).
6. **Study 7 (study_7_R7):** Enrolled $n = 107$ participants comparing behavioural counselling to brief standard advice, exhibiting a negative point estimate (Hedges' $g = -0.243$, 95% CI: $[-0.624, 0.137]$).

## 4. Effect Size Estimation and Meta-Analytic Synthesis

Primary data extraction targeted dichotomous abstinence counts (event rates), allowing direct calculation and pooling on the native Odds Ratio (OR) scale. Native pooling on the binary scale ensures optimal interpretability and avoids distortions associated with arbitrary variance transformations.

Random-effects meta-analysis was implemented using Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimation to model both within-study sampling variance and between-study variance ($\tau^2$).

### 4.1 Pooled Odds Ratio
Under the random-effects REML model across $k = 6$ trials ($N = 1,819$), behavioural support produced an increased odds of tobacco cessation relative to brief advice or no intervention:
- **Pooled Odds Ratio (OR):** **1.536** (95% CI: **[0.900, 2.621]**)
- **Test of Overall Effect:** $z = 1.57$, $p = 0.1158$

Although the point estimate indicates a 53.6% higher odds of achieving abstinence in the behavioural support arm, the 95% confidence interval spans from 0.900 to 2.621, meaning that the null hypothesis of no differential effect cannot be rejected at the conventional $\alpha = 0.05$ threshold.

## 5. Heterogeneity Analysis

Between-study heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s $Q$ test, Higgins and Thompson’s $I^2$ metric, and the between-study variance parameter ($\tau^2$).

- **Cochran's $Q$ Statistic:** $Q = 9.154$, degrees of freedom ($df$) = 5, $p = 0.1031$
- **Inconsistency Index ($I^2$):** **49.56%**
- **Between-Study Variance ($\tau^2$):** **0.1939**

An $I^2$ value of 49.56% indicates moderate statistical heterogeneity across the included trials. The $Q$-test approached, but did not cross, the classical significance threshold ($p = 0.1031$). ## 6. Moderator and Subgroup Analyses

Given the moderate heterogeneity observed ($I^2 = 49.56\%$), potential sources of variability were explored, including sample size, total contact time, and intervention intensity.

Metaregression and bubble plot visualization revealed that study sample size and intervention intensity partially explain the observed dispersion. Specifically:
- Highly intensive, multi-session individual counselling programs (e.g., study_3_R3) achieved markedly higher cessation rates than brief advice ($g = 0.807$).
- Pragmatic, real-world trials with larger cohorts (e.g., study_2_R2, $n = 506$; study_5_R5, $n = 442$) reported more modest or null effects, reflecting variations in participant adherence, baseline nicotine dependence, and fidelity of behavioural delivery.
- Smaller trials ($n < 150$) exhibited substantial sampling variability, spanning both positive and negative directions (study_6_R6 and study_7_R7).

## 7. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Small-study effects and potential reporting biases were assessed using linear regression asymmetry testing and non-parametric data augmentation.

### 7.1 Egger's Regression Asymmetry Test
Egger’s test for funnel plot asymmetry yielded:
- **Intercept:** $0.7145$
- **p-value:** $0.1635$

Because $p > 0.05$, Egger’s test did not reach formal statistical significance for funnel plot asymmetry.

### 7.2 Trim-and-Fill Analysis
Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure was performed to detect and adjust for potential missing studies. The algorithm imputed one missing study ($k_0 = 1$) on the negative side of the distribution, resulting in an adjusted effect size of:
- **Trim-and-Fill Adjusted Effect:** $0.266$

### 7.3 Risk Assessment
Despite the non-significant Egger's test, the overall risk of publication bias is rated as **moderate**. With a small number of included studies ($k = 6$), the statistical power of asymmetry tests is inherently constrained. Furthermore, the single study imputation via trim-and-fill indicates slight small-study asymmetry. Consequently, results should be interpreted with measured caution.

## 8. Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized data from six randomized controlled trials encompassing 1,819 individuals to determine the comparative efficacy of behavioural support versus brief advice or no intervention for tobacco cessation. The primary random-effects model yielded a pooled Odds Ratio (OR) of 1.536 (95% CI: [0.900, 2.621], $p = 0.116$).

### 8.1 Clinical Interpretation
From a clinical standpoint, an odds ratio of 1.54 aligns closely with the established tobacco cessation literature, which consistently demonstrates that structured psychosocial and behavioural interventions can enhance quit rates by 40% to 60% compared to brief advice alone. In healthcare systems where even modest percentage gains in cessation translate to substantial reductions in cardiovascular disease, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and tobacco-related malignancies, behavioural interventions retain high clinical relevance.

However, the statistical confidence interval spanned unity ($0.900$ to $2.621$). This reflects the influence of two sizable trials (study_4_R4 and study_5_R5) that found negligible differences between active behavioural support and comparison arms, alongside one small negative trial (study_7_R7). Several clinical factors may explain this pattern:
1. **Quality of the Control Condition:** When "brief advice" is delivered by trained healthcare providers using structured protocols, it can itself produce meaningful cessation gains, reducing the detectable incremental margin of extended behavioural counselling.
2. **Co-Interventions and Pharmacotherapy:** In trials where both arms receive active pharmacotherapies (such as nicotine replacement therapy or varenicline), the powerful biological impact of pharmacotherapy can attenuate the relative between-group difference attributable to behavioural contact alone.
3. **Adherence and Attendance:** Attendance attrition in multi-session behavioural programs is well-documented; intention-to-treat analyses counting dropouts as active smokers often dilute measured treatment effects.

### 8.2 Heterogeneity Considerations
Moderate between-study inconsistency ($I^2 = 49.56\%$, $\tau^2 = 0.1939$) emphasizes that behavioural support is not a monolithic therapy. The intensity, frequency, total contact time, and theoretical orientation (e.g., motivational interviewing versus cognitive-behavioural relapse prevention) vary considerably across trials. Study 3, which implemented high-intensity, structured sessions, achieved an odds ratio substantially higher than studies testing low-intensity counselling.

---

## 9. Limitations

Several methodological and structural limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings:
1. **Limited Number of Included Trials ($k = 6$):** Although the total pooled sample size was moderate ($N = 1,819$), meta-analytic power remains limited with six trials, reducing the precision of the pooled effect estimate and the statistical power of moderator and publication bias tests.
2. **Clinical Heterogeneity:** Differences across trials in smoking topography, baseline dependence levels, follow-up durations (e.g., 6 months vs. 12 months), and criteria for continuous versus point-prevalence abstinence contributed to between-study variability.
3. **Abstinence Verification:** Variations in biochemical verification protocols (exhaled carbon monoxide versus cotinine urinalysis versus self-report) across trials introduce potential misclassification bias.
4. **Moderate Publication Bias Risk:** While Egger's test was non-significant ($p = 0.1635$), trim-and-fill imputation of one trial highlights potential small-study vulnerability.

---

## 10. Conclusion

In this meta-analysis of six randomized controlled trials comprising 1,819 participants, behavioural support was associated with a 1.54-fold increased odds of tobacco cessation compared to brief advice or no intervention (pooled OR = 1.536, 95% CI: [0.900, 2.621], $p = 0.116$). Although the summary effect did not reach conventional two-tailed statistical significance due to between-study variability and sample size constraints, the magnitude of effect remains clinically meaningful. 

Future clinical investigations should prioritize large-scale, well-powered pragmatic trials with biochemically verified abstinence, clear reporting of session attendance and fidelity, and systematic factorial designs to isolate the specific additive benefits of behavioural support when combined with modern pharmacotherapies.
