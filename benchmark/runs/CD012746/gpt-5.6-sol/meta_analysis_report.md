# Personalised Versus Standard Parenting or Family Interventions for Child Conduct Problems: A Random-Effects Meta-analysis of Mother-Reported ECBI Problem Scores

## Abstract

**Background:** Personalisation may improve the effects of parenting or family interventions for children with conduct problems, but its incremental benefit over the standard version of the same active intervention is uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate the end-of-treatment effect of a personalised parenting or family intervention, compared with its standard non-personalised version, on mother-reported Eyberg Child Behavior Inventory (ECBI) Problem scale scores.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons evaluated an individually tailored or subgroup-adapted parenting or family intervention against the standard non-personalised version of the same active intervention. The prespecified outcome was the mother-reported ECBI Problem scale at the first assessment after treatment ended. Post-treatment arm-level means were pooled using a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model. The effect was the raw mean difference (MD), defined as personalised minus standard; negative values favour personalisation.

**Results:** The supplied evidence set contained six studies, of which five contributed effect estimates, representing 220 participants. The pooled MD was -3.534258 ECBI Problem points (95% CI -7.301 to 0.232; p = 0.065899). The point estimate favoured the personalised intervention, but the confidence interval included no difference. Heterogeneity was moderate to substantial (I² = 59.26%; τ² = 10.453216; Q = 9.745, p = 0.045; k = 5). Egger’s intercept was 0.2178 (p = 0.3696), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0). These diagnostics did not identify funnel-plot asymmetry, although their power was limited by the small number of studies.

**Conclusions:** Personalisation was associated with an estimated 3.53-point lower mother-reported ECBI Problem score immediately after treatment than the standard version of the same intervention. Statistical uncertainty and significant between-study heterogeneity preclude a firm conclusion that personalisation provides an incremental benefit. Better-reported, adequately powered head-to-head trials are needed.

## Introduction

Conduct problems in childhood are associated with substantial impairment for children, families, schools, and health and social-care systems. Parenting and family interventions are established approaches to reducing disruptive behaviour, but treatment response varies. Personalised interventions seek to address that variation by tailoring intervention content or delivery to an individual child or family, or by adapting a programme for a defined subgroup. Their incremental value should be assessed against the standard version of the same active intervention rather than against no treatment or usual care.

This meta-analysis addressed the following question: among children with conduct problems, what is the end-of-treatment effect of a personalised parenting or family intervention, compared with the standard non-personalised version of the same intervention, on mother-reported child conduct problems measured by the Eyberg Child Behavior Inventory (ECBI) Problem scale?

The target estimand was the raw post-treatment mean difference in ECBI Problem points, calculated as personalised minus standard non-personalised intervention. Because lower ECBI Problem scores indicate fewer behaviours perceived as problematic, a negative MD favours personalisation.

## Search and Screening

The eligibility framework required:

- **Population:** children with conduct problems;
- **Intervention:** a parenting or family intervention tailored to the individual or adapted to a subgroup;
- **Comparator:** the standard non-personalised version of the same active parenting or family intervention;
- **Outcome:** the mother-reported ECBI Problem scale;
- **Time point:** the first assessment after treatment ended; and
- **Data:** post-treatment mean, standard deviation, and randomised-arm sample size, with standard errors converted to standard deviations where necessary.

ECBI Intensity scores, other child-behaviour measures, parent outcomes, and later follow-up assessments were outside the prespecified outcome definition. Mother report was preferred when multiple reporters were available.

## Effect Sizes

The primary effect measure was the **Mean Difference (MD)** in post-treatment ECBI Problem scores, calculated as:

> mean in the personalised-intervention arm minus mean in the standard non-personalised-intervention arm.

Thus, MD < 0 favours the personalised intervention. Native-scale pooling was used, as explicitly requested. No 0–100 rescaling was applied because the instrument range was stated for only two of the five rows; the analysis retained values in the papers’ reported raw ECBI units.

A random-effects model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). This model allows the true intervention effect to vary across studies rather than assuming a single common effect. Statistical uncertainty was expressed using a 95% confidence interval and a two-sided p value.

## Meta-Analytic Result

Across five comparisons and 220 participants, the REML random-effects **pooled Mean Difference (MD)** was **-3.534258 ECBI Problem points** (95% CI **-7.301 to 0.232**; p = **0.06589907436222353**).

According to the prespecified direction and the analysis result’s recorded favouring field, the point estimate favoured the **personalised parenting or family intervention**. On average, the personalised groups had an estimated 3.53-point lower mother-reported ECBI Problem score at the end of treatment than groups receiving the standard non-personalised version. However, the 95% confidence interval crossed zero, ranging from a 7.301-point advantage for personalisation to a 0.232-point advantage for the standard intervention. The result therefore did not reach the conventional two-sided 0.05 threshold and does not establish a statistically conclusive incremental benefit.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was statistically significant and moderate to substantial:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 9.745 with 4 degrees of freedom;
- **Q-test p value:** 0.045;
- **I²:** 59.26%; and
- **τ²:** 10.453216 squared ECBI Problem points.

The I² estimate indicates that approximately 59% of the observed variation in effects was attributed to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error under the model. The τ² estimate quantifies the estimated between-study variance on the raw ECBI Problem scale. With only five studies, however, both heterogeneity estimates are uncertain, and the Q test has limited operating characteristics.

Potential sources of heterogeneity could include the basis for personalisation, subgroup characteristics, severity of conduct problems, intervention content or dose, delivery setting, and post-treatment assessment timing. The supplied data did not permit these explanations to be tested. A graphical heterogeneity summary is available at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## Moderators

With only five effect estimates, conventional meta-regression would in any event be underpowered and at substantial risk of unstable or spurious findings. ## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **0.2178** (p = **0.3696**). Trim-and-fill estimated **no missing studies** (k₀ = **0**) and returned an adjusted standardized value of **-0.3708**. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as low.

These analyses did not identify evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Nevertheless, absence of detected asymmetry should not be interpreted as proof that reporting bias is absent. With only five studies, Egger’s test and trim-and-fill have very limited ability to identify asymmetry, and trim-and-fill can be unreliable in the presence of heterogeneity. In addition, the trim-and-fill value is on a standardized-effect scale and must not be interpreted as an adjusted raw ECBI Problem-point MD or compared directly with the primary pooled MD.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This random-effects synthesis estimated that personalised parenting or family interventions reduced mother-reported ECBI Problem scores by **3.534258 points more** at the end of treatment than the standard non-personalised versions of the same active interventions. The point estimate favoured personalisation, but the confidence interval included no effect and the p value was 0.06589907436222353. The evidence therefore suggests a possible incremental benefit but remains statistically inconclusive.

The active-comparator design is an important feature of the question. The estimated contrast does not represent the effect of parenting or family treatment versus no intervention. Instead, it isolates, as far as the included trials permit, the added effect of personalising an intervention relative to providing its standard form.

### Interpretation of magnitude and certainty

The pooled effect is expressed directly in ECBI Problem points, which preserves clinical interpretability better than a standardized effect when studies use the same instrument. However, a minimal clinically important difference was not supplied. It is therefore not possible to determine from these results alone whether a 3.53-point average difference would be perceptible or clinically important to children and families.

The confidence interval is also clinically relevant: it includes both a potentially meaningful benefit for personalisation and a very small effect in favour of the standard intervention. Significant heterogeneity further indicates that the average effect may not describe all intervention adaptations or populations equally well.

### Comparison with study-level evidence

The supplied standardized study-level estimates varied in size, with one notably large negative estimate (study_2_R2, -1.286125; 95% CI -2.102 to -0.471) and four estimates whose confidence intervals included zero. These standardized values are not interchangeable with the raw MD used in the primary model. Nonetheless, their dispersion is qualitatively consistent with the observed heterogeneity and suggests that the effect of personalisation may differ among intervention contexts.

### Implications for practice and research

Current evidence does not justify a universal conclusion that personalised versions are superior to standard versions, but it also does not rule out a potentially useful incremental effect. Decisions to personalise should therefore consider implementation burden, cost, feasibility, family preferences, and whether the adaptation addresses a clearly identified barrier or need.

Future trials should prospectively specify the adaptation mechanism, retain a directly comparable standard-intervention arm, report arm-level post-treatment means and standard deviations, and provide mother-reported ECBI Problem and Intensity results separately. Adequate allocation concealment, blinded or otherwise independent outcome assessment where feasible, preregistration, complete reporting, and longer-term follow-up would strengthen the evidence. Individual-participant-data meta-analysis could test whether baseline characteristics identify children or families who benefit most from personalisation.

## Data Quality and Reporting Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` object was supplied, several limitations in the provided dataset require explicit notice:

1. The pipeline lists six studies but only five effect sizes; the identity of, and reason for excluding, the sixth study from pooling were not reported.
2. Arm-level post-treatment means, standard deviations, and sample sizes were not supplied, preventing independent verification of the raw MD calculations and any conversion from standard errors to standard deviations.
3. The study-level table contains standardized effects, while the primary synthesis is a native-scale MD. These are different metrics and cannot be directly combined or compared numerically.
4. The instrument range was documented for only two of five rows. No 0–100 rescaling was performed; the primary analysis retained the reported raw units.
5. Search, screening, study-characteristic, and risk-of-bias information was insufficient for a complete PRISMA flow account or certainty-of-evidence assessment.
6. The publication-bias trim-and-fill estimate was reported on a standardized scale rather than the raw MD scale used for the primary synthesis.

## Limitations

This report has several limitations. First, the synthesis included only five comparisons and 220 participants. Small meta-analyses produce imprecise pooled effects, unstable heterogeneity estimates, and underpowered assessments of publication bias.

Second, the absence of trial-level bibliographic and methodological details prevented evaluation of randomisation, allocation concealment, missing outcome data, selective reporting, adherence, intervention fidelity, and other risk-of-bias domains. No GRADE assessment could be completed from the supplied data.

Third, the significant heterogeneity indicates that the pooled average may obscure meaningful differences among adaptations, populations, or delivery contexts. No adequately supported moderator analysis was available to explain this variation.

Fourth, independent reproduction of the pooled native-scale estimate was not possible because arm-level means and standard deviations were omitted. The incomplete reporting of the instrument range also introduces ambiguity, although the analysis explicitly avoided unsupported rescaling.

Fifth, the analysis concerns only the mother-reported ECBI Problem scale at the first post-treatment assessment. Findings should not be generalised automatically to ECBI Intensity scores, other reporters, other behavioural instruments, parent outcomes, or durability of effects at later follow-up.

Finally, the conventional p = 0.05 threshold should not be treated as a binary indicator of whether personalisation works. The estimate, confidence interval, heterogeneity, study quality, and clinical importance all matter. The present evidence remains compatible with both a meaningful benefit and little or no incremental benefit.

## Conclusion

In five comparisons involving 220 participants, personalised parenting or family interventions produced an estimated **3.534258-point lower** mother-reported ECBI Problem score at the end of treatment than standard non-personalised versions of the same interventions. The random-effects pooled **Mean Difference (MD)** was **-3.534258** (95% CI **-7.301 to 0.232**; p = **0.06589907436222353**), with the point estimate favouring the personalised intervention. Between-study heterogeneity was significant (**I² = 59.26%; τ² = 10.453216; Q = 9.745, p = 0.045**).

The evidence suggests a possible advantage of personalisation but is not conclusive. The small evidence base, substantial heterogeneity, incomplete methodological reporting, and inability to verify arm-level data limit confidence in the estimate. Additional well-powered and transparently reported head-to-head trials are required to determine whether, how, and for whom personalisation improves outcomes beyond a standard active intervention.
