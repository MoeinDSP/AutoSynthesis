# Does Personalisation Add Value? A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomised Trials Comparing Tailored versus Standard Parenting and Family Interventions for Child Conduct Problems (ECBI Problem Scale, End of Treatment)

## Abstract

**Background.** Parenting and family interventions are first-line treatments for childhood conduct problems, and there is growing enthusiasm for personalising them — tailoring content, dose, delivery format, or cultural framing to the individual family or to a pre-specified subgroup. Whether personalisation adds incremental benefit over the standard version of the *same* active programme is an additive-design question that has rarely been synthesised.

**Objective.** To estimate the effect of a personalised, tailored, adapted, or subgroup-matched parenting/family intervention versus the standard non-personalised version of the same active intervention on child conduct problems at the first post-treatment assessment, measured with the Eyberg Child Behavior Inventory (ECBI) Problem scale (range 0–36; mother-report preferred).

**Methods.** We conducted a systematic search and screening pipeline for randomised trials with a head-to-head personalised-versus-standard comparison of the same active programme. Post-treatment means, SDs and per-arm sample sizes on the ECBI Problem scale were extracted (SE/CI converted to SD where required). ECBI Intensity scores, follow-up timepoints, alternative child behaviour scales and parent-level outcomes were excluded. Effects were pooled on the raw ECBI Problem metric as a mean difference (MD) using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Six studies were included in the review and five contributed extractable post-treatment ECBI Problem data (k = 5; N = 220 children). The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was **−3.53 ECBI Problem points (95% CI −7.301 to 0.232; p = 0.0659)**, a direction that **favours the personalised/tailored intervention** but which does not reach conventional statistical significance; the confidence interval includes no difference. Native-scale pooling was used, so this is a confirmed raw-unit ECBI Problem mean difference and not a standardized surrogate. Study-level standardized effects ranged from g = +0.069 (favouring standard care) to g = −1.286, and between-study heterogeneity was moderate and statistically significant (Q = 9.745, df = 4, p = 0.045; I² = 59.26%; τ² = 10.453216, i.e. τ ≈ 3.23 ECBI points). Egger's test showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept = 0.2178, p = 0.3696) and trim-and-fill imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized estimate g = −0.3708), supporting a low assessed risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** Across a small evidence base, personalised versions of parenting and family interventions produced ECBI Problem scores approximately 3.5 points lower than standard versions of the same programme at end of treatment — a clinically non-trivial direction of effect favouring personalisation, but one estimated with substantial imprecision, moderate heterogeneity, and a confidence interval compatible with both a large benefit and a small harm. The evidence is best described as *suggestive but inconclusive*; adequately powered additive-design trials reporting the ECBI Problem scale in raw units are needed.

## 1. Introduction

Conduct problems in early and middle childhood — persistent non-compliance, aggression, tantrums, defiance and rule-breaking — are among the most common reasons for referral to child mental health services and are robust precursors of later antisocial behaviour, academic failure, and service dependency. Behavioural parenting programmes and broader family interventions (e.g. Parent–Child Interaction Therapy, Incredible Years, Triple P, Parent Management Training variants, and multicomponent family therapies) constitute the best-established treatments for this presentation, and their average benefit against waitlist or treatment-as-usual is no longer seriously in question.

The live question has instead shifted from *whether* these programmes work to *for whom*, *in what form*, and *with what adjustments*. Attrition, non-response, and variable engagement — particularly among families facing socioeconomic adversity, parental mental health difficulties, minority-language or minority-culture contexts, or child comorbidity — have motivated a large family of **personalisation strategies**. These include (i) *tailoring*, in which module selection, session sequence, dose or pacing is driven by individual family assessment data; (ii) *cultural or contextual adaptation*, in which content, examples, and delivery are modified for a defined population; (iii) *subgroup matching*, in which families are allocated to the variant predicted to suit their baseline profile; and (iv) *augmentation*, in which standard content is supplemented with individually indicated components (e.g. an adjunctive parental depression or partner-support module).

Personalisation is intuitively attractive and is now embedded in many implementation frameworks. It is also costly: it increases assessment burden, requires more highly trained clinicians, complicates fidelity monitoring, and can dilute the active ingredients of a manualised programme. The decisive evidential test is therefore not the standard comparison against an inactive control — which personalised programmes will almost always win, as would the unmodified programme — but the **additive (dismantling-style) comparison against the standard non-personalised version of the same active intervention**. Only this design isolates the incremental value of personalisation itself from the value of the underlying programme.

This review addresses that question for a single, well-defined, clinically interpretable outcome: the **Eyberg Child Behavior Inventory (ECBI) Problem scale**. The ECBI is the most widely used parent-report measure in this literature. Its Problem scale counts how many of 36 listed child behaviours the parent currently endorses as "a problem for you" (range 0–36), and is conceptually distinct from the Intensity scale, which rates frequency on a 7-point scale (range 36–252). Because the two scales have incommensurable metrics and capture different constructs (parental problem appraisal versus behaviour frequency), they should not be pooled together; this review is restricted to the Problem scale, at the first post-treatment assessment, with mother-report preferred where multiple informants were available.

**Research question.** In randomised trials of parenting or family interventions for children with conduct problems, does a personalised (tailored, adapted, or subgroup-matched) version of an intervention reduce child conduct problems at end of treatment on the ECBI Problem scale, compared with the standard non-personalised version of the same active intervention?

**Direction of effect.** A *negative* mean difference on the ECBI Problem scale indicates fewer behaviours endorsed as problematic in the personalised arm and therefore **favours personalisation**. Readers should note that the raw statistical sign convention used throughout (treatment minus control) means that, for this outcome, the lower raw value is the better one.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

| Domain | Inclusion | Exclusion |
|---|---|---|
| **Design** | Randomised controlled trial (including cluster- and factorially-randomised designs) with a head-to-head comparison of two versions of the same active programme | Non-randomised, single-arm, quasi-experimental; comparisons against waitlist, no-treatment, or a *different* programme |
| **Population** | Children with conduct problems (clinically referred, screened-elevated, or diagnosed with ODD/CD/disruptive behaviour) and their parents/carers | Samples defined solely by ADHD, ASD, internalising problems, or developmental delay without a conduct-problem indication |
| **Intervention** | Personalised, tailored, individually adapted, culturally adapted, or subgroup-matched version of a parenting or family intervention | Entirely novel programmes with no standard comparator version |
| **Comparator** | Standard, generic, or non-personalised version of the *same* active parenting/family intervention | Inactive controls; active controls drawn from a different treatment family |
| **Outcome** | ECBI **Problem** scale at the **first post-treatment** assessment; mother-report preferred | ECBI **Intensity** scale; any follow-up timepoint; other child behaviour instruments (e.g. CBCL, SDQ, DPICS observational codes); parent-level outcomes (parenting stress, depression, discipline practices) |
| **Data** | Post-treatment mean, SD (or convertible SE/CI) and n per arm | Reports providing only change scores, medians/IQRs, or test statistics without dispersion information |

### 2.2 Information sources and screening flow

Records were identified through a structured database and citation search, deduplicated, and screened in two stages (title/abstract, then full text) against the criteria above. The screening pipeline retained **6 studies** as eligible for the review. Of these, **5 studies contributed an extractable post-treatment ECBI Problem effect size** and entered the quantitative synthesis (k = 5; N = 220 participants). The sixth eligible study was retained in the narrative review but could not be pooled because the required post-treatment mean/SD/n information for the ECBI Problem scale was not recoverable in a usable form (the common reasons in this literature being reporting of the Intensity scale only, reporting of adjusted change scores without dispersion, or reporting of follow-up rather than end-of-treatment values).

A PRISMA flow summary of the quantitative yield is therefore: 6 studies eligible → 5 studies, 5 independent effect sizes, 220 participants pooled. Note that the effect-size count (5) is one fewer than the study count (6); every pooled effect size corresponds to one distinct study, so no study contributed more than one estimate and no unit-of-analysis (double-counting) correction was required.

## 3. Included Studies

Five trials provided analysable end-of-treatment ECBI Problem data. Their standardized effect sizes, precision, and sample sizes are summarised below. All effects are expressed as Hedges' *g* at the study level for comparability across differently-scaled reports; the pooled synthesis (Section 5) was conducted on the **raw ECBI Problem metric**.

| Study ID | Hedges' *g* | 95% CI | n | Direction at study level |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | +0.0693 | [−0.594, 0.732] | 36 | Marginally favours standard version |
| study_2_R2 | −1.2861 | [−2.102, −0.471] | 28 | Strongly favours personalisation (statistically significant) |
| study_4_R4 | −0.1729 | [−0.565, 0.219] | 101 | Slightly favours personalisation |
| study_5_R5 | −0.3618 | [−1.384, 0.661] | 15 | Favours personalisation (very imprecise) |
| study_6_R6 | −0.4100 | [−1.037, 0.217] | 40 | Favours personalisation |

Several features of this evidence base shape the interpretation that follows.

**Small samples.** Per-study samples ranged from 15 to 101 participants, with a median of 36 and a total of 220. Four of the five trials enrolled fewer than 50 participants in total across both arms. Additive designs of this kind require substantially larger samples than programme-versus-waitlist designs, because the expected incremental effect of personalisation on top of an already-active programme is small relative to the between-family variability in response. All five trials are therefore materially underpowered for the comparison they attempt, and only study_4_R4 (n = 101) approaches a sample size at which a modest incremental effect could be detected with reasonable confidence.

**One markedly larger effect.** study_2_R2 (n = 28) reported a large and statistically significant advantage for the personalised arm (g = −1.286, 95% CI −2.102 to −0.471). This single estimate is both the largest in magnitude and among the least precisely estimated; its influence on the pooled result and on the heterogeneity statistics is examined in Sections 6 and 7.

**One null/reversed effect.** study_1_R1 (n = 36) is the only trial with a point estimate on the standard-care side of the line of no effect (g = +0.069), and its confidence interval is almost perfectly symmetric about zero — a result best read as "no detectable difference" rather than as evidence that personalisation is harmful.

**Consistency of the remaining three.** study_4_R4, study_5_R5 and study_6_R6 cluster in a narrow band of small-to-moderate benefit (g = −0.17 to −0.41), which is where the bulk of the information in this synthesis lies once the extreme estimate is set aside.

## 4. Effect Sizes and Analytic Approach

### 4.1 Effect measure

The pre-specified and analysed effect measure is the **Mean Difference (MD)** on the raw ECBI Problem scale (`measure_label`: "Mean Difference (MD)"; `effect_measure_source`: explicit user request). **Native-scale pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used` = true): the pooled value is a genuine raw-unit ECBI Problem mean difference, expressed in the papers' own units (number of behaviours endorsed as a problem, out of 36), and is **not** a standardized fallback or a back-transformed approximation. It must therefore **not** be described or cited as a Hedges' *g*. Study-level Hedges' *g* values are reported in Section 3 purely as a descriptive aid for comparing trials of differing scale-reporting conventions, and the trim-and-fill sensitivity estimate in Section 8 is also on the *g* metric; neither should be confused with the primary pooled MD.

One measurement caveat is attached to the primary analysis and is reported here for transparency. The instrument range (0–36) was explicitly stated in the source reports for only **3 of the 5** contributing rows. Because the range could not be verified for all studies, **no rescaling to a common 0–100 metric was applied**, and the MD was pooled in the papers' raw units as reported. This is the conservative choice — it avoids imposing an unverified transformation — but it carries the residual assumption that all five trials used the standard 36-item ECBI Problem scoring and not an abbreviated or locally modified variant. If any trial used a shortened form, its contribution to the pooled raw MD would be attenuated relative to the others. This issue is restated in the Data Quality & Validation Notes (Section 10).

### 4.2 Pooling model

Effects were combined with a **random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**. A random-effects specification is appropriate on substantive grounds: the included trials personalise different programmes, by different mechanisms (tailoring, adaptation, matching), in different populations, so a single common true effect is not a credible assumption. REML was preferred over DerSimonian–Laird because it is less downwardly biased in estimating τ² when k is small, though with k = 5 all between-study variance estimators remain unstable.

Heterogeneity was characterised using Cochran's Q (with its χ² p-value), the I² statistic (proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error), and τ² with its square root τ expressed in ECBI Problem points.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Results

### 5.1 Primary pooled estimate

The synthesis comprises a **single outcome/timepoint group** — ECBI Problem scale, first post-treatment assessment — so one pooled estimate is reported; no `meta_analysis_groups` with multiple entries were present, and no separate per-group results are applicable.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Mean Difference (MD)**, raw ECBI Problem points |
| Native-scale pooling confirmed | Yes |
| Studies (k) | 5 |
| Participants (N) | 220 |
| **Pooled MD** | **−3.534** ECBI Problem points |
| 95% confidence interval | **[−7.301, 0.232]** |
| p-value | **0.0659** |
| Favours | **Personalised / tailored or subgroup-adapted parenting or family intervention** |
| Model | Random effects, REML |
| τ² | 10.453216 (τ ≈ 3.23 points) |
| I² | 59.26% |
| Q (df = 4) | 9.745, p = 0.045 |

The pooled Mean Difference of **−3.534 points (95% CI −7.301 to 0.232; p = 0.0659)** indicates that, averaged across trials, children whose families received the personalised version of a parenting or family intervention were rated by their mothers as having approximately **3.5 fewer behaviours endorsed as "a problem"** at end of treatment than children whose families received the standard non-personalised version of the same programme. Per the `favors` field, the direction of this result **favours the personalised/tailored intervention**.

Two qualifications are essential. First, the result does **not** reach the conventional 5% significance threshold (p = 0.0659), and the 95% confidence interval **crosses zero**, extending from a substantial benefit of 7.3 points to a small disadvantage of 0.23 points. The data are therefore compatible with personalisation producing a large clinical gain, and also — though less compatibly — with it producing essentially nothing or a trivially worse outcome. Second, the interval is extremely wide, spanning roughly 7.5 ECBI Problem points on a 36-point scale; this is a direct consequence of a total sample of 220 participants spread across five small trials with moderate heterogeneity.

### 5.2 Clinical interpretation of magnitude

To put −3.5 points in context: the ECBI Problem scale has a clinical cut-off conventionally set at 15, and typical treated samples in this literature move from the high teens/low twenties pre-treatment to the low teens post-treatment. A 3.5-point incremental difference, if real, would represent a meaningful fraction of the total change typically achieved by a parenting programme — roughly the difference between a child whose parent endorses 14 problem behaviours and one who endorses 10 or 11. It would also plausibly be large enough to shift a proportion of families across the clinical cut-off. The clinical question is therefore not whether 3.5 points would matter — it would — but whether the point estimate is trustworthy given the width of the interval and the influence of a single trial (Section 6).

A consistency check against the standardized metric is informative. The trim-and-fill procedure (Section 8) returned an adjusted standardized estimate of **g = −0.3708**, and three of the five study-level *g* values fall between −0.17 and −0.41. A *g* of roughly −0.37 corresponds, for plausible ECBI Problem standard deviations of 6–9 points in treated samples, to a raw difference of approximately 2.2–3.3 points. The primary raw MD of −3.53 therefore sits at or slightly above the upper end of what the standardized evidence implies, consistent with the raw-scale pooling being somewhat more influenced by the large, imprecise study_2_R2 estimate than the standardized pooling is.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **moderate and statistically significant**:

- **Cochran's Q = 9.745** on **df = 4**, **p = 0.045** — the null hypothesis of a common underlying effect is rejected at the 5% level. With only five studies, Q has low power, so a significant result here indicates heterogeneity that is difficult to attribute to chance.
- **I² = 59.26%** — approximately 59% of the observed variability in effects reflects genuine between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. By the conventional Cochrane bands this sits in the "may represent substantial heterogeneity" region (50–90%), at its lower end. I² itself is estimated imprecisely when k = 5 and its confidence interval (not computable reliably here) would be very wide, plausibly spanning from near-zero to upwards of 85%.
- **τ² = 10.453216**, i.e. **τ ≈ 3.23 ECBI Problem points** — the estimated standard deviation of true effects across trials. This is of the same order as the pooled effect itself (−3.53), meaning the dispersion of true effects is roughly as large as their average. Practically, this implies that the true incremental benefit of personalisation plausibly ranges from strongly positive in some settings to null or slightly negative in others.

**Approximate prediction interval.** Combining τ² with the standard error of the pooled estimate (SE ≈ 1.92, derived from the reported 95% CI) and using a t-distribution with k − 2 = 3 degrees of freedom gives an approximate 95% prediction interval of roughly **−15.5 to +8.4 ECBI Problem points** for the true effect in a new, comparable trial. This interval is extremely wide and straddles zero generously; it should be treated as indicative only, since prediction intervals estimated from five studies are notoriously unstable. Its substantive message, however, is unambiguous: the present evidence cannot tell a clinician or commissioner what effect personalisation would have in their own service.

**Sources of heterogeneity.** The dominant contributor is almost certainly study_2_R2 (g = −1.286), which lies well outside the confidence intervals of the other four trials. Its removal would be expected to reduce I² substantially and to shrink the pooled MD toward the −2 to −3 point region, while narrowing the interval. A formal leave-one-out analysis was not part of the pre-specified pipeline output and is recommended for any update of this review. Beyond this single estimate, plausible substantive sources of heterogeneity include: the *type* of personalisation (individual tailoring versus population-level cultural adaptation versus subgroup matching), the *intensity* of the personalisation contrast (some "standard" arms in this literature already permit considerable clinician discretion, narrowing the true contrast), the underlying programme, baseline severity, and informant/assessment procedures.

## 7. Moderator Analyses

**No formal moderator analysis or meta-regression was conducted, and none can be defensibly conducted on this dataset.** With k = 5 studies and significant residual heterogeneity, even a single-covariate meta-regression would have fewer than the commonly recommended minimum of ~10 studies per covariate; estimates would be driven by individual trials and the risk of spurious findings would be high. Subgroup analysis by personalisation type, programme, or population is likewise not feasible, since most candidate subgroups would contain one or two studies.

Three descriptive observations are offered in lieu of formal moderation, and should be read as hypothesis-generating only:

1. **Precision and effect magnitude.** The largest trial (study_4_R4, n = 101) reported the smallest non-zero benefit (g = −0.173), while the second-smallest trial (study_2_R2, n = 28) reported the largest (g = −1.286). This inverse pattern between sample size and effect magnitude is the classic signature of small-study effects — although, as Section 8 reports, the formal tests did not detect asymmetry, and with k = 5 such tests are nearly powerless. The relationship is visualised in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, where bubble area encodes precision.
2. **Direction consistency.** Four of five trials point in the direction favouring personalisation, and the single exception (study_1_R1, g = +0.069) is effectively null rather than contrary. Directional consistency of 4/5 is weak evidence on its own (the probability of 4+ in one direction under a null is not small with k = 5) but is at least not inconsistent with a modest true benefit.
3. **Stability over accumulation.** The cumulative plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) should be consulted to judge whether the pooled estimate has converged. Given the width of the final interval, it has not.

Any future update with a larger study base should pre-specify moderators for: type of personalisation, strength of the personalisation contrast (i.e. how constrained the standard arm actually was), baseline ECBI severity, programme identity, dose, and risk-of-bias domain ratings.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Two complementary assessments were performed.

- **Egger's regression test for funnel plot asymmetry:** intercept = **0.2178**, **p = 0.3696**. There is no statistically significant relationship between effect size and study precision, so no detectable small-study effect.
- **Duval & Tweedie trim-and-fill:** **k₀ = 0 studies imputed**; adjusted standardized estimate **g = −0.3708**. Because no studies were imputed, the funnel plot is already symmetric under this method's assumptions and the pooled estimate requires no adjustment for potentially suppressed results. (The adjusted value is reported on the standardized *g* metric and is not directly comparable to the primary raw-unit MD; it is noted here as a convergent sensitivity check, and is consistent with the three mid-range study-level *g* values.)

**Overall assessed risk of publication bias: low.**

**Caveat (as flagged in the pipeline interpretation).** The absence of evidence is not strong evidence of absence. Both Egger's test and trim-and-fill have very limited statistical power when the number of included studies is small — conventionally fewer than 10, and here there are only 5 — and both perform poorly in the presence of genuine between-study heterogeneity, which is present (I² = 59.26%). The appropriate conclusion is therefore **"no publication bias detected"**, not "no publication bias present". A further, non-statistical consideration applies with particular force to additive designs: trials finding that an expensive personalisation protocol confers no advantage over the standard programme may be less likely to be written up and published than those finding an advantage, and this form of selective reporting would be invisible to funnel-based methods applied to so few studies.

## 9. Discussion

This meta-analysis asked a focused and practically consequential question: once a family is already receiving an evidence-based parenting or family intervention, does personalising that intervention produce additional reduction in child conduct problems at end of treatment?

The answer, on the available evidence, is **probably yes but not demonstrably so**. The pooled Mean Difference of **−3.534 ECBI Problem points (95% CI −7.301 to 0.232; p = 0.0659)** favours the personalised/tailored intervention, is clinically meaningful in magnitude, and is supported by directional consistency across four of five trials and by a convergent standardized sensitivity estimate near g = −0.37. But it fails the conventional significance threshold, its interval includes the null, its heterogeneity is significant (Q = 9.745, p = 0.045; I² = 59.26%; τ ≈ 3.23 points), and the entire evidence base rests on 220 participants in five underpowered trials — one of which (study_2_R2, n = 28, g = −1.286) exerts disproportionate leverage on both the point estimate and the dispersion.

Several substantive interpretations are consistent with these data.

**(a) A modest true benefit, under-detected.** The most parsimonious reading is that personalisation confers a small-to-moderate incremental benefit (plausibly in the region of 2–3 raw ECBI Problem points, or g ≈ −0.2 to −0.4) and that the present trials are individually and collectively too small to establish it. On this reading, the formally non-significant p-value reflects a power failure rather than a null effect, and the appropriate response is larger trials rather than abandonment of the hypothesis.

**(b) Heterogeneous benefit conditional on context.** With τ of the same order as the mean effect, it is equally plausible that personalisation works well in some circumstances — e.g. where the standard programme is poorly matched to the population, where engagement barriers are severe, or where comorbid parental difficulties require augmentation — and adds little where the standard programme already fits. This interpretation is attractive but, with k = 5, untestable here.

**(c) A diluted contrast.** A recurring methodological problem in additive designs is that the "standard" comparator is rarely as standardised as the label implies. Clinicians delivering manualised parenting programmes routinely exercise judgement about pacing, emphasis and examples, which is itself a form of informal personalisation. To the extent that control arms contained such informal tailoring, the true contrast between arms is smaller than the design nominally specifies, biasing observed effects toward the null. This mechanism would tend to make genuine personalisation effects *harder* to detect, and is a further argument against reading the non-significant result as evidence of no effect.

**(d) Inflation by a single trial.** It must also be acknowledged that if study_2_R2 is an outlier produced by chance, baseline imbalance, or small-sample volatility, the true effect may be closer to the −0.17 to −0.41 *g* band of the other four trials, i.e. toward the lower end of clinical importance.

**Relation to the wider literature.** These findings are broadly concordant with the general pattern in dismantling and augmentation research on psychosocial interventions, where incremental components typically yield small effects relative to the main programme, and where additive trials are chronically underpowered. They are also concordant with cultural-adaptation meta-analyses, which generally report modest and heterogeneous advantages for adapted over generic programmes. The present analysis adds specificity by restricting to one scale (ECBI Problem), one timepoint (end of treatment), one informant preference (mother), and one design (same-programme head-to-head), thereby avoiding the construct and timepoint mixing that inflates heterogeneity in broader syntheses — at the predictable cost of a very small k.

**Implications for practice.** The present evidence does not justify a general recommendation that services invest in resource-intensive personalisation protocols on the expectation of better child conduct outcomes at end of treatment; nor does it justify dismissing personalisation. Where personalisation is adopted for other legitimate reasons — engagement, acceptability, equity of access for underserved populations, reduction of attrition — the current data give no reason to fear that it compromises conduct-problem outcomes, and some reason to hope it improves them.

**Implications for research.** The field needs (i) adequately powered additive trials, sized for an incremental effect of roughly g = 0.3 rather than for the much larger programme-versus-waitlist effect; (ii) explicit, auditable specification of what is personalised and how, so that personalisation type can be coded as a moderator; (iii) active constraint and documentation of the standard arm so that the contrast is not diluted; (iv) consistent reporting of **both** ECBI subscales in raw units with explicit statement of the instrument version and range; and (v) routine reporting of post-treatment means and SDs per arm, not only adjusted change scores, so that trials remain poolable.

## 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-handling and validation issues arose and are reported plainly.

1. **Effect measure provenance.** The effect measure was the **Mean Difference (MD)** on the raw ECBI Problem scale, set by explicit user specification (`effect_measure_source`: explicit_user_request). **Native-scale pooling was used and confirmed** (`native_pooling_used` = true), so the pooled value of −3.534 is a genuine raw-unit ECBI Problem mean difference, not a standardized fallback or an approximation. It should not be labelled or cited as Hedges' *g*.
2. **Incomplete instrument-range verification (recorded conflict).** The instrument range (0–36) was explicitly stated in only **3 of the 5** contributing study rows. Consequently **no rescaling to a 0–100 common metric was applied**, and the MD was pooled in the papers' raw units. This conservative choice avoids an unverified transformation, but leaves a residual assumption that all five trials used the standard 36-item ECBI Problem scoring. If any trial used an abbreviated or locally modified form, its raw-scale contribution would not be strictly commensurable with the others, and the pooled MD would be correspondingly distorted.
3. **Study count versus effect-size count.** Six studies were eligible for the review but only **five** yielded an extractable post-treatment ECBI Problem effect size; the sixth is described narratively but contributed no data to the pooled estimate. Readers should note that the headline k = 5 is smaller than the number of eligible studies.
4. **No double-counting.** Each pooled effect size derives from a distinct study; no study contributed multiple estimates, so no unit-of-analysis correction or within-study aggregation was necessary.
5. **Single outcome group.** All five effect sizes pertain to one outcome and one timepoint (ECBI Problem scale, first post-treatment assessment). No multi-group synthesis was required, and no analysis was flagged as having insufficient data for pooling.
6. **Mixed metrics within the report.** Study-level effects and the trim-and-fill adjusted estimate are expressed as Hedges' *g*; the primary pooled estimate is a raw-unit MD. These two metrics are reported side by side deliberately as a cross-check, but they are not interchangeable and should not be compared numerically without conversion.
7. **Statistical caveats attached to small k.** τ², I², Egger's test and trim-and-fill are all estimated on five studies and are correspondingly unstable; the prediction interval reported in Section 6 is an approximation and should be treated as indicative only.

No further validation flags (e.g. reviews or duplicates excluded, unclassifiable outcomes, or effect measures requiring separate pooling) were raised by the pipeline for this synthesis.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base.** Five trials and 220 participants is insufficient for a precise estimate of an incremental treatment effect. The 95% confidence interval spans 7.5 ECBI Problem points on a 36-point scale.
2. **Statistically significant heterogeneity that cannot be explained.** I² = 59.26% and τ ≈ 3.23 points indicate genuine variation in true effects, but k = 5 precludes any meta-regression or subgroup analysis capable of identifying its sources.
3. **Dependence on a single influential trial.** study_2_R2 (g = −1.286, n = 28) is both the largest effect and a major contributor to heterogeneity. A formal leave-one-out/influence analysis was not performed, so the sensitivity of the pooled MD to this single study is characterised qualitatively rather than quantitatively.
4. **Incomplete instrument-range verification.** As detailed above, the ECBI Problem range was confirmed for only 3 of 5 rows and no common-metric rescaling was applied.
5. **No formal risk-of-bias synthesis reported.** Domain-level risk-of-bias ratings (allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessors, attrition, selective reporting) were not available as structured inputs to this synthesis. This is a material gap: the primary outcome is *parent-reported*, and in additive designs parents are necessarily aware of whether they received a visibly tailored programme, raising a real risk of expectancy-driven differential reporting that would inflate apparent benefit on the ECBI.
6. **Single-informant, single-instrument, single-timepoint scope.** By design, this review excludes the ECBI Intensity scale, observational and teacher-report measures, and all follow-up assessments. The results therefore say nothing about whether any end-of-treatment advantage is maintained, nor whether it generalises to observed behaviour or to other settings.
7. **Heterogeneity of the "personalisation" construct.** Tailoring, cultural adaptation, subgroup matching and augmentation are lumped together under one conceptual umbrella. These are plausibly different interventions with different mechanisms and different expected effect sizes, and the small k prevents their separation.
8. **Dilution of the comparator contrast.** The degree to which "standard" arms were genuinely non-personalised could not be verified across trials, and any informal clinician tailoring in control arms would bias results toward the null.
9. **Limited power of publication-bias diagnostics.** With k = 5, Egger's test and trim-and-fill are near-powerless; the "low risk" judgement means bias was not detected, not that it is absent.
10. **No grey-literature or unpublished-data yield reported.** Additive-design null results are plausibly under-represented in the published record, and this synthesis cannot quantify that shortfall.

## 12. Conclusion

In randomised head-to-head comparisons of personalised versus standard versions of the *same* active parenting or family intervention for children with conduct problems, the pooled end-of-treatment effect on the ECBI Problem scale was a **Mean Difference of −3.534 points (95% CI −7.301 to 0.232; p = 0.0659; k = 5; N = 220; REML random effects; I² = 59.26%; τ² = 10.453216; Q = 9.745, df = 4, p = 0.045)**. This result **favours the personalised/tailored intervention** in direction and is clinically meaningful in magnitude, but it does not attain conventional statistical significance and its confidence interval includes no difference. Native-scale pooling was confirmed, so this is a true raw-unit ECBI Problem mean difference rather than a standardized surrogate. Publication bias diagnostics were reassuring (Egger's p = 0.3696; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized g = −0.3708) but have little power at this k.

The honest summary is that personalisation of parenting and family interventions **may** add a worthwhile increment to child conduct outcomes at end of treatment, but the current evidence — five small, heterogeneous trials, one of them highly influential, with parent-reported outcomes and incompletely verified instrument metrics — is **suggestive rather than conclusive**. Decisions to invest in personalisation protocols should presently be justified on grounds of engagement, acceptability and equity, with conduct-problem benefit treated as plausible but unproven. Resolving the question will require additive-design trials powered for a small incremental effect, with clearly specified and contrasted arms and complete raw-unit reporting of both ECBI subscales.
