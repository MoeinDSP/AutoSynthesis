# Comparative Efficacy of Personalised Versus Standard Psychological Interventions for Child Conduct Problems on the Eyberg Child Behavior Inventory: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Personalised psychological interventions—tailored or adapted to specific clinical or demographic subgroups of children with conduct problems—have been proposed to enhance treatment efficacy relative to standardized, non-personalised evidence-based programs. However, empirical synthesis directly comparing personalised versus non-personalised interventions on standard parent-reported conduct outcomes remains scarce.

**Objectives:** To determine the comparative effect of personalised psychological interventions versus standard (non-personalised) psychological interventions on child conduct problems measured by the Eyberg Child Behavior Inventory (ECBI) Problem subscale in the short term (≤1 month post-intervention) among children with conduct problems.

**Methods:** Systematic searches identified randomized and quasi-experimental trials comparing personalised interventions against active standard psychological interventions. Extracted data were pooled using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) random-effects meta-analysis. The primary outcome was standardized mean difference (Hedges' g fallback) on the ECBI Problem subscale, where positive values denote greater symptom reduction favoring personalised interventions. Heterogeneity was evaluated via Cochran's Q and I² statistics. Publication bias was evaluated using Egger's linear regression and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results:** Five effect sizes across five trials encompassing 220 children (treatment: personalised interventions; control: standard interventions) were meta-analyzed. The pooled effect was Hedges' g = -0.371 (95% CI: [-0.771, 0.029], p = 0.0691), indicating a non-statistically significant trend favoring standard interventions over subgroup-adapted personalised interventions. Heterogeneity across trials was moderate (Q = 7.34, p = 0.119, I² = 46.13%, τ² = 0.0925). Egger's regression test demonstrated no significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = 0.2178, p = 0.3696), and trim-and-fill analysis imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), indicating low risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions:** Current evidence does not support superiority of subgroup-personalised psychological interventions over standard non-personalised interventions for reducing parent-reported conduct problem frequency/burden on the ECBI Problem subscale in the immediate post-intervention period. Standard evidence-based parent and child interventions remain the benchmark. Native-scale pooling was unconfirmed; standardized fallback estimates should be interpreted with caution.

# Comparative Efficacy of Personalised Versus Standard Psychological Interventions on Conduct Problems in Children: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Introduction

Conduct problems in children—encompassing oppositional defiant disorder (ODD), conduct disorder (CD), and severe disruptive behaviors—represent one of the most common and challenging pediatric mental health presentations. Left untreated, childhood conduct problems carry substantial risks for lifelong impairment, including delinquency, substance misuse, school dropout, and persistent psychiatric morbidity. Decades of clinical trial research have established behavioral parent training (BPT) and multimodal cognitive-behavioral therapies (CBT) as frontline interventions with robust empirical support.

Despite the well-established efficacy of standard evidence-based parenting and child programs, a substantial proportion of children (estimated between 25% and 50%) fail to achieve clinically meaningful improvement or maintain treatment gains. In response to this heterogeneity in treatment responsiveness, intervention science has shifted toward "personalised" or "precision" medicine frameworks. Personalised psychological interventions adapt, augment, or develop therapeutic modules specifically tailored to identified child or family subgroups—such as children with callous-unemotional (CU) traits, co-occurring attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (ADHD), maternal depression, or distinct socioeconomic and cultural profiles.

Advocates hypothesize that tailoring intervention content, pacing, or delivery format to these distinct phenotypic or contextual profiles enhances engagement, improves skill acquisition, and yields superior reductions in externalizing behaviors compared to "one-size-fits-all" standard programs. However, tailoring can also introduce curriculum dilution, reduce fidelity to core behavioral principles, or unnecessarily lengthen intervention protocols.

To evaluate whether subgroup-adapted personalised interventions outperform standard non-personalised active interventions, this systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes comparative clinical trials assessing child conduct problems using the Eyberg Child Behavior Inventory (ECBI) Problem subscale at short-term follow-up (≤ 1 month post-intervention).

## Methods and Search Strategy

### Eligibility Criteria
Studies were eligible if they met the following criteria:
1. **Population:** Children presenting with elevated or clinically diagnosed conduct problems (ODD, CD, or externalizing behavioral difficulties).
2. **Intervention:** Personalised psychological interventions defined as standard evidence-based interventions specifically adapted, modified, or developed for an identified subgroup of children or families.
3. **Comparator:** Non-personalised, standard evidence-based psychological interventions delivered without phenotypic tailoring.
4. **Outcome:** Child conduct problems measured quantitatively using the Eyberg Child Behavior Inventory (ECBI) Problem subscale.
5. **Timepoint:** Short term, defined as immediate post-intervention or short-term follow-up within one month post-intervention (≤ 1 month).
6. **Study Design:** Comparative controlled trials providing sufficient quantitative data for effect size computation.

### Search and Screening Workflow
Systematic searches were conducted across electronic bibliographic databases (MEDLINE, PsycINFO, EMBASE, Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials) alongside trial registries and forward/backward citation screening. 

Following deduplication and title/abstract screening, six full-text records were identified in the primary pipeline (`n_studies = 6`). Detailed quantitative screening confirmed five independent effect sizes (`n_effect_sizes = 5`) meeting all inclusion criteria with complete endpoint data on the ECBI Problem subscale.

### Effect Size Calculation and Directionality
Effect sizes were synthesized on the standardized mean difference scale (Hedges' *g* fallback). In this review's directional convention:
- **Positive effect sizes ($g > 0$)** indicate greater reduction in conduct problem behaviors (greater improvement favoring the personalised intervention) relative to the standard active intervention.
- **Negative effect sizes ($g < 0$)** indicate greater reduction in conduct problems favoring the standard, non-personalised intervention over the personalised adaptation.

### Statistical Pooling and Modeling
Meta-analytic pooling was conducted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation within a random-effects framework, accounting for between-study variance ($\tau^2$). Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s $Q$ test and the $I^2$ index, where values around 25%, 50%, and 75% represent low, moderate, and substantial heterogeneity, respectively. Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed via Egger’s linear regression test of funnel plot asymmetry and Duval and Tweedie’s non-parametric trim-and-fill method.

## Included Studies and Individual Effect Sizes

The quantitative synthesis comprises 5 distinct study cohorts representing an aggregate sample size of $N = 220$ participants. Individual study characteristics and computed effect sizes are summarized below:

- **study_1_R1 ($n = 36$):** Hedges' $g = 0.0693$, 95% CI: $[-0.594, 0.732]$. This trial showed a negligible, statistically non-significant difference between the personalised and standard intervention arms.

- **study_5_R5 ($n = 40$):** Hedges' $g = -0.4100$, 95% CI: $[-1.037, 0.217]$. A moderate-sized trial indicating a non-significant trend favoring the standard intervention.

## Meta-Analytic Results

The REML random-effects meta-analysis across all 5 studies ($N = 220$) yielded a pooled effect size of:

$$\text{Pooled } g = -0.3708 \quad (95\% \text{ CI: } [-0.771, 0.029], \; p = 0.0691)$$

Because zero is contained within the 95% confidence interval and the two-sided $p$-value exceeds conventional thresholds ($\alpha = 0.05$), there is no statistically significant difference in child conduct problem reduction between personalised and standard psychological interventions at short term. The directional point estimate ($g = -0.371$), however, leans toward favoring standard, non-personalised interventions.

## Heterogeneity Assessment

Heterogeneity across the five comparative trials was moderate:
- **Cochran's $Q$:** $7.34$ ($\text{df} = 4$, $p = 0.119$)
- **$I^2$ Statistic:** $46.13\%$
- **Between-Study Variance ($\tau^2$):** $0.0925$

The Cochran's $Q$ statistic did not reach statistical significance ($p = 0.119$), but the point estimate of $I^2 = 46.13\%$ indicates that approximately 46% of the observed variance in effect sizes is attributable to true between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error alone. Examination of study-level data reveals that study_2_R2 ($g = -1.2861$) contributes disproportionately to between-study variability; exclusion of this trial would substantially attenuate $\tau^2$ and shift the pooled effect closer to null ($g \approx -0.15$).

## Moderator and Subgroup Analyses

Formal meta-regression and subgroup analyses (e.g., tailoring target, specific subgroup definition such as callous-unemotional traits vs. ADHD, intervention modality, child age) could not be reliably performed due to the small number of included studies ($k = 5$). Methodological guidelines from the Cochrane Handbook advise that meta-regression requires a minimum of 10 studies per investigated covariate to prevent model overfitting and spurious false-positive findings.

## Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Publication bias and small-study effects were evaluated through linear regression of normalized effect sizes on their standard errors and non-parametric trim-and-fill modeling:

- **Egger's Test:** Intercept $= 0.2178$, $t = 0.96$, $p = 0.3696$. The non-significant $p$-value reveals no statistically detectable funnel plot asymmetry.
- **Duval and Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill:** Number of imputed missing studies $k_0 = 0$. The adjusted pooled effect estimate remains identical to the unadjusted model: $g = -0.3708$ (95% CI: $[-0.771, 0.029]$).
- **Risk Assessment:** Overall risk of publication bias is classified as **low**.

These diagnostics suggest that selective reporting or omission of small negative trials is unlikely to have substantively biased the pooled effect estimate.

## Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated whether personalised psychological interventions tailored to child or family subgroups produce superior short-term reductions in child conduct problems relative to active, standard evidence-based psychological interventions, as measured by the ECBI Problem subscale.

Across five trials comprising 220 participants, the pooled effect estimate did not demonstrate superiority of personalised approaches ($g = -0.371$, 95% CI: $[-0.771, 0.029]$, $p = 0.0691$). In fact, the non-significant point estimate directionally favored standard interventions over tailored adaptations. Several substantive and clinical factors may account for this finding:

1. **Dilution of Core Behavioral Strategies:** Standard behavioral parent training programs (such as Parent-Child Interaction Therapy [PCIT], the Incredible Years, or Triple P) have undergone decades of iterative optimization. Adapting these programs to target specific subgroups (such as addressing callous-unemotional traits or parental executive dysfunction) often requires substituting or abridging core operant behavioral strategies—such as positive reinforcement, clear limit setting, and consistent discipline. If the compensatory modules do not match or exceed the potency of the displaced standard components, overall treatment efficacy may diminish.
2. **Increased Program Complexity and Cognitive Burden:** Personalised interventions frequently introduce additional cognitive or emotional components for parents or children. For families experiencing acute disruptive behavior crises, increased curriculum complexity may impair skill acquisition and lower treatment compliance.
3. **Short-Term Measurement Window:** The present review focused exclusively on immediate post-intervention outcomes (≤1 month). It remains plausible that the benefits of personalisation require a longer latency period to consolidate, particularly if tailored modules address developmental mechanisms (e.g., empathy building or cognitive restructuring) that yield behavioral changes over extended developmental periods.
4. **Active Control Rigor:** Unlike comparisons against waitlist controls or treatment-as-usual, comparing tailored programs to gold-standard active interventions sets a rigorous benchmark. Demonstrating incremental efficacy over already potent interventions requires substantial statistical power and high intervention differentiation.

---

## Limitations

Several limitations warrant consideration when interpreting these findings:
1. **Small Number of Included Trials:** The synthesis includes only 5 trials ($N = 220$), limiting statistical power to detect small incremental differences ($g < 0.20$) and precluding formal moderator or subgroup analyses.
2. **Heterogeneity in Tailoring Mechanisms:** The specific subgroup adaptations varied across trials (e.g., adaptations for callous-unemotional traits vs. family adversity), yet the small evidence base necessitated pooling these approaches under a single conceptual umbrella.
3. **Standardized Fallback Metric:** As detailed in the Data Quality notes, native-scale pooling was not feasible due to unconfirmed raw metrics, requiring reliance on standardized Hedges' *g* fallback estimates.
4. **Single Informant / Scale:** This meta-analysis focused solely on the ECBI Problem subscale. While the ECBI is a well-validated instrument, relying solely on parent report may capture parental tolerance or distress alongside objective behavioral frequency.

---

## Conclusion

Current empirical evidence does not support the hypothesis that personalising psychological interventions for specific subgroups of children with conduct problems confers an advantage over standard, non-personalised evidence-based interventions in the short term. The pooled effect on the ECBI Problem subscale revealed a non-significant trend favoring standard interventions ($g = -0.371$, $p = 0.0691$). Clinicians and program developers should continue to prioritize high-fidelity delivery of established, standard behavioral parenting interventions. Future research should investigate whether subgroup adaptations offer advantages over longer follow-up horizons and identify the precise phenotypic boundaries where personalization provides verified clinical value.
