# Fenoldopam Versus Placebo or Saline for Prevention of Acute Kidney Injury Across Patient Populations: A Random-Effects Meta-Analysis With a Prespecified Cardiac-Surgery Subgroup

## Abstract

**Background:** Fenoldopam has been evaluated for prevention of acute kidney injury (AKI), but the evidence base may span multiple patient populations. This meta-analysis evaluated fenoldopam versus placebo or saline control using AKI as a binary trial outcome, with cardiac-surgery patients intended as a prespecified subgroup rather than the sole eligible population.

**Methods:** The supplied evidence pipeline contained eight studies and six extracted effect-size records. Risk ratios (RRs) were to be pooled from randomized denominators on an intention-to-treat basis where possible. Five studies, comprising 946 randomized participants, had inputs suitable for native RR pooling. One additional record was excluded from the native RR analysis because the required raw binary inputs were unavailable; two of the eight studies had no extracted effect-size record. A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were assessed using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill. The supplied data did not identify studies by patient population, so the prespecified cardiac-surgery subgroup could not be estimated.

**Results:** The pooled risk ratio was **0.6604756919014616** (95% CI **0.417 to 1.047**; p = **0.07745428677468799**; k = **5**; N = **946**). The supplied favorability field classified this result as **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)”**; therefore, despite an RR numerically below 1, the result is not characterized here as favoring either arm without confirmation of event coding. Heterogeneity was substantial (Q = **11.106**, p = **0.0254**; I² = **60.99%**; τ² = **0.138148**). Egger’s intercept was **0.3539** (p = **0.0823**), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀ = **0**). Publication-bias risk was classified as moderate.

**Conclusions:** The available native-scale analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant effect of fenoldopam versus placebo or saline on AKI. The estimate was imprecise, crossed the null value of RR = 1, and showed substantial between-study heterogeneity. Interpretation is constrained by incomplete study-level data, unresolved outcome/event coding, lack of risk-of-bias information, and inability to conduct the prespecified cardiac-surgery subgroup analysis.

## Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a clinically important complication across surgical and nonsurgical patient populations. Fenoldopam, a selective dopamine-1 receptor agonist, has been investigated as a renal-protective intervention because of its renal vasodilatory and natriuretic effects. However, trial findings may differ across clinical settings, and analyses restricted to cardiac surgery alone would not answer the broader question of effectiveness across all eligible patient populations.

The objective of this meta-analysis was to evaluate the effect of fenoldopam, compared with placebo or saline control, on the development of AKI as a binary trial outcome. The intended estimand was the risk ratio (RR) based on the number of participants developing AKI and the total randomized in each trial arm, using randomized denominators on an intention-to-treat basis where possible. Cardiac-surgery patients were intended to form a prespecified subgroup rather than the sole eligible population.

## Meta-Analytic Results

A random-effects meta-analysis estimated using **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** included **5 studies** and **946 participants**. The resulting **pooled risk ratio was 0.6604756919014616**, with a **95% confidence interval from 0.417 to 1.047** and **p = 0.07745428677468799**.

The confidence interval included the null value of RR = 1, and the two-sided test was not statistically significant at the conventional 0.05 threshold. The point estimate was numerically below 1, but the supplied result classified favorability as **neither arm** because the direction depended on confirmation of which event was counted. Accordingly, the analysis does not establish that fenoldopam prevents AKI.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was substantial:

- **Cochran’s Q = 11.106**;
- **Q-test p = 0.0254**;
- **I² = 60.99%**; and
- **τ² = 0.138148**.

The significant Q test indicates that variability among study estimates exceeded that expected from sampling error alone. An I² of 60.99% suggests that a substantial proportion of observed variation was attributable to between-study differences. The estimated between-study variance, τ² = 0.138148, further supports use of a random-effects model.

Potential sources include differences in patient population, baseline AKI risk, clinical setting, fenoldopam regimen, control intervention, cointerventions, AKI definition, and ascertainment time. None could be examined directly because the necessary study-level covariates were not provided.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an **intercept of 0.3539** with **p = 0.0823**. This was not statistically significant at the 0.05 level and therefore did not provide clear evidence of small-study asymmetry. Nevertheless, the test was based on very few studies and cannot reliably exclude small-study effects.

Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0**, indicating that no potentially missing studies were imputed. The pipeline also supplied a trim-and-fill standardized estimate of **-0.4534**. This value is not a confirmed native-scale adjusted RR and should not be compared directly with the pooled RR of 0.6604756919014616. No trim-and-fill-adjusted RR was supplied.

The overall publication-bias risk was classified as **moderate**. This classification is appropriate given the nonsignificant but borderline Egger result, the small number of studies, and the limited ability of funnel-plot methods to distinguish publication bias from genuine heterogeneity.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across the five studies with data suitable for native RR pooling, the REML random-effects estimate was **RR 0.6604756919014616 (95% CI 0.417 to 1.047; p = 0.07745428677468799)**. The confidence interval was wide and crossed the null. The supplied favorability classification was **neither arm**, reflecting unresolved event-direction coding; thus, this synthesis does not claim a preventive effect for fenoldopam despite the point estimate being numerically below 1.

The evidence was heterogeneous (I² = 60.99%; Q p = 0.0254), indicating that a single average effect may not adequately describe all clinical contexts. This is especially relevant to the broad research question, which intentionally encompasses multiple patient populations. The inability to identify and analyze cardiac-surgery trials separately prevents assessment of whether that setting explains some of the observed heterogeneity.

### Clinical interpretation

The pooled estimate is compatible with effects ranging from a potentially meaningful reduction in AKI risk to a small increase in risk, as reflected by the 95% CI of 0.417 to 1.047. Because the interval includes no effect and the p value is 0.07745428677468799, the current analysis does not provide statistically conclusive evidence that fenoldopam changes AKI incidence relative to placebo or saline.

A risk ratio alone does not convey absolute clinical impact. Absolute risk reduction and number needed to treat would require a credible control-group AKI risk and verified event coding, neither of which was supplied. Safety outcomes, mortality, need for renal replacement therapy, length of stay, and hypotension were also not available and cannot be weighed against the AKI result.

### Consistency and robustness

The random-effects model was appropriate given the substantial heterogeneity. However, with only five studies, τ² and the pooled confidence interval may be sensitive to modeling choices and individual studies. No fixed-effect sensitivity analysis, leave-one-out analysis, alternative τ² estimator, Hartung–Knapp adjustment, or influence diagnostics were supplied. The cumulative plot may illustrate how the estimate evolved as studies accumulated, but numerical cumulative results were not provided and should not be inferred from the image alone.

Small-study analyses did not detect statistically significant asymmetry, but their power was poor. Egger’s p value of 0.0823 cannot establish absence of publication bias, and trim-and-fill’s estimate of zero missing studies should not be treated as definitive.

## Data Quality and Validation Notes

Several data-integrity issues materially affect interpretation:

1. The pipeline identified **8 studies**, but only **6 effect-size records** were supplied and only **5 studies** entered native RR pooling.
2. `study_3_R3` was excluded from native RR pooling because it lacked the raw binary inputs needed to compute an RR.
3. The requested RR conflicted with odds ratios explicitly reported in at least one source. The RR request was retained as authoritative, and incompatible results were not pooled together.
4. The six listed study-level values are standardized effect records, whereas the principal meta-analysis used native RRs. These scales must not be treated as interchangeable.
5. The analysis output did not confirm consistent event coding and classified the pooled result as favoring neither arm. Direction of benefit therefore requires verification against the source trial event definitions.
6. Patient-population classifications were absent, preventing the prespecified cardiac-surgery subgroup analysis.
7. Complete bibliographic details, arm-level AKI counts, and risk-of-bias assessments were not provided.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations.

First, the search and screening process was not documented sufficiently to establish that the evidence base was comprehensive. Databases, search dates, search terms, registry searches, duplicate handling, and reasons for full-text exclusion were unavailable.

Second, only five of eight identified studies contributed to native RR pooling. The effect of the two studies without extracted effect records is unknown, and the 80-participant `study_3_R3` record could not be included on the native RR scale.

Third, arm-specific AKI events and randomized denominators were not supplied. Consequently, the intention-to-treat calculations could not be independently reproduced, zero-event handling could not be evaluated, and consistency of AKI event coding could not be verified.

Fourth, the study populations and clinical settings were not identified. The central prespecified cardiac-surgery subgroup therefore could not be estimated, and clinical sources of substantial heterogeneity could not be explored.

Fifth, AKI definitions and assessment time points were unavailable. Different diagnostic criteria or ascertainment windows may have contributed to heterogeneity and may limit the clinical comparability of the pooled trials.

Sixth, no formal risk-of-bias assessment or certainty-of-evidence evaluation was provided. The confidence placed in the pooled estimate therefore cannot be graded using a framework such as GRADE.

Seventh, publication-bias methods were applied to only five studies. Egger’s test and trim-and-fill have limited reliability with such a small evidence base, especially in the presence of substantial heterogeneity.

Finally, no safety or secondary clinical outcomes were provided. The analysis cannot determine the overall benefit–harm profile of fenoldopam.

## Conclusion

In five studies comprising **946 participants**, the REML random-effects **pooled risk ratio was 0.6604756919014616 (95% CI 0.417 to 1.047; p = 0.07745428677468799)** for fenoldopam versus placebo or saline control. The result was not statistically significant, and the supplied direction field classified it as favoring **neither arm** pending confirmation of event coding. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (**I² = 60.99%; τ² = 0.138148; Q = 11.106, p = 0.0254**).

The available evidence therefore does not establish that fenoldopam reduces development of AKI across patient populations. The prespecified cardiac-surgery subgroup could not be evaluated because population classifications were missing. Future synthesis should retrieve complete trial reports, verify arm-level AKI events and randomized denominators, harmonize AKI definitions and time points, classify clinical populations prospectively, assess trial-level risk of bias, and report both relative and absolute effects.
