# Interleukin-1 Receptor Antagonist Therapy and All-Cause Mortality in Cardiovascular Secondary Prevention: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Interleukin-1 inhibition has been evaluated as an anti-inflammatory strategy in adults with established cardiovascular disease, but its effect on all-cause mortality remains uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate the class-level effect of anakinra or canakinumab, compared with placebo or usual care, on all-cause mortality at the longest reported follow-up.

**Methods:** The supplied evidence set contained 8 studies, of which 7 contributed an effect size to the prespecified random-effects synthesis. All eligible doses or regimens were combined within trials. The primary effect measure was the Risk Ratio (RR), pooled using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Seven studies comprising 10,663 participants contributed to the meta-analysis. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.9389302272873031 (95% CI 0.835–1.056; p = 0.29149054524518886). The result was classified by the supplied analytic output as favoring neither arm. No statistical heterogeneity was detected (Q = 3.737, p = 0.7122; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). Egger’s intercept was 0.1504 (p = 0.804), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0); publication-bias risk was classified as low.

**Conclusions:** In the supplied evidence base, interleukin-1 receptor antagonist therapy did not demonstrate a statistically significant class-level effect on all-cause mortality compared with placebo or usual care. The confidence interval remained compatible with both modest benefit and modest harm. Interpretation is limited by the small number of contributing studies, domination of the sample by one large study, absent trial-level death counts and study descriptors, and unresolved differences between the requested RR and hazard ratios reportedly used in source papers.

## Introduction

Residual inflammation is a potential therapeutic target among adults with established cardiovascular disease receiving secondary prevention. Anakinra blocks the interleukin-1 receptor, whereas canakinumab neutralizes interleukin-1β; both therefore intervene in interleukin-1 signaling, although through different pharmacological mechanisms. This review addressed whether these therapies, treated as one prespecified class, affect all-cause mortality compared with placebo or usual care.

The primary outcome was death from any cause among randomized participants at the longest reported follow-up. Cardiovascular death and other cause-specific outcomes were not accepted as substitutes. The primary synthesis was a random-effects meta-analysis of Risk Ratios (RRs), with all eligible doses or regimens combined within each trial.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A class-level random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood included 7 studies and 10,663 participants. The pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** was **0.9389302272873031** (95% CI **0.835 to 1.056**; p = **0.29149054524518886**). The confidence interval included the null value of 1, and the hypothesis test did not show a statistically significant difference between interleukin-1 receptor antagonist therapy and placebo or usual care.

In accordance with the supplied `favors` classification, this result favored **neither arm**—described in the analytic output as “essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted.” Given that the outcome was all-cause death, the point estimate alone should not be presented as proof of mortality benefit; the interval estimate remains compatible with a modest effect in either direction.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **0.1504** with p = **0.804**. Thus, the test did not detect funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies, and the supplied trim-and-fill output was **0.037**. Because the scale of the latter value was not documented and it does not correspond to the pooled RR, it was not interpreted as an adjusted Risk Ratio. The supplied analysis classified the risk of publication bias as **low**.

These findings should be interpreted cautiously. With only 7 studies, Egger’s test has limited power, and the extreme sample-size imbalance may complicate the assessment of asymmetry. Absence of statistically detected asymmetry is not proof that reporting bias is absent.

## Discussion

This random-effects meta-analysis did not identify a statistically significant class-level effect of anakinra or canakinumab on all-cause mortality in adults with established cardiovascular disease receiving secondary prevention. The pooled RR was 0.9389302272873031, with a 95% CI from 0.835 to 1.056 and p = 0.29149054524518886. The supplied analysis therefore classified the result as favoring neither arm. Statistical heterogeneity was absent by conventional measures, and the available diagnostics did not indicate publication bias.

The result is important because all-cause mortality avoids the adjudication and competing-classification issues inherent in cause-specific mortality. Nevertheless, it may be less responsive than inflammatory or recurrent cardiovascular endpoints, particularly in small, short-duration trials. The lack of statistical significance does not establish equivalence or no effect: the confidence interval includes effects that could be clinically meaningful in either direction. Absolute effects and numbers needed to treat or harm could not be calculated because arm-level mortality counts and baseline risks were unavailable.

The class-level synthesis also requires clinical caution. Anakinra and canakinumab affect interleukin-1 signaling differently and may have different pharmacokinetic profiles, dosing schedules, treatment durations, adverse effects, and trial populations. The prespecified combined analysis answers a class-level question, but no drug-specific subgroup results were supplied to assess consistency between agents.

Although I² and τ² were both zero, this finding should not be overinterpreted. The largest study contained 94.353% of all participants, whereas each of the six remaining studies enrolled between 34 and 189 participants. The small studies consequently add limited information about mortality, even if the fitted random-effects model does not identify between-study variance.

There is also an effect-measure provenance issue. The analytical record states that RR was explicitly requested and used as the authoritative measure, while source papers reportedly presented hazard ratios. RRs and hazard ratios are not interchangeable: RRs compare cumulative risks at a specified time, whereas hazard ratios compare event rates over follow-up under additional modeling assumptions. The native pooling output labels the synthesis as RR, but without arm-level death counts, follow-up data, or extraction formulas, independent verification is impossible.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Several features of the supplied dataset require explicit qualification:

1. The pipeline reported 8 studies but only 7 effect sizes; the reason one study did not contribute was not provided.
2. Trial-level numbers of deaths and randomized participants by arm were absent, preventing verification of the all-cause mortality RRs and preventing calculation of absolute risks.
3. Individual study outputs were supplied as standardized effects, whereas the pooled result was a native-scale RR. The standardized values were therefore reported separately and were not treated as study-specific RRs.
4. The analysis record documented a conflict between the requested RR and hazard ratios reportedly presented in source papers. The requested RR was retained as authoritative, but the transformation or reconstruction process was not available.
5. Study citations and key design details were missing, so duplicate populations, overlapping reports, longest-follow-up selection, and outcome classification could not be independently checked.
6. The trim-and-fill value of 0.037 lacked a documented effect scale and was not interpreted as an adjusted RR.

## Limitations

First, the search and screening methods were incompletely reported, precluding full assessment of PRISMA compliance, search sensitivity, and selection bias. Second, only 7 studies contributed to the synthesis, limiting the precision of heterogeneity estimates, publication-bias tests, and any potential moderator analysis. Third, one study dominated the participant count, reducing the effective breadth of the evidence base.

Fourth, no trial-level death counts, arm-specific denominators, follow-up durations, or individual RRs were provided. This prevented independent recalculation, continuity-correction assessment for zero-event trials, evaluation of differential follow-up, and estimation of absolute treatment effects. Fifth, risk-of-bias judgments and certainty-of-evidence assessments were unavailable. Sixth, combining anakinra and canakinumab may conceal genuine clinical or pharmacological differences, and no drug-specific analysis was reported. Seventh, use of longest available follow-up may introduce variation in risk windows across trials. Finally, the conflict between RRs and reportedly published hazard ratios introduces uncertainty regarding effect-measure derivation and comparability.

## Conclusion

Across 7 studies with 10,663 participants, the REML random-effects pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** for all-cause mortality was **0.9389302272873031** (95% CI **0.835–1.056**; p = **0.29149054524518886**). The supplied analytic classification favored neither interleukin-1 receptor antagonist therapy nor placebo/usual care. No statistical heterogeneity was detected (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), and publication-bias diagnostics were negative, although both assessments were limited by the small number of studies. On the available evidence, anakinra or canakinumab has not demonstrated a statistically significant class-level effect on all-cause mortality in cardiovascular secondary prevention. Better-reported analyses with verifiable arm-level mortality counts, harmonized follow-up, drug-specific estimates, and formal risk-of-bias assessment are needed.
