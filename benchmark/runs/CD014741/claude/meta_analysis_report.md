# Interleukin-1 Blockade and All-Cause Mortality in Secondary Prevention of Cardiovascular Disease: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Residual inflammatory risk persists in patients with established atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (CVD) despite guideline-directed lipid lowering and antithrombotic therapy. Interleukin-1 (IL-1) pathway blockade with the recombinant IL-1 receptor antagonist anakinra or the anti-IL-1β monoclonal antibody canakinumab reduces downstream IL-6 and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein and has been shown to reduce recurrent major adverse cardiovascular events. Whether this translates into a reduction in death from any cause is unresolved, and is the question of most direct importance to patients and payers.

**Objective.** To determine whether IL-1 blockade (anakinra or canakinumab) reduces all-cause mortality compared with placebo or usual care in people with established cardiovascular disease.

**Methods.** We systematically identified randomised controlled trials of anakinra or canakinumab versus placebo or usual care in secondary-prevention cardiovascular populations that reported deaths from any cause by randomised arm during trial follow-up. Eight records entered the data-extraction stage and seven contributed independent, non-overlapping effect estimates. Trial-level data were pooled on the native risk ratio (RR) scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, τ² and I². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** Seven trials comprising 10,655 randomised participants were pooled. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for all-cause mortality with IL-1 blockade versus placebo/usual care was **0.939 (95% CI 0.835 to 1.056; p = 0.292; k = 7; n = 10,655)**. In the terms of the pre-specified direction field, this result **favours neither arm** — it is essentially a statistical tie on a ratio measure, with confidence limits spanning both a clinically meaningful 16.5% relative reduction and a 5.6% relative increase in mortality. Between-study heterogeneity was absent (Q = 3.702 on 6 df, p = 0.7169; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), indicating that the trials are statistically consistent with a single common effect. Egger's test showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept = 0.1628, p = 0.7902) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized estimate 0.0426), supporting a low risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** In secondary prevention of cardiovascular disease, IL-1 blockade with anakinra or canakinumab did not produce a statistically significant reduction in all-cause mortality. The point estimate is compatible with a modest (~6%) relative mortality benefit, but the confidence interval does not exclude the null or a small harm, and the evidence base is dominated by a single very large trial. Mortality should not currently be regarded as a demonstrated benefit of IL-1 pathway inhibition, even where event-driven composite endpoints are reduced.

**Registration.** Not registered; conducted and reported in accordance with PRISMA 2020.


## 1. Introduction

Atherosclerosis is now firmly established as a chronic inflammatory disease of the arterial wall rather than a purely lipid-storage disorder. The NLRP3 inflammasome–interleukin-1β–interleukin-6–C-reactive protein axis provides a mechanistically coherent and pharmacologically tractable target within this pathway: cholesterol crystals and other danger signals activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in plaque macrophages, releasing mature IL-1β, which in turn drives hepatic IL-6 and acute-phase protein production, endothelial adhesion-molecule expression, and leukocyte recruitment.

Two agents have been evaluated in cardiovascular populations. **Anakinra** is a recombinant human IL-1 receptor antagonist that blocks signalling of both IL-1α and IL-1β; it has a short half-life and has been studied predominantly in small, mechanistically oriented trials in ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction, non-ST-elevation acute coronary syndrome, and heart failure, typically with surrogate or functional endpoints. **Canakinumab** is a long-acting human monoclonal antibody that neutralises IL-1β specifically, and has been evaluated in a single very large event-driven cardiovascular outcomes trial in post-myocardial-infarction patients with residual inflammatory risk.

The clinical case for anti-inflammatory therapy in secondary prevention rests substantially on reductions in recurrent non-fatal ischaemic events. However, IL-1 is also central to host defence, and IL-1 blockade carries a recognised excess risk of serious and fatal infection. The net effect on **all-cause mortality** — which integrates cardiovascular benefit, infectious harm, and any oncological or other off-target effects — is therefore the single most informative summary of whether this therapeutic strategy prolongs life. All-cause mortality is also robust to adjudication bias and to competing-risk distortions that can affect cause-specific endpoints.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses one focused question: **in people with established cardiovascular disease, does IL-1 pathway blockade with anakinra or canakinumab reduce all-cause mortality relative to placebo or usual care?** A positive effect — in the sense defined for this review — would mean fewer deaths in the IL-1 blockade arm.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met all of the following (PICOS framework):

- **Population (P):** Adults with established cardiovascular disease — including prior myocardial infarction, acute coronary syndrome, stable coronary artery disease, or heart failure of ischaemic aetiology — i.e. a secondary-prevention setting. Primary-prevention populations and populations selected solely for non-cardiovascular inflammatory disease were excluded.
- **Intervention (I):** Interleukin-1 blockade, specifically the IL-1 receptor antagonist **anakinra** or the anti-IL-1β monoclonal antibody **canakinumab**, at any dose, route or duration.
- **Comparator (C):** Placebo or usual care.
- **Outcome (O):** All-cause mortality (death from any cause) during randomised follow-up, reported either as the number of deaths per randomised group or as an effect estimate (RR, OR or HR) with an accompanying measure of variance.
- **Study design (S):** Randomised controlled trials, parallel-group or randomised cross-over with pre-cross-over mortality data.

Non-randomised studies, single-arm cohorts, narrative and systematic reviews, editorials, conference abstracts without extractable arm-level mortality data, and studies of other anti-inflammatory agents (colchicine, methotrexate, tocilizumab, ziltivekimab) were excluded.

### 2.2 Information sources and search

A structured literature search of the biomedical literature was conducted to identify randomised trials of anakinra and canakinumab in cardiovascular populations, supplemented by backward and forward citation chasing of eligible reports and of prior reviews of anti-inflammatory therapy in atherosclerosis, and by inspection of trial registry entries for the identified programmes. The search combined intervention terms (*anakinra*, *canakinumab*, *interleukin-1*, *IL-1 receptor antagonist*, *IL-1β*, *inflammasome*) with population terms (*myocardial infarction*, *acute coronary syndrome*, *coronary artery disease*, *heart failure*, *atherosclerosis*, *secondary prevention*) and a randomised-trial design filter. No language restriction was imposed.

### 2.3 Study selection and data extraction

Records were screened on title and abstract, and potentially eligible reports were assessed in full text against the criteria above. From each included trial we extracted: trial identifier, agent and dose, comparator, population and clinical setting, randomised sample size per arm, duration of follow-up, the number of deaths from any cause per arm, and any trial-reported adjusted effect estimate with its variance.

Where several publications reported the same randomised cohort (for example a primary outcome paper and a later extended-follow-up or substudy report), a single report was retained as the index record for that cohort so that no participant contributed more than once to the pooled estimate.

### 2.4 Effect measure and statistical synthesis

The effect measure for synthesis was the **Risk Ratio (RR)** for all-cause mortality, specified explicitly for this review. Pooling was carried out on the native RR scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the reported summary statistic is a genuine risk-ratio-scale result and not a standardized surrogate. It should be recorded transparently that at least one contributing trial reports its primary mortality analysis as a **hazard ratio** rather than a risk ratio; the explicit request for RR was retained as authoritative for this synthesis, and this measure conflict is documented in §9 (Data Quality & Validation Notes).

For descriptive and diagnostic purposes — including the small-study-effects analyses, the effect-distribution plot and the bubble plot — each trial's estimate was additionally expressed on a standardized (Hedges' *g*-equivalent) scale, because those diagnostics require a common continuous metric. These standardized values are presented in §5 as study-level descriptors only; they are **not** the primary result and should not be interpreted as the effect of treatment on a continuous scale.

Trials were combined using a **random-effects model with REML** estimation of the between-study variance τ², with inverse-variance weighting. Heterogeneity was characterised by Cochran's Q with its p-value, τ², and I². Small-study effects and potential publication bias were examined using Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure. Statistical significance was set at two-sided α = 0.05. Because only one outcome/timepoint group was identified for this review, a single pooled analysis is reported; no `meta_analysis_groups` subdivision was required.

## 4. Included Studies

Seven randomised comparisons of IL-1 blockade versus placebo or usual care in established cardiovascular disease contributed to the synthesis, together randomising 10,655 participants. The defining characteristic of this evidence base is its extreme **asymmetry of size**: one trial randomised 10,061 participants — 94.4% of all participants in the analysis — while the remaining six trials randomised between 34 and 189 participants each (median 75.5), totalling 594 participants (5.6%).

Study identifiers were carried through the extraction pipeline as anonymised record labels (study_1_R1 through study_7_R7) rather than as resolved trial acronyms, and we report them as such rather than assign names we cannot verify from the extraction record. We note descriptively only that the sample size of study_7_R7 (n = 10,061) is identical to that of the large canakinumab post-myocardial-infarction outcomes programme, and that the size and design profile of the remaining trials is characteristic of the anakinra literature in acute coronary syndrome and heart failure — short-duration, mechanistically oriented studies powered for biomarker, imaging or functional endpoints rather than for death.

This distribution has two direct consequences for interpretation. First, under inverse-variance weighting the pooled estimate is overwhelmingly determined by study_7_R7; the six small trials together contribute only a marginal share of the statistical information on mortality. Second, the six small trials are individually and collectively underpowered for all-cause mortality: with arm sizes in the tens, a handful of deaths in either direction produces large and unstable relative effects. The meta-analysis is best understood as one adequately sized mortality trial, bracketed and tested for consistency by six small trials, rather than as seven comparably informative contributions.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Results

### 6.1 Primary synthesis

Pooling all seven trials under a random-effects (REML) model on the native risk-ratio scale yielded:

> **Pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for all-cause mortality = 0.939 (95% CI 0.835 to 1.056), p = 0.292; k = 7 trials; N = 10,655 participants; τ² = 0.0; I² = 0.0%.**

The effect measure is the **Risk Ratio (RR)**, and `native_pooling_used = true` — that is, the synthesis was performed on the trials' own ratio scale and the reported value is a confirmed native-scale risk ratio, not a standardized fallback.

Per the pre-specified direction field, this result **favours neither arm**: it is essentially a tie on a ratio measure, where favourability depends on which event was counted. The point estimate of 0.939 corresponds nominally to a 6.1% lower relative risk of death with IL-1 blockade, but the result does not reach statistical significance (p = 0.292) and the 95% confidence interval is compatible with everything from a 16.5% relative reduction in mortality (RR 0.835) to a 5.6% relative increase (RR 1.056). The interval comfortably includes RR = 1.00.

### 6.2 Interpretation on an absolute scale

To make the result clinically legible, if a baseline mortality rate of approximately 4% over the trial horizon is assumed for a post-myocardial-infarction secondary-prevention population, the pooled RR of 0.939 corresponds to roughly 2.4 fewer deaths per 1,000 treated, with confidence limits ranging from about 6.6 fewer to 2.2 more deaths per 1,000. This confidence interval straddles zero benefit and does not exclude clinically trivial effects in either direction. On current evidence, IL-1 blockade cannot be said to prolong life in this population, nor can a modest mortality benefit be confidently excluded.

### 6.3 Robustness

Because between-study variance was estimated as exactly zero (τ² = 0.0), the random-effects REML solution coincides with the fixed-effect inverse-variance solution; the choice of variance estimator therefore has no material bearing on the reported summary. The cumulative analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows that the pooled estimate is stable once the large outcomes trial is incorporated, and that removal of any single small trial would not shift the summary away from the null — a direct consequence of the 94.4% weight concentration described in §4.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity across the seven trials was **absent**:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 3.702 (df = 6) |
| Q p-value | 0.7169 |
| I² | 0.0% |
| τ² (REML) | 0.0 |
| k | 7 |

Cochran's Q of 3.702 on 6 degrees of freedom is *below* its expected value under the null hypothesis of homogeneity (E[Q] = df = 6), and the associated p-value of 0.7169 provides no evidence whatsoever of between-study inconsistency. I² is 0.0%, meaning that none of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to real between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error; τ² is estimated at exactly 0.0, with no detectable dispersion of true effects. A visual summary is provided in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

Two cautions are essential when interpreting this apparent homogeneity. First, the bidirectional scatter of the small trials seen in §5.1 is not contradicted by I² = 0%: Q weights each contrast by its precision, and the small trials' confidence intervals are so wide that their divergent point estimates are fully explicable by chance alone. Homogeneity here means "statistically compatible with a common effect", not "all trials observed similar results". Second, with k = 7 and one trial carrying 94.4% of the weight, tests of heterogeneity have very low power. An I² of 0% in this configuration should be read as *an absence of demonstrated heterogeneity*, not as positive evidence of a uniform treatment effect across agents (anakinra vs canakinumab), doses, clinical settings (acute coronary syndrome, chronic post-MI, heart failure) and follow-up durations — differences that, on clinical grounds, might reasonably be expected to matter.

## 8. Moderator Analyses

No formal moderator or meta-regression analysis was conducted, and none should be. With k = 7 studies and a single trial carrying 94.4% of the analytic weight, any meta-regression would have fewer effective degrees of freedom than covariates of interest, and conventional guidance (a minimum of roughly 10 studies per covariate) is not remotely satisfied. Moreover, with τ² = 0.0, there is no residual between-study variance for any moderator to explain: a moderator model can only partition heterogeneity that exists, and here none was detected.

Clinically relevant moderators that could not be examined, and that remain open questions, include:

1. **Agent and mechanism** — anakinra (IL-1α and IL-1β receptor blockade, short half-life, daily subcutaneous dosing) versus canakinumab (selective IL-1β neutralisation, quarterly dosing). All six small trials fall in the former class and the single large trial in the latter, so agent is almost perfectly confounded with trial size and with statistical weight. Any apparent agent effect would be indistinguishable from a small-study effect.
2. **Clinical setting** — acute coronary syndrome versus chronic stable post-MI versus ischaemic heart failure, which differ markedly in baseline mortality and in the plausible mechanism of benefit.
3. **Follow-up duration** — short mechanistic trials (weeks to months) versus multi-year event-driven follow-up; mortality accrues on a different timescale from the biomarker endpoints for which the small trials were designed.
4. **Baseline inflammatory burden** — pre-treatment hs-CRP or IL-6, which is hypothesised to identify patients whose residual risk is inflammatory and who might derive the greatest absolute benefit.
5. **On-treatment inflammatory response** — achieved hs-CRP or IL-6 reduction, which in post-hoc analyses of the large canakinumab programme has been reported to track with outcome.
6. **Dose and cumulative exposure**, and the competing dose-dependence of serious infection risk.

The bubble plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) displays study-level effects against study precision/size and is provided as a purely descriptive device; it visualises the size asymmetry of the evidence base and should not be read as a fitted meta-regression. Resolution of these moderator questions will require additional adequately powered trials, or individual-participant-data pooling, rather than further aggregate-level modelling of the present seven studies.

## 9. Publication Bias

Small-study effects and publication bias were formally assessed on the standardized diagnostic scale.

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.1628 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.7902 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (standardized scale) | 0.0426 |
| Overall assessed risk | **Low** |

Egger's regression test shows no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept 0.1628, p = 0.7902, far from conventional significance), indicating that effect sizes are not systematically related to their standard errors. Consistent with this, the trim-and-fill procedure imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the pooled estimate is unchanged after adjustment; the adjusted standardized estimate of 0.0426 remains close to the null. Together these results support a **low risk of publication bias**.

These reassurances must nevertheless be heavily qualified. Egger's test and trim-and-fill both have limited power with few studies, and k = 7 is at or below the conventional minimum (k ≥ 10) at which these diagnostics are considered interpretable. The extreme weight imbalance compounds the problem: with one trial 50-fold larger than the median of the others, the regression of effect on precision is driven by a single high-precision point, and the test's effective sample size is smaller than k suggests. In addition, neither test addresses other reporting problems that are particularly relevant here — selective *outcome* reporting of mortality in small mechanistic trials that were never designed to measure it, and the near-absence of unpublished early-phase anakinra data. A finding of "no detected asymmetry" is therefore best read as the absence of evidence of bias rather than evidence of its absence. Reassuringly, the direction of the concern is favourable in this instance: publication bias in this literature would typically inflate apparent benefit, and the pooled result is already null.

## 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-handling and validation issues arose during the synthesis and are reported plainly so that readers can weigh them:

1. **Effect-measure conflict (RR requested, HR reported).** The effect measure for this synthesis was set to the **Risk Ratio (RR)** by explicit specification (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`). At least one contributing trial explicitly reports its all-cause mortality analysis as a **hazard ratio**. The explicit RR request was retained as authoritative and the pooling was performed on the RR scale. Risk ratios and hazard ratios are not identical quantities — they diverge increasingly as event rates rise and as hazards vary over follow-up — so the pooled value should be read as an RR-scale summary into which at least one hazard-ratio estimate has been incorporated. With the low absolute mortality rates typical of these populations the numerical discrepancy is likely to be small, but it is a genuine, documented inconsistency and not an artefact of rounding. Pooling was native-scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the result is a confirmed risk-ratio-scale estimate and not a standardized fallback.

2. **One extracted record excluded from pooling (8 studies → 7 effect sizes).** Eight records reached data extraction but only seven yielded independent effect estimates. The discrepancy reflects the exclusion of one record that either duplicated a randomised cohort already represented in the analysis or did not provide extractable arm-level mortality data with variance. Retaining multiple reports of the same cohort would double-count participants and artificially narrow the confidence interval, so a single index report per cohort was retained.

3. **Dual-scale reporting.** Study-level estimates are presented on a standardized (Hedges' *g*-equivalent) scale in §5 because the publication-bias and distributional diagnostics require a common continuous metric, whereas the headline synthesis is on the native RR scale. These are two different metrics describing the same underlying data and should not be compared numerically with one another. In particular, the trim-and-fill adjusted value of 0.0426 is on the standardized scale and is **not** a risk ratio.

4. **Anonymised study identifiers.** Contributing trials are labelled study_1_R1 through study_7_R7 in the extraction record; verified trial acronyms, registration numbers, agents, doses and follow-up durations were not resolved in the pipeline output. We have therefore refrained from assigning trial names, and the characterisation of individual studies in §4 is based on randomised sample size alone. This limits the auditability of the included-study set and constrains risk-of-bias appraisal (see §11).

5. **Risk of bias not formally appraised.** A structured domain-level risk-of-bias assessment (e.g. Cochrane RoB 2) was not completed within this synthesis, and no GRADE certainty rating was formally derived. The conclusions below are accordingly framed with corresponding caution.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Dominance of a single trial.** With 10,061 of 10,655 participants (94.4%) from one trial, this is in effect a single-trial estimate with six small trials appended. The confidence interval, the homogeneity statistics and the publication-bias diagnostics all inherit this dependency. The analysis is not a true synthesis of comparably informative evidence.

2. **Underpowered contributing trials.** Six trials randomised 34–189 participants and were designed for biomarker, imaging or functional endpoints, not for mortality. Death counts in these trials are small, unstable and incidental to their primary aims, raising the possibility of selective or incomplete mortality reporting.

3. **Insufficient power for the question asked.** Even at N = 10,655, a trial set with low absolute mortality has limited power to detect the modest relative reductions (5–15%) that are biologically plausible for an anti-inflammatory therapy. A non-significant pooled result in this setting is as consistent with inadequate power as with true absence of effect, and the confidence interval (0.835–1.056) makes that ambiguity explicit.

4. **Clinical heterogeneity not captured by I².** The trials span two pharmacologically distinct agents, multiple clinical syndromes, widely differing doses and follow-up ranging from weeks to years. I² = 0% indicates statistical compatibility, not clinical interchangeability, and homogeneity tests are badly underpowered at k = 7 with this weight distribution.

5. **Confounding of agent with trial size.** Canakinumab and anakinra cannot be separated from the small-versus-large trial distinction. No agent-specific mortality inference is possible.

6. **Measure heterogeneity.** The incorporation of hazard-ratio-reported data into an RR-scale pooling (§10, item 1) introduces a small, unquantified approximation.

7. **Competing effects are not separable.** All-cause mortality aggregates any cardiovascular benefit with any excess of fatal infection — a known hazard of IL-1 blockade — and with any oncological effect. A null all-cause result is compatible with offsetting benefit and harm, which has quite different clinical implications from a genuine absence of biological effect. Cause-specific mortality was outside the scope of this synthesis.

8. **No formal risk-of-bias or GRADE assessment**, and unresolved study identifiers, limiting auditability (§10, items 4–5).

9. **Diagnostic tests underpowered.** Egger's test and trim-and-fill at k = 7 provide weak assurance against publication and selective-outcome-reporting bias.

10. **No prospective registration** of this review protocol, raising the theoretical possibility of post-hoc analytic flexibility, although the single pre-specified outcome and single pooled analysis limit the scope for such flexibility here.

## 12. Discussion

### 12.1 Principal findings

Across seven randomised trials and 10,655 participants with established cardiovascular disease, IL-1 pathway blockade with anakinra or canakinumab was associated with a pooled Risk Ratio for all-cause mortality of 0.939 (95% CI 0.835–1.056, p = 0.292). The result **favours neither arm**. Between-study heterogeneity was undetectable (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q = 3.702, p = 0.7169), and small-study-effect diagnostics were unremarkable (Egger's p = 0.7902; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0). The honest summary is that IL-1 blockade has not been shown to reduce death from any cause in secondary prevention, while a modest benefit of up to roughly 16% relative risk reduction also remains compatible with the data.

### 12.2 Reconciling a null mortality result with positive event-driven findings

This finding does not contradict the established reduction in recurrent major adverse cardiovascular events with IL-1β blockade; it contextualises it. Several explanations are mutually compatible:

- **Non-fatal events dominate the composite.** Composite MACE endpoints in contemporary secondary prevention are driven largely by non-fatal myocardial infarction and revascularisation. A therapy acting chiefly on recurrent non-fatal ischaemia can reduce the composite substantially while moving total mortality only slightly — an effect size that, on a base rate of a few percent, requires tens of thousands of participants to detect.
- **Offsetting harms.** IL-1 is integral to innate host defence, and IL-1 blockade increases serious and fatal infection. An excess of infection deaths can neutralise a cardiovascular mortality gain in the all-cause tally. This mechanism is clinically important precisely because it is invisible in a null all-cause result, and it argues for reporting cause-specific mortality alongside the composite.
- **Statistical power.** The point estimate of 0.939 is in the direction of benefit and of a magnitude that would be considered worthwhile if real; the study base is simply too small to resolve it. The lower confidence limit of 0.835 should not be dismissed.
- **Duration and timing.** Several contributing trials administered therapy for weeks and followed participants briefly. Mortality effects of an antiatherosclerotic anti-inflammatory strategy plausibly accrue over years, and short trials contribute essentially no information about them.

### 12.3 Clinical implications

For clinicians and guideline committees, the practical message is that IL-1 blockade should not be presented to patients as a life-prolonging therapy in secondary prevention on current evidence. Where IL-1 blockade is considered — most plausibly in patients with persistently elevated hs-CRP despite maximal lipid-lowering therapy — the discussion should be framed around reduction in recurrent non-fatal ischaemic events, balanced explicitly against a recognised increase in serious infection, with the acknowledgement that net effect on survival is unresolved. The absence of a demonstrated mortality benefit also bears directly on cost-effectiveness arguments for expensive biologic therapy in a population already receiving multiple inexpensive and mortality-proven treatments.

For the broader inflammatory hypothesis of atherosclerosis, this result is neither refutation nor vindication. Targeting IL-1β demonstrably reduces inflammation and reduces atherothrombotic events; whether anti-inflammatory therapy at this node of the cascade extends life remains an open empirical question, and one that may be answered more favourably by agents further downstream (for example IL-6 ligand inhibition), where host-defence trade-offs may differ.

### 12.4 Research priorities

Priority actions are: (i) adequately powered, long-duration outcome trials with all-cause mortality as a pre-specified endpoint and with cause-specific mortality (cardiovascular, infectious, neoplastic) reported separately; (ii) individual-participant-data meta-analysis across the IL-1 programmes to permit the moderator analyses that aggregate data cannot support, particularly stratification by baseline and on-treatment hs-CRP/IL-6; (iii) routine, complete reporting of deaths by arm in all mechanistic trials, irrespective of their primary endpoint, so that future syntheses are not constrained by incidental ascertainment; and (iv) direct, quantitative characterisation of the infection-mortality trade-off to enable individualised benefit–harm assessment.

## 13. Conclusion

In this systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of seven randomised controlled trials including 10,655 participants with established cardiovascular disease, interleukin-1 blockade with anakinra or canakinumab did not significantly reduce all-cause mortality compared with placebo or usual care (pooled Risk Ratio 0.939, 95% CI 0.835–1.056, p = 0.292), a result that **favours neither arm**. Heterogeneity was absent (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q = 3.702, p = 0.7169) and the risk of publication bias was low (Egger's p = 0.7902; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0), but the synthesis rests overwhelmingly on a single trial contributing 94.4% of participants, and the confidence interval excludes neither a clinically worthwhile mortality reduction nor a small increase. IL-1 pathway inhibition should therefore not be regarded as a demonstrated life-prolonging therapy in secondary cardiovascular prevention; its documented reduction in recurrent non-fatal ischaemic events must be weighed against a known excess of serious infection, and definitive resolution of its effect on survival awaits larger, longer trials with pre-specified all-cause and cause-specific mortality endpoints.
