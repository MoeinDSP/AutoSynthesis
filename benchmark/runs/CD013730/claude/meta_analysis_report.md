# Early Low-Dose Systemic Dexamethasone (<2 mg/kg cumulative, initiated before 7 days of age) versus Placebo or No Treatment for the Prevention of Death or Bronchopulmonary Dysplasia at 36 Weeks' Postmenstrual Age in Preterm Infants: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Postnatal corticosteroids reduce bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) in preterm infants but have been associated with adverse neurodevelopmental outcomes, prompting interest in regimens that start early yet deliver a low cumulative dose. Whether early (<7 days of postnatal age), low cumulative dose (<2 mg/kg) systemic dexamethasone reduces the composite outcome of death or BPD at 36 weeks' postmenstrual age (PMA) remains uncertain.

**Objective.** To determine whether early low-dose systemic dexamethasone, compared with placebo or no treatment, reduces the number of preterm infants (<37 weeks' gestation) who die or have an oxygen requirement (BPD/chronic lung disease) at 36 weeks' PMA, using an intention-to-treat denominator.

**Methods.** A systematic search of randomised and quasi-randomised controlled trials was conducted and screened against pre-specified PICO criteria. Eleven records entered the data-extraction stage; seven randomised comparisons contributed extractable, non-overlapping 2×2 data for the composite outcome of death or BPD at 36 weeks' PMA. The primary analysis pooled risk ratios (RR) on the native binary scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Seven trials randomising 1,312 infants were pooled. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for death or BPD at 36 weeks' PMA was 0.921 (95% CI 0.804 to 1.056; p = 0.239; k = 7; n = 1,312). This result is formally indeterminate — the confidence interval crosses the line of no effect and the direction classification returned was "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)". Native-scale (risk ratio) pooling was confirmed and used. Between-study heterogeneity was low: Q = 9.509 (df = 6), p = 0.1469; I² = 16.29%; τ² = 0.005537 (log-RR scale). Egger's regression showed no statistical evidence of funnel asymmetry (intercept 0.0921; p = 0.5204) and trim-and-fill imputed only a single potentially missing study (k₀ = 1; adjusted standardized estimate −0.0238); the overall small-study-effect risk was nonetheless graded **moderate** because both procedures are underpowered with fewer than ten studies.

**Conclusions.** In this meta-analysis of seven randomised comparisons, early low-dose systemic dexamethasone did not produce a statistically detectable reduction in death or BPD at 36 weeks' postmenstrual age. The point estimate is compatible with anything from a clinically worthwhile ~20% relative risk reduction to a ~6% relative increase, and the result should be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no effect. Given the known neurodevelopmental hazards of early systemic dexamethasone, these data do not support its routine early use at low cumulative dose for BPD prophylaxis; adequately powered trials reporting both pulmonary and long-term neurological outcomes are required.

**Registration.** Not registered. **Funding.** None.

## 1. Introduction

Bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) remains the most common serious morbidity of extremely preterm birth and is strongly associated with prolonged respiratory support, recurrent hospitalisation, and adverse long-term pulmonary and neurodevelopmental trajectories. Because inflammation is a central mechanism in the evolution of neonatal chronic lung disease, systemic corticosteroids — principally dexamethasone — have been studied for more than three decades as a means of preventing or ameliorating BPD.

The evidence base has, however, been dominated by a therapeutic tension. Early (first week of life), high cumulative dose dexamethasone regimens reliably facilitate extubation and reduce the incidence of BPD, but have been linked in randomised trials and follow-up studies to gastrointestinal perforation, hyperglycaemia, hypertension, somatic growth failure, cerebral palsy and adverse neurodevelopmental outcome. These harms led professional bodies to recommend against the routine early use of high-dose dexamethasone. The subsequent research question has therefore shifted: can a **low cumulative dose** (conventionally defined as <2 mg/kg total) given **early** (initiated before 7 days of postnatal age) retain a pulmonary benefit while limiting dose-dependent toxicity?

This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses that specific question. We asked whether, in preterm infants born before 37 weeks' gestation and at risk of BPD, early low-dose systemic dexamethasone compared with placebo or no treatment reduces the composite outcome of **death or BPD (oxygen requirement/chronic lung disease/oxygen dependence) at 36 weeks' postmenstrual age (PMA)**, analysed on an intention-to-treat denominator with deaths before 36 weeks added to surviving infants with BPD when reported separately. The composite was chosen deliberately because death and BPD are competing outcomes: a treatment that reduces BPD only by increasing mortality would show a spurious pulmonary benefit if BPD were analysed among survivors alone.

**Effect direction convention.** For this review, a result favouring dexamethasone corresponds to a risk ratio below 1 (equivalently, a positive Hedges' g in the standardized diagnostic metric) — that is, fewer infants died or developed BPD at 36 weeks' PMA in the dexamethasone arm. The directional classification reported in the Results section is taken from the deterministic direction field generated by the analysis pipeline and is not inferred from the sign of the point estimate alone.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

This report follows the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement in its structure and content. The review was not prospectively registered; this is acknowledged as a limitation (Section 11).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria (PICO)

- **Population.** Preterm infants born at <37 weeks' gestation, at risk of BPD.
- **Intervention.** Systemic (intravenous or oral) dexamethasone, initiated **before 7 days of postnatal age**, with a **cumulative dose <2 mg/kg**.
- **Comparator.** Placebo or no treatment.
- **Outcome.** Number of infants who died **or** had BPD (defined by oxygen requirement / chronic lung disease / oxygen dependence) at **36 weeks' PMA**, out of the number randomised to each arm (intention-to-treat denominator). Where death and BPD were reported separately, deaths before 36 weeks were added to survivors with BPD to reconstruct the composite.
- **Study design.** Randomised or quasi-randomised controlled trials.

Studies were excluded if the corticosteroid was inhaled rather than systemic, if the regimen was initiated at or after 7 days of age, if the cumulative dose was ≥2 mg/kg, if the comparator was an active corticosteroid regimen rather than placebo/no treatment, or if the composite outcome at 36 weeks' PMA could not be reconstructed on an intention-to-treat denominator.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

Bibliographic databases and trial registries covering neonatal randomised controlled trials were searched for records combining terms for preterm birth, dexamethasone/postnatal corticosteroids, and bronchopulmonary dysplasia/chronic lung disease/oxygen dependence. Reference lists of retrieved trials and relevant systematic reviews were hand-searched for additional eligible trials.

### 2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Records were screened against the PICO criteria at title/abstract level and then in full text. For each eligible trial, the following were extracted: number randomised per arm, number of infants who died or had BPD at 36 weeks' PMA per arm, timing of initiation, cumulative dexamethasone dose, and comparator type. Where multiple publications reported the same trial, a single primary report was retained to avoid double-counting of participants.

### 2.5 Effect measures and synthesis

The **primary effect measure was the Risk Ratio (RR)**, pooled on the native binary scale. The analysis pipeline confirmed that native-scale pooling was used (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the pooled value reported below is a genuine risk-ratio result and **not** a standardized fallback. The effect measure was specified a priori by the review question (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`); no conflict between the requested and the detected measure was recorded.

Pooling used a **random-effects model with REML** estimation of τ². In parallel, study-level estimates were also expressed as standardized mean differences (Hedges' g) for graphical display and for the regression-based small-study-effect diagnostics, which require a continuous effect/standard-error pair; these standardized values are reported in Section 5 for transparency but are **not** the primary inferential metric.

### 2.6 Heterogeneity, moderators and small-study effects

Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q, the I² statistic and τ². Small-study effects were examined using Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure, with an overall qualitative risk grading. Because only seven trials contributed, formal subgroup analysis and meta-regression were restricted to descriptive inspection (Section 8).

## 3. Search and Screening Results

The search and screening process identified a pool of records that was reduced, through title/abstract and full-text assessment against the PICO criteria, to **11 study records entering data extraction**. Of these, **7 randomised comparisons provided extractable, non-overlapping 2×2 data** for the composite outcome of death or BPD at 36 weeks' PMA and were carried forward into the quantitative synthesis.

The four remaining records did not contribute an effect size to the pooled analysis. Reasons in this category comprise records that did not report the composite outcome at the 36-week PMA timepoint in a form permitting intention-to-treat reconstruction, and records representing secondary or companion reports of an already-included trial, which were set aside so that no cohort of infants was counted twice. No separate effect-measure strata were required: all seven contributing comparisons reported the same dichotomous outcome at the same timepoint, so a **single meta-analytic group** was formed rather than multiple outcome/timepoint groups.

A PRISMA-style summary of flow is therefore: 11 records assessed at extraction → 4 not contributing extractable, non-duplicated composite-outcome data → **7 trials, 1,312 randomised infants, included in the meta-analysis**.

## 4. Included Studies

Seven randomised comparisons, enrolling a total of **1,312 preterm infants**, contributed to the synthesis. All compared early (<7 days of postnatal age), low cumulative dose (<2 mg/kg) systemic dexamethasone against placebo or no treatment, and all reported death or BPD (oxygen requirement) at 36 weeks' PMA.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Study-level estimates, expressed in the standardized diagnostic metric (Hedges' g, with positive values indicating fewer deaths/BPD cases in the dexamethasone arm, i.e. favouring treatment), were as follows:

|---|---|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | −0.573 | −1.206 to 0.060 | 40 |
| study_3_R3 | +0.163 | −0.087 to 0.413 | 248 |
| study_4_R4 | −0.058 | −0.322 to 0.206 | 220 |
| study_6_R6 | −0.303 | −0.557 to −0.049 | 241 |
| study_7_R7 | +0.006 | −0.195 to 0.206 | 384 |
| study_9_R9 | +0.306 | −0.166 to 0.778 | 70 |

Six of the seven individual confidence intervals crossed zero; only **study_6_R6** (g = −0.303, 95% CI −0.557 to −0.049) excluded the null, and its point estimate lay on the side **unfavourable** to dexamethasone in the standardized metric. The remaining estimates were scattered symmetrically and tightly around the null, ranging from −0.573 to +0.306, with the two extremes contributed by the two smallest trials (n = 40 and n = 70) — a pattern typical of sampling noise in underpowered studies rather than of genuine effect modification. The distribution of study-level effects is shown in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`.

**Important measurement caveat.** These standardized g values are presented for graphical and diagnostic purposes only. The primary, pre-specified effect measure for this review is the **Risk Ratio (RR)**, and the pooled inference reported in Section 6 was computed on the native risk-ratio scale.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was analysed (one outcome, one timepoint), so no separate per-group results are required.

**Primary synthesis — death or BPD at 36 weeks' postmenstrual age**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Risk Ratio (RR)** — native-scale pooling confirmed |
| Pooled RR | **0.921** |
| 95% confidence interval | **0.804 to 1.056** |
| p-value | **0.239** |
| Trials (k) | **7** |
| Infants (n) | **1,312** |
| Model | Random effects, REML |
| Direction classification | **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"** |

The pooled Risk Ratio of **0.921 (95% CI 0.804–1.056, p = 0.239)** did not reach statistical significance at the conventional α = 0.05 threshold. Per the deterministic direction field returned by the analysis, this result **favours neither arm** — it is essentially a tie, and for a ratio measure the favourable side depends on which event was counted; the reported value retains the raw treatment-minus-control sign convention and should not be reinterpreted by eye.

Interpreted as a relative risk on the counted event (death or BPD), the interval is compatible with as much as a ~19.6% relative reduction and as much as a ~5.6% relative increase in the composite outcome. In other words, while the central estimate leans numerically towards fewer events with dexamethasone, the data cannot exclude no effect, and cannot exclude a small excess of the composite outcome. The honest summary is that the trial evidence for early low-dose dexamethasone on death-or-BPD at 36 weeks' PMA is **inconclusive**, not negative in the sense of a demonstrated absence of benefit.

Because native (risk-ratio) pooling was confirmed, this value is a true risk-ratio result; no standardized fallback was substituted. The forest plot with individual and pooled estimates is at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`.

**Stability of the estimate.** The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows the running pooled estimate converging towards the null-adjacent final value as information accrued, without any single trial driving the result away from the region of no effect. Given that the largest trial (study_7_R7, n = 384) reported an essentially null standardized effect (g = +0.006), the pooled estimate is not dependent on any one dominant study.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **low**:

- Cochran's **Q = 9.509** on 6 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.1469** — no statistically significant excess variation beyond chance;
- **I² = 16.29%** — only a small proportion of observed variability attributable to between-study differences rather than sampling error, conventionally interpreted as "might not be important";
- **τ² = 0.005537** on the log-risk-ratio scale, corresponding to a between-study standard deviation of approximately 0.074 in log-RR units — a small amount of true dispersion.

An approximate 95% prediction interval constructed from this τ² spans roughly RR 0.71 to 1.20, indicating that the plausible true effect in a new, exchangeable trial setting still straddles the null, though the interval is appreciably wider than the confidence interval for the mean effect. The low τ² means that the random-effects and fixed-effect syntheses would yield very similar point estimates, and that the non-significant pooled result is not an artefact of heterogeneity-inflated uncertainty.

A graphical summary of the heterogeneity diagnostics is provided in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

The one trial whose individual interval excluded the null (study_6_R6) is the principal contributor to the observed Q; nevertheless, even with that trial included, heterogeneity remained in the low range, implying that the seven trials are reasonably consistent with a common underlying effect close to the null.

## 8. Moderators

With only **k = 7** trials, formal meta-regression and subgroup analysis were not statistically justifiable: the conventional minimum of approximately ten studies per covariate was not met, and any such analysis would be severely underpowered and highly susceptible to false-positive findings. **No moderator analysis was therefore performed, and no moderator effect estimates are reported.** This should be read as an absence of analysis, not as an absence of moderation.

Descriptively, however, two features are worth noting:

1. **Study precision.** The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) plots effect size against study size. The two most extreme effects in either direction came from the two smallest trials (n = 40, g = −0.573; n = 70, g = +0.306), while the three largest trials clustered within ±0.31 of the null. This funnel-like convergence with increasing precision is the expected pattern under a near-null common effect with no systematic small-study distortion.
2. **Residual clinical variability.** Even within the pre-specified bounds of this review (initiation <7 days, cumulative dose <2 mg/kg), trials necessarily differed in exact total dose, taper schedule, day of initiation within the first week, gestational-age distribution, baseline BPD risk, surfactant and antenatal steroid exposure, and era-specific ventilation strategies. The low τ² suggests these differences did not translate into large differential effects on the composite outcome, but the analysis has no power to exclude dose-response or risk-stratified effects. The widely discussed hypothesis that corticosteroid benefit on death-or-BPD increases with baseline BPD risk could not be tested here.

## 9. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Two complementary assessments were undertaken.

- **Egger's regression test.** Intercept = **0.0921**, **p = 0.5204**. There is no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry; the p-value sits well above conventional thresholds, and the intercept is close to zero.
- **Trim-and-fill (Duval & Tweedie).** The procedure imputed **k₀ = 1** potentially missing study, with an adjusted standardized estimate of **g = −0.0238**. Imputation of a single study implies that the pooled estimate would be essentially unchanged by any such adjustment and would remain adjacent to the null.
- **Overall qualitative risk grading: moderate.**

The "moderate" grading, despite two reassuring quantitative tests, reflects a power limitation rather than a positive signal of bias. Both Egger's regression and trim-and-fill have low statistical power when fewer than about ten studies are pooled, so a non-significant Egger test here mainly reflects limited sensitivity rather than a demonstrated absence of small-study effects. Accordingly, **these findings are reassuring but not definitive**. Conclusions about reporting bias should be qualified by non-statistical considerations — in particular the comprehensiveness of grey-literature and trial-registry searching, the possibility of unpublished early-corticosteroid trials terminated or unreported after safety concerns emerged in the field, and the skewed distribution of study precision (two very small trials alongside three moderately large ones).

It is also relevant that the field of early postnatal corticosteroids was subject to an unusual publication dynamic: once neurodevelopmental harms were recognised, recruitment into early-dexamethasone trials became difficult and several studies stopped early. Early-stopped trials tend to be both small and, by construction, to have imprecise and potentially extreme effect estimates, which can distort funnel-based diagnostics in ways these tests cannot detect.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis of seven randomised comparisons and 1,312 preterm infants found that early (<7 days), low cumulative dose (<2 mg/kg) systemic dexamethasone did **not** significantly change the composite outcome of death or BPD at 36 weeks' postmenstrual age relative to placebo or no treatment (pooled Risk Ratio 0.921, 95% CI 0.804–1.056, p = 0.239). The direction classification for this result is **"neither"** — the comparison is, on the available data, essentially a tie.

Three features of the result deserve emphasis.

**First, the result is inconclusive rather than definitively null.** The 95% confidence interval includes a relative risk reduction approaching 20%, which, applied to a population with a composite event rate frequently in the range of 50–70%, would translate into a clinically meaningful absolute benefit. It also includes a modest relative increase. With 1,312 infants and seven trials, the analysis has reasonable but not conclusive precision; a true effect of moderate size cannot be excluded in either direction. Describing this as "no effect" would overstate what the data support.

**Second, the consistency of the trials is notable.** With I² = 16.29%, τ² = 0.005537 and a non-significant Q test (p = 0.1469), the seven trials tell a reasonably coherent story. Six of seven individual intervals crossed the null, and the single trial that did not (study_6_R6) pointed away from benefit in the standardized metric. The near-null result is therefore not an artefact of averaging strongly discordant trials — it reflects a genuine clustering of trial results around no important difference.

**Third, the dose-reduction hypothesis is not supported by these data.** The rationale for restricting the review to cumulative doses below 2 mg/kg was that lower-dose regimens might preserve pulmonary benefit while attenuating harm. Historical syntheses of **high-dose** early dexamethasone have generally shown a reduction in BPD among survivors, at the cost of substantial short- and long-term adverse effects. The present synthesis finds no statistically detectable pulmonary composite benefit at low cumulative dose. The most parsimonious reading is that the pulmonary effect of early systemic dexamethasone is dose-dependent, and that reducing the cumulative dose below 2 mg/kg may reduce benefit along with (rather than instead of) toxicity. This is a hypothesis generated, not confirmed, by the present data, since no formal dose-response moderator analysis was feasible.

**Clinical implications.** The composite outcome used here deliberately binds death to BPD, guarding against the possibility that a reduction in BPD is achieved through mortality. On that composite, early low-dose dexamethasone offers no demonstrated advantage. Set against the well-documented risks of early systemic corticosteroid exposure in the first week of life — including gastrointestinal perforation (especially with concurrent indomethacin or ibuprofen), hyperglycaemia, hypertension, impaired somatic and head growth, and increased cerebral palsy in the high-dose literature — the absence of a demonstrable benefit on death-or-BPD argues against routine early prophylactic use of low-dose dexamethasone outside clinical trials. Clinicians considering postnatal corticosteroids for established, evolving severe lung disease after the first week of life should note that this review does **not** address that indication; later, lower-dose, risk-targeted regimens are a separate question with a distinct evidence base.

**Research implications.** Three priorities follow. (i) Adequately powered trials are needed: with a plausible target of detecting a 15% relative reduction in a composite with a ~60% control event rate, required sample sizes substantially exceed the 1,312 infants pooled here. (ii) Future trials should stratify or model by **baseline BPD risk**, since the benefit–harm balance of corticosteroids is widely hypothesised to shift with predicted risk. (iii) All such trials must report **long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes at 18–24 months corrected age or later** alongside the 36-week pulmonary composite, because a pulmonary endpoint alone is insufficient to guide practice for this drug class.

## 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No formal validation flags were returned by the analysis pipeline for this synthesis. For full transparency, the following data-handling points are nonetheless recorded:

- **Records not contributing effect sizes.** Eleven study records entered data extraction, but only **seven** yielded extractable, non-overlapping 2×2 data for the composite outcome at 36 weeks' PMA. The remaining four records did not contribute to the pooled estimate. This category covers records lacking the composite outcome at the required timepoint in an intention-to-treat-reconstructible form, and secondary/companion reports of an already-included trial that were deliberately excluded to **avoid double-counting the same infants**. Readers should note that the pooled n of 1,312 therefore reflects seven trials, not eleven records.
- **Effect-measure handling.** The pre-specified measure (Risk Ratio) matched the measure detected in the data; `effect_measure_conflict` was null and `native_pooling_used` was **true**. The pooled value is a confirmed native-scale risk ratio. **No standardized fallback was substituted for the primary result.**
- **Dual metrics in this report.** Study-level estimates appear in this report in the standardized Hedges' g metric (Section 5) because that metric underpins the distribution plot, the bubble chart and the regression-based bias diagnostics (including the trim-and-fill adjusted value of −0.0238). These standardized values are **not** the primary effect measure and must not be referred to as the pooled result; the pooled result is the Risk Ratio of 0.921.
- **Single outcome group.** All seven contributing comparisons reported the same outcome at the same timepoint, so only one meta-analytic group was formed. No outcomes required separate pooling, and no outcome group was marked as having insufficient data.
- **Small-study-effect risk graded moderate** despite non-significant Egger's test (p = 0.5204) and minimal trim-and-fill imputation (k₀ = 1), because both diagnostics are underpowered at k = 7. This grading is retained as stated and is not downgraded on the basis of the non-significant test results.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Limited number of trials and participants.** Seven trials and 1,312 infants provide moderate but not definitive precision. The analysis cannot exclude a clinically relevant benefit (up to ~20% relative risk reduction) or a modest harm (up to ~6% relative increase) on the composite outcome.
2. **No moderator analysis.** With k = 7, meta-regression and subgroup analyses were not statistically defensible. Effects of exact cumulative dose, day of initiation within the first week, gestational-age stratum, baseline BPD risk, antenatal steroid or surfactant exposure, and treatment era could not be examined. Residual clinical heterogeneity within the eligibility window is therefore unquantified, even though statistical heterogeneity was low.
3. **Underpowered publication-bias diagnostics.** The "moderate" small-study-effect grading reflects the inherent insensitivity of Egger's test and trim-and-fill at small k. The non-significant asymmetry test is not evidence of absent reporting bias, and the field's history of early-terminated and possibly unreported corticosteroid trials is a specific concern.
4. **Outcome definition variability.** BPD was defined by oxygen requirement/oxygen dependence at 36 weeks' PMA, but trials differed in whether a physiological oxygen-reduction test was applied, in oxygen-saturation targets, and in local thresholds for supplemental oxygen. Such definitional drift can attenuate or inflate measured treatment effects.
5. **Composite outcome interpretation.** Death and BPD are combined by design, which protects against competing-risk artefacts but obscures component-specific effects. This review did not analyse mortality or BPD-in-survivors separately, so it cannot determine whether the near-null composite masks offsetting component effects.
6. **No long-term outcomes.** The review was restricted to the 36-week pulmonary composite. Neurodevelopmental outcomes — the pivotal consideration for early systemic dexamethasone — and short-term adverse effects (hyperglycaemia, hypertension, gastrointestinal perforation, growth) were outside its scope. The findings must not be read as a statement about the overall safety or benefit–harm balance of the intervention.
7. **Risk of bias within trials.** Formal per-domain risk-of-bias assessment and GRADE certainty rating are not reported in this synthesis. Quasi-randomised designs were eligible, and unblinded or inadequately concealed allocation could bias clinician decisions about oxygen weaning — a subjective component of the outcome definition. Certainty in the pooled estimate should be regarded as no better than moderate, and plausibly low, on grounds of imprecision and unassessed within-study bias.
8. **No prospective registration.** The absence of a registered protocol raises the possibility of post hoc decisions in eligibility or analysis, although the effect measure here was pre-specified by the review question.

## 13. Conclusion

Across seven randomised comparisons enrolling 1,312 preterm infants, early (<7 days of postnatal age), low cumulative dose (<2 mg/kg) systemic dexamethasone did not significantly reduce the composite outcome of death or bronchopulmonary dysplasia at 36 weeks' postmenstrual age compared with placebo or no treatment: **pooled Risk Ratio 0.921 (95% CI 0.804–1.056; p = 0.239; k = 7; n = 1,312)**, a result that **favours neither arm**. Heterogeneity was low (I² = 16.29%; τ² = 0.005537; Q = 9.509, p = 0.1469), indicating that the trials were broadly consistent with one another. Small-study-effect diagnostics were reassuring (Egger's p = 0.5204; trim-and-fill k₀ = 1) but underpowered, and the overall risk of such effects was graded moderate.

The evidence is best characterised as **inconclusive**: a clinically worthwhile benefit has not been demonstrated, but neither has it been excluded. Because the established harms of early systemic dexamethasone are substantial and this synthesis provides no offsetting demonstration of pulmonary composite benefit at low cumulative dose, these data do not support routine early prophylactic use of low-dose systemic dexamethasone in preterm infants at risk of BPD. Larger, risk-stratified randomised trials reporting both the 36-week pulmonary composite and long-term neurodevelopmental outcomes are required before clinical recommendations can change.
