# Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors versus Placebo for Self-Rated Overall Premenstrual Symptom Severity in Premenstrual Syndrome and Premenstrual Dysphoric Disorder: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are the most widely recommended pharmacological treatment for premenstrual syndrome (PMS) and premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD), and are given continuously, in the luteal phase only, or at symptom onset. Most syntheses have pooled clinician-rated or composite endpoints; the effect on the woman's *own* global rating of her premenstrual symptoms at the end of treatment has been less consistently quantified.

**Objective.** To estimate the effect of SSRIs compared with placebo on self-rated overall premenstrual symptom severity at end of treatment in women with PMS or PMDD.

**Methods.** Randomised placebo-controlled trials of sertraline, fluoxetine, paroxetine, escitalopram, citalopram or comparable SSRIs in women with PMS/PMDD were eligible. One self-rated global or total measure per trial was extracted (daily symptom diary total such as the DRSP, Daily Symptom Rating Form, Penn Daily Symptom Report or Calendar of Premenstrual Experiences total; a visual analogue scale total; or the Patient Global Evaluation), at the last treatment cycle or the cycle designated primary. Means, SDs and n per arm were extracted; SE/SEM were converted (SD = SE × √n); change-from-baseline values were used only when no end value was reported. All scales were oriented so that a higher score denotes more symptoms, with reverse-signing where necessary. Clinician-rated scales, physical-symptom-only subscales, adverse events, dropouts and functional impairment were not pooled. Effect sizes were Hedges'-g-corrected standardized mean differences (SMD) pooled under a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Twelve studies were retained after screening; eleven contributed a poolable self-rated global effect size (N = 1,435 randomised participants). The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was **−0.188 (95% CI −0.540 to 0.163; p = 0.2933; k = 11)**. In the pipeline's raw sign convention (treatment minus control), this point estimate **favours SSRI (continuous, luteal-phase, or symptom-onset dosing)**, but the confidence interval crosses the null, so no statistically significant difference from placebo was demonstrated for this outcome in this pooled analysis. Heterogeneity was very high and statistically significant (Q = 69.037, df = 10, p < 0.001; I² = 90.27%; τ² = 0.304845). Eight of the eleven study-level estimates were negative (favouring SSRI), seven of them with intervals excluding zero and a median g of −0.475 among the eight; two estimates (g = +1.080 and g = +1.027) were large, precise and in the opposite direction and account for most of the dispersion. Egger's test was not significant (intercept −0.652, p = 0.2767); trim-and-fill imputed 2 studies and attenuated the pooled estimate to g = −0.0765. Overall publication-bias risk was rated moderate.

**Conclusions.** The majority of trials of self-rated global premenstrual symptom severity show a small-to-moderate benefit of SSRIs over placebo, but the pooled random-effects estimate is not statistically significant because of extreme between-study heterogeneity driven by two strongly discordant trials. The pooled value of −0.188 should therefore be read as an unstable summary of a genuinely heterogeneous evidence base rather than as evidence of no effect; the discordant estimates require verification of scale orientation and arm assignment before the summary is treated as definitive.

## 1. Introduction

Premenstrual syndrome (PMS) and its severe, DSM-defined variant premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) affect a substantial minority of women of reproductive age and are characterised by affective, cognitive and somatic symptoms that recur in the late luteal phase and remit shortly after the onset of menses. Because the disorder is defined by prospectively self-recorded symptoms, the woman's own rating of her overall premenstrual symptom burden has particular construct validity as a primary endpoint — arguably more so than clinician-administered depression scales, which were developed for a different population and a different symptom structure.

Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) have a strong mechanistic rationale in PMDD: symptom relief is typically rapid (within one cycle, and often within days), which is inconsistent with the delayed response seen in major depressive disorder and has motivated three distinct dosing strategies — continuous daily dosing, luteal-phase-only (intermittent) dosing, and symptom-onset dosing. Each strategy has been tested against placebo, but trials differ widely in the self-report instrument used (Daily Record of Severity of Problems [DRSP], Daily Symptom Rating Form, Penn Daily Symptom Report, Calendar of Premenstrual Experiences [COPE], visual analogue scales, Patient Global Evaluation), in how the "end of treatment" cycle is defined, and in whether totals, subscales or change scores are reported.

This review addresses a deliberately narrow question: **in women with PMS or PMDD, what is the effect of SSRIs (continuous, luteal-phase or symptom-onset dosing) compared with placebo on the woman's own overall rating of premenstrual symptoms at end of treatment?** Restricting the synthesis to one self-rated global or total measure per trial avoids both double-counting and the silent substitution of clinician-rated endpoints, at the cost of a smaller evidence base.

### 1.1 Note on effect direction and sign convention

Two mirror-image conventions are in play and are stated explicitly here to prevent misreading. Under the *protocol* convention, a **positive** Hedges' g means the SSRI group reported fewer or less severe overall premenstrual symptoms than placebo. Under the *analysis pipeline's raw statistical* convention — treatment minus control on a scale where higher = more symptoms — a **negative** value favours the SSRI. All numerical results in this report are presented in the pipeline's raw convention (negative favours SSRI); the protocol-convention equivalent is obtained by sign-flipping (pooled g = −0.188 ⇔ protocol g = +0.188). Throughout, the arm favoured by each estimate is stated using the analysis system's own `favors` designation rather than being inferred from the sign.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

The review and its reporting follow the PRISMA 2020 statement. The review question, eligibility criteria, outcome definition, data-conversion rules and effect-measure choice were specified in advance of synthesis.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Women meeting criteria for PMS or PMDD (prospectively confirmed where reported).

**Intervention.** Any SSRI — sertraline, fluoxetine, paroxetine, escitalopram, citalopram or a pharmacologically similar agent — administered continuously, during the luteal phase only, or at symptom onset.

**Comparator.** Placebo.

**Outcome.** Self-rated overall premenstrual symptom severity at end of treatment, defined as the last treatment cycle or the cycle reported by the trialists as primary. Exactly **one** self-rated global or total measure was used per trial, selected in the following priority order: (i) a daily symptom diary total (DRSP, Daily Symptom Rating Form, Penn Daily Symptom Report, COPE total); (ii) a visual analogue scale total; (iii) the Patient Global Evaluation. The following were explicitly **not** pooled: clinician-rated scales (HAM-D, MADRS, CGI), physical-symptom-only subscales, adverse-event counts, dropout/withdrawal counts, and functional-impairment measures.

**Design.** Randomised, placebo-controlled trials (parallel-group or crossover with usable first-period or end-of-treatment data).

### 2.3 Information sources and search

Bibliographic records were retrieved from the electronic literature sources available to the review pipeline, supplemented by inspection of the reference lists of prior systematic reviews of SSRIs for PMS/PMDD. The search combined terms for the population (premenstrual syndrome, premenstrual dysphoric disorder, PMS, PMDD, late luteal phase dysphoric disorder), the intervention (SSRI, serotonin reuptake inhibitor, and the individual drug names), and the design (randomised, randomized, placebo, double-blind). No language restriction was applied at the search stage.

### 2.4 Study selection

Records were screened on title and abstract against the eligibility criteria, and potentially eligible reports were assessed in full text. Narrative and systematic reviews, duplicate records and secondary/companion reports of an already-included trial were removed so that each randomised sample contributed at most once to the synthesis.

### 2.5 Data extraction

For each included trial the following were extracted: the self-rated global instrument and the treatment cycle to which it refers; and the mean, standard deviation and analysed n for the SSRI and placebo arms. Standard errors or SEMs were converted to standard deviations as SD = SE × √n. Change-from-baseline values were used **only** where no end-of-treatment value was reported, and only where a change SD was available for both arms. Instruments on which a higher score denotes clinical improvement were reverse-signed so that, in every included trial, a higher score denotes **more** premenstrual symptoms.

### 2.6 Effect measures and synthesis

The pre-specified effect measure was the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, computed as Hedges' g with the small-sample bias correction, together with its 95% confidence interval. The effect measure was set by explicit user/protocol request, and **native pooling on the SMD scale was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`): the reported pooled value is a confirmed SMD, not a fallback conversion from another metric, and no effect-measure conflict was recorded.

Effect sizes were pooled using a **random-effects model with REML estimation** of the between-study variance, which was considered the only defensible model given the diversity of instruments, dosing regimens and populations. Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q, the I² statistic and τ².

### 2.7 Assessment of small-study effects

Funnel-plot asymmetry was assessed by Egger's regression test, and the sensitivity of the pooled estimate to potentially missing studies by the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. A qualitative overall risk rating (low / moderate / high) was assigned by integrating these signals.

## 3. Search and Screening

Screening and de-duplication yielded **12 studies** that met the population, intervention and comparator criteria. Of these, **11 contributed a usable self-rated global or total effect size** (one mean, one SD and one n per arm) and entered the quantitative synthesis; **one study was retained as an included report but could not be pooled**, because no self-rated global/total endpoint satisfying the outcome definition was extractable in the required mean/SD/n form (for example, where only clinician-rated scales, subscale-level data, or a responder dichotomy were reported). This is a reconciliation of the pipeline counts: `n_studies = 12` versus `n_effect_sizes = 11`.

The eleven pooled trials together randomised **1,435 participants**. Study-level analysed samples ranged from 26 to 234 (median 109), indicating an evidence base in which a small number of moderately sized multicentre trials sit alongside several small single-centre trials.

## 5. Effect Sizes

The eleven study-level standardized mean differences span a very wide range, from **g = −0.851** (study_8_R8, the smallest trial, N = 26) to **g = +1.080** (study_9_R9, N = 101) — a spread of almost two standard-deviation units.

The distribution is clearly bimodal rather than merely dispersed (see the effect-size distribution plot, `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`):

- **A dominant cluster of eight negative estimates** (study_1_R1, study_2_R2, study_5_R5, study_6_R6, study_7_R7, study_8_R8, study_10_R10, study_11_R11) ranging from −0.851 to −0.323, with a **median of −0.475**. Seven of these eight have confidence intervals that exclude zero; only study_7_R7 (−0.323, 95% CI −0.701 to 0.056) is individually inconclusive. This cluster is highly internally consistent and corresponds to a small-to-moderate advantage for the SSRI on the woman's own global symptom rating.
- **One near-null estimate**, study_3_R3 (+0.103, 95% CI −0.336 to 0.541), which is statistically indistinguishable from no difference.
- **Two large positive estimates**, study_9_R9 (+1.080, 95% CI 0.663 to 1.498) and study_12_R12 (+1.027, 95% CI 0.265 to 1.788), whose intervals exclude zero in the *opposite* direction, i.e. they indicate fewer self-rated premenstrual symptoms in the **placebo** arm. Study_9_R9 is of moderate size and relatively precise; study_12_R12 is the second-smallest trial (N = 30) and correspondingly imprecise.

These two discordant estimates are not merely statistical noise — study_9_R9's interval lies entirely above +0.66 — and they are the single most important feature of this dataset. Their magnitude (approximately one standard deviation favouring placebo) is implausible on pharmacological grounds and is of the kind most commonly produced by a **scale-orientation or arm-labelling error at the extraction stage**: an instrument on which higher = improvement that was not reverse-signed, or a Patient Global Evaluation coded in the improvement direction. This possibility cannot be resolved from the summary data available here and is flagged as a priority for source verification (see §10 and §11).

The forest plot of all eleven estimates with the pooled summary is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`.

A single outcome/timepoint group was analysed; no additional `meta_analysis_groups` were defined, so one pooled estimate is reported.

The pooled **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) is −0.188 (95% CI −0.540 to 0.163; p = 0.2933)**. The point estimate **favours SSRI (continuous, luteal-phase, or symptom-onset dosing)**, corresponding in the protocol's mirror-image convention to g = +0.188 in favour of the SSRI, i.e. a small reduction in self-rated overall premenstrual symptom severity at end of treatment. However, the 95% confidence interval comfortably includes zero and extends to +0.163 on the placebo-favouring side, and the p-value of 0.2933 provides **no statistically significant evidence of a difference between SSRI and placebo on this particular pooled endpoint**.

Two cautions are essential to the correct reading of this number. First, the confidence interval is wide because of the between-study variance, not because of a shortage of participants: with 1,435 randomised women, a homogeneous dataset of this size would have estimated a g of this magnitude with a standard error near 0.053 rather than the observed ≈0.179. Second, an approximate **95% prediction interval**, derived from τ² = 0.304845 and the standard error of the pooled estimate, spans roughly **−1.50 to +1.12** — meaning that the model itself expects the true effect in a new, exchangeable trial to be anywhere from a large benefit to a large harm. A pooled estimate accompanied by a prediction interval of that width is not a usable summary of a single underlying treatment effect; it is a statement that the included studies do not share one.

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) makes this instability visible: the running estimate tracks in the SSRI-favouring region while the concordant trials accumulate and is pulled sharply toward and across the null as the two discordant estimates enter.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity is extreme and unambiguous:

- **Cochran's Q = 69.037 on 10 degrees of freedom, p < 0.001** — the null hypothesis of a common effect is decisively rejected (Q is nearly seven times its expected value under homogeneity).
- **I² = 90.27%** — approximately nine-tenths of the observed variability in effect estimates reflects genuine between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. This is in the "considerable heterogeneity" band by any conventional threshold.
- **τ² = 0.304845**, i.e. a between-study standard deviation of τ ≈ 0.552 standardized units. The dispersion between studies is roughly three times the magnitude of the pooled effect itself.

The heterogeneity summary figure (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`) displays these diagnostics together.

Crucially, the heterogeneity is **structural rather than diffuse**. The eight SSRI-favouring estimates are mutually compatible to a degree that would, on their own, generate modest heterogeneity (they lie in a narrow band from −0.85 to −0.32, most with overlapping intervals). Essentially all of the excess Q is contributed by study_9_R9 and study_12_R12, whose intervals do not overlap those of the majority cluster at all, with a smaller contribution from the near-null study_3_R3. In other words, the dataset behaves like two populations of studies: a coherent set showing small-to-moderate SSRI benefit, and a pair of outliers showing large placebo benefit.

Plausible sources of this bimodality, in descending order of likelihood:

1. **Data-handling / orientation errors.** As noted, an un-reverse-signed "higher = better" instrument (e.g. a Patient Global Evaluation or an improvement-rated VAS) would flip a moderate benefit into a moderate apparent harm — exactly the pattern seen.
2. **Instrument heterogeneity.** Daily diary totals, VAS totals and single-item global evaluations are not interchangeable measures of the same construct and have very different variances and responsiveness.
3. **Comparator performance.** Placebo response in PMDD trials is notoriously large and variable (commonly 30–50% responder rates); trials with exceptional placebo performance will attenuate or reverse the apparent drug effect.
4. **Clinical and design diversity.** Dosing regimen (continuous vs. luteal-phase vs. symptom-onset), agent and dose, number of treatment cycles, diagnostic confirmation (prospective vs. retrospective), crossover vs. parallel design, and the definition of the "end of treatment" cycle all differ across the set.

Given I² = 90.27%, a fixed-effect analysis would be indefensible and was not performed.

## 8. Moderators

No moderator analyses could be performed as pre-specified, because trial-level covariates (dosing regimen, SSRI agent, daily dose, number of treatment cycles, diagnostic criteria, instrument type, publication year, risk-of-bias domain ratings) were not supplied to the synthesis in machine-readable form. No meta-regression or formal subgroup analysis is therefore reported, and none should be inferred. This is a substantive gap: with I² = 90.27% and τ² = 0.304845, moderator analysis is precisely the analysis that this dataset most requires.

What can be reported is the relationship between effect size and precision, displayed in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`), in which marker area is proportional to study weight:

- The four largest trials (study_6_R6, N = 234; study_11_R11, N = 229; study_1_R1, N = 218; study_10_R10, N = 191) **all** yield negative estimates in a tight band from −0.661 to −0.382, every one with a confidence interval excluding zero. The large, well-powered trials are thus unanimous in favouring the SSRI, and their weighted average lies near −0.46.
- The two discordant positive estimates come from a moderate trial (study_9_R9, N = 101) and the second-smallest trial (study_12_R12, N = 30).
- The most extreme SSRI-favouring estimate (study_8_R8, g = −0.851) comes from the smallest trial (N = 26) and carries very little weight.

A descriptive, hypothesis-generating observation follows: restricted to the eight concordant trials, the study-level estimates have a median of **−0.475** and a range of **−0.851 to −0.323**. This is not a recomputed pooled estimate — no leave-out or outlier-excluded model was fitted by the analysis pipeline, and no such pooled value, confidence interval or p-value is claimed here. It is offered only to characterise where the bulk of the evidence sits, and it indicates that a sensitivity analysis excluding the two discordant trials would be expected to produce a substantially larger, substantially less heterogeneous, and most likely statistically significant SSRI-favouring estimate. Running that analysis formally, after source verification of the discordant trials, is the single highest-value next step.

## 9. Publication Bias

**Egger's regression test** for funnel-plot asymmetry gave an intercept of **−0.652 with p = 0.2767** — no statistically significant asymmetry. This result must be interpreted cautiously: with k = 11, Egger's test has low power, and a non-significant result does not exclude small-study effects. Its interpretation is further complicated here by the extreme heterogeneity, since τ² of this magnitude inflates the residual variance of the regression and makes asymmetry testing unreliable in principle.

The **trim-and-fill** procedure imputed **2 potentially missing studies** and produced an adjusted pooled estimate of **g = −0.0765**, i.e. an attenuation of roughly 60% relative to the observed −0.188. Because the observed estimate was already non-significant, the adjusted estimate is likewise non-significant and closer still to the null; direction is preserved but magnitude is not. Trim-and-fill under high heterogeneity frequently misattributes genuine between-study variation to selective publication, so the imputation here should be read as a sensitivity signal rather than as a bias-corrected truth.

Integrating these signals, the overall publication-bias risk is rated **moderate**. The two instruments disagree in emphasis — no significant asymmetry on formal testing, but non-trivial imputation and attenuation under trim-and-fill — and neither is well behaved in the presence of I² = 90.27%. Publication bias cannot be confidently excluded, and the pooled effect should be regarded as potentially slightly overestimated pending sensitivity analyses and a deliberate search of grey and unpublished literature (trial registries, regulatory submissions, dissertations). In a therapeutic area as heavily industry-sponsored as SSRI trials in PMDD, selective availability of neutral results is an a priori plausible concern independent of what these two tests show.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 Principal findings

Across eleven randomised placebo-controlled trials and 1,435 women with PMS or PMDD, the pooled random-effects Standardized Mean Difference for self-rated overall premenstrual symptom severity at end of treatment was **−0.188 (95% CI −0.540 to 0.163; p = 0.2933)**, a point estimate that **favours SSRI (continuous, luteal-phase, or symptom-onset dosing)** but does not reach statistical significance. Between-study heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 90.27%; τ² = 0.304845; Q = 69.037, df = 10, p < 0.001).

The central analytic message is that **this pooled number is a poor summary of its own input data**. Eight of eleven trials — including all four of the largest — report fewer self-rated premenstrual symptoms on SSRI than on placebo, with a median g of −0.475 and seven of eight intervals excluding zero. Two trials report the opposite with comparable confidence. A random-effects average of two mutually contradictory bodies of evidence lands near the null not because the treatment is inert but because the inputs disagree. Reporting "SSRIs did not differ from placebo" as the conclusion of this analysis would be a serious misreading; the defensible conclusion is that the evidence as assembled is internally inconsistent, with the weight of it, and all of the best-powered trials, favouring the SSRI.

### 10.2 Relationship to the wider literature

The concordant cluster of this dataset aligns closely with the established literature. Prior systematic reviews of SSRIs in PMS/PMDD, including large Cochrane syntheses, have consistently reported small-to-moderate benefits over placebo on premenstrual symptom endpoints, with standardized effects typically in the 0.3–0.5 range and with benefit demonstrable for both continuous and luteal-phase dosing. The median of −0.475 among the eight concordant trials here, and the −0.661 to −0.382 band among the four largest trials, sit squarely within that expectation. It is the two discordant estimates — not the majority cluster — that are anomalous relative to the field.

This observation strengthens the case that the discordance is methodological rather than clinical. Among the explanations canvassed in §7, the most parsimonious remains a **scale-orientation or arm-assignment error** affecting study_9_R9 and study_12_R12. The outcome specification for this review explicitly required reverse-signing of instruments on which a higher score indicates improvement (notably the Patient Global Evaluation and improvement-anchored VAS variants); a failure of that step in two trials would generate exactly the observed pattern of large, precise, oppositely-signed estimates. Until the source data for those two trials are re-verified, the pooled estimate of −0.188 should be treated as provisional.

### 10.3 Clinical interpretation

On the assumption that the concordant cluster reflects the true signal, a standardized effect in the region of 0.4–0.5 corresponds to a clinically meaningful though not transformative reduction in self-rated premenstrual symptom burden — broadly consistent with the symptomatic, non-curative role SSRIs occupy in PMDD management, and consistent with the high placebo response rates that characterise this indication. The present analysis does **not** permit any statement about the relative merits of continuous versus luteal-phase versus symptom-onset dosing, about differences between individual SSRIs, or about dose–response, because no moderator data were available. Nor does it speak to tolerability, discontinuation, functional outcomes or physical symptoms, all of which were excluded by design from the pooled endpoint; clinical decisions require those data alongside efficacy.

## 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No formal `validation_flags` were returned by the analysis pipeline for this synthesis, so no pipeline-adjudicated exclusions (reviews, duplicates, unclassifiable outcomes, separately-pooled effect measures or double-counted companion reports) are available to report. The following data-quality observations arise from the delivered results themselves and are stated plainly:

1. **One included study contributed no effect size.** Twelve studies were retained after screening but only eleven were pooled (`n_studies = 12`, `n_effect_sizes = 11`). One trial (label `study_4`, absent from the effect-size set) yielded no self-rated global/total outcome extractable as mean, SD and n per arm. Its exclusion from the pooled estimate is a partial, outcome-driven loss of evidence of unknown direction.
2. **Two effect estimates are directionally discordant with the remainder and with the prior literature.** study_9_R9 (g = +1.080, 95% CI 0.663 to 1.498) and study_12_R12 (g = +1.027, 95% CI 0.265 to 1.788) indicate large placebo advantage and together drive most of the I² = 90.27%. Scale orientation (reverse-signing of "higher = improvement" instruments) and arm labelling for these two trials have not been independently verified against source reports and should be audited before the pooled estimate is used.
3. **Effect measure is confirmed native.** The reported statistic is a **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** pooled natively on the SMD scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), specified by explicit request, with no recorded effect-measure conflict. It is not a fallback conversion.
4. **No moderator covariates were available**, so the heterogeneity documented above is described but unexplained.
5. **Derived quantities.** Where standard errors were reported instead of standard deviations, SDs were reconstructed as SE × √n; where no end-of-treatment value was reported, change-from-baseline values with change SDs in both arms were substituted. Both conversions introduce approximation, and the latter in particular alters the variance structure of the effect estimate.
6. **No risk-of-bias assessment is reported.** Domain-level risk-of-bias judgements (randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding, attrition, selective reporting) were not supplied and therefore neither GRADE certainty nor a bias-stratified sensitivity analysis could be produced.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Extreme, unexplained heterogeneity.** With I² = 90.27% and τ² = 0.304845, the pooled estimate summarises a distribution rather than a common effect. The approximate 95% prediction interval (≈ −1.50 to +1.12) spans substantial benefit through substantial harm and renders the pooled point estimate of limited practical use on its own.
2. **Unverified discordant estimates.** Two trials with precise, large, oppositely-signed effects dominate the heterogeneity. Their data orientation has not been source-verified; if they reflect extraction error, the entire pooled result changes materially.
3. **No sensitivity or outlier-excluded models were fitted.** No leave-one-out analysis, outlier-excluded pooled estimate, or influence diagnostics were computed, so the quantitative robustness of −0.188 to the discordant trials is unquantified. The descriptive median of −0.475 among the eight concordant trials is not a substitute for that analysis.
4. **Small number of studies.** k = 11 limits the power of Egger's test, the reliability of trim-and-fill, and the feasibility of meta-regression (conventionally ≥10 studies per covariate).
5. **Instrument heterogeneity within a single pooled endpoint.** Daily diary totals, VAS totals and single-item global evaluations were pooled together as "self-rated global symptom severity". They differ in granularity, recall window, variance and responsiveness, and standardization does not fully reconcile them.
6. **No subgroup evidence on dosing strategy.** The review question explicitly encompasses continuous, luteal-phase and symptom-onset dosing, but the synthesis cannot distinguish among them — a notable gap given that intermittent dosing is a defining feature of SSRI use in this indication.
7. **Moderate publication-bias risk.** Trim-and-fill imputed 2 studies and attenuated the estimate to g = −0.0765; grey and unpublished literature were not systematically searched.
8. **Narrow outcome scope.** Clinician-rated scales, physical-symptom subscales, functional impairment, adverse events and dropouts were excluded by design. The report therefore characterises self-rated global efficacy only and must not be read as a benefit–harm assessment.
9. **No certainty-of-evidence grading.** In the absence of risk-of-bias data, no GRADE rating is offered; informally, the combination of inconsistency (very serious) and imprecision (serious) would place certainty for this outcome at low or very low.

## 13. Conclusion

In women with PMS or PMDD, eleven randomised placebo-controlled trials (N = 1,435) yielded a pooled **Standardized Mean Difference of −0.188 (95% CI −0.540 to 0.163; p = 0.2933)** for self-rated overall premenstrual symptom severity at end of treatment. The point estimate **favours SSRI (continuous, luteal-phase, or symptom-onset dosing)**, but the interval includes the null, so this pooled analysis does not demonstrate a statistically significant difference from placebo on this endpoint.

That headline conceals a more informative pattern. Heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 90.27%; τ² = 0.304845; Q = 69.037, df = 10, p < 0.001) and structural: eight of eleven trials — including all four of the largest, each with an interval excluding zero — report a small-to-moderate SSRI advantage (median g = −0.475), while two trials report a large advantage for placebo and drive almost all of the dispersion. The non-significant pooled estimate is therefore better understood as the arithmetic consequence of averaging contradictory evidence than as evidence of equivalence. Publication-bias risk was rated moderate (Egger's p = 0.2767; trim-and-fill imputed 2 studies, adjusted g = −0.0765).

Three actions would change the strength of this conclusion: (i) source-verification of scale orientation and arm assignment in the two discordant trials; (ii) formal outlier-excluded and leave-one-out sensitivity analyses with full reporting of the resulting estimates and heterogeneity; and (iii) assembly of trial-level moderators — above all dosing regimen, agent and instrument type — to permit the meta-regression that a dataset with τ² of this magnitude demands. Until then, the honest summary is that the preponderance of evidence, and all of the best-powered trials, indicate that SSRIs reduce women's own ratings of overall premenstrual symptoms relative to placebo, but that the pooled estimate from the full set of trials is too heterogeneous to confirm it.
