# Selective Serotonin Reuptake Inhibitors for Self-Rated Overall Premenstrual Symptom Severity: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) are used continuously, during the luteal phase, or from symptom onset to treat prospectively diagnosed premenstrual syndrome (PMS) and premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD). This review assessed their effect on women’s self-rated overall premenstrual symptom severity at the end of treatment.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons evaluated an SSRI against placebo in women with prospectively diagnosed PMS or PMDD. The outcome was one eligible patient-reported global or overall symptom measure per trial at the last treatment cycle or designated primary cycle. Lower severity favored SSRI treatment. Study-level effects were expressed as bias-corrected standardized mean differences; the prespecified pooled statistic was the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**. A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were examined using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Twelve studies were represented in the review pipeline; seven independent effect sizes, including 1,169 participants, were meta-analyzed. The pooled SMD was **−0.4209150003596836** (95% CI **−0.537 to −0.305**; p = **1.2409110809543682 × 10⁻¹²**), favoring SSRI treatment. Heterogeneity was not detected (Q = **8.254**, p = **0.2201**; I² = **0.0%**; τ² = **0.0**). Egger’s intercept was **−0.9037** (p = **0.1107**), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀ = **0**), leaving the pooled estimate at **−0.4209**. These analyses provided no clear evidence of small-study effects and indicated a low assessed risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions:** Compared with placebo, SSRI treatment was associated with lower self-rated overall premenstrual symptom severity at the end of treatment, with a moderate standardized effect and no detected between-study heterogeneity. Interpretation is limited by the absence of study-level clinical descriptors, risk-of-bias judgments, dosing-strategy subgroup data, and complete numerical outcome data for five of the twelve studies represented in the pipeline.

## Introduction

Premenstrual syndrome (PMS) and premenstrual dysphoric disorder (PMDD) are characterized by recurrent symptoms arising in relation to the menstrual cycle. Because symptom severity fluctuates cyclically and the disorders are defined partly by their timing, prospective symptom recording is important for diagnosis and treatment evaluation. Selective serotonin reuptake inhibitors (SSRIs) may be administered throughout the menstrual cycle, during the luteal phase only, or from symptom onset. Their efficacy can be assessed directly through women’s own global or overall ratings of premenstrual symptom burden.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among women with prospectively diagnosed PMS or PMDD, what is the effect of SSRI treatment—administered continuously, during the luteal phase only, or from symptom onset—versus placebo on women’s self-rated overall premenstrual symptom severity at the end of treatment?

Under the specified direction of effect, negative values indicate lower symptom severity and favor SSRI treatment, whereas positive values indicate greater symptom severity with an SSRI and favor placebo.

## Search and Screening

Eligible studies compared an SSRI—including sertraline, fluoxetine, paroxetine, escitalopram, citalopram, or a similar agent—with placebo in women with prospectively diagnosed PMS or PMDD. Eligible dosing schedules were continuous, luteal-phase-only, and symptom-onset administration.

The outcome was restricted to the woman’s own overall rating of premenstrual symptoms at the end of treatment. One eligible self-rated global or overall symptom measure was selected per trial, such as the Daily Record of Severity of Problems total, Daily Symptom Rating Form total, Penn Daily Symptom Report total, Calendar of Premenstrual Experiences total, an overall visual analogue scale, or Patient Global Evaluation. The intended time point was the last treatment cycle or the cycle designated as primary by the study. End values were preferred. When only change scores were available, the eligible approach was to use mean change and its standard deviation in both arms; end values and change scores were not to be mixed across arms.

Clinician-rated outcomes, physical symptoms alone, adverse events, withdrawals or dropouts, and functional-impairment outcomes were outside the outcome definition. The supplied dataset did not report databases, search dates, full search strings, counts at each screening stage, reviewer procedures, or reasons for exclusion other than duplicate-fragment handling. A complete PRISMA flow diagram therefore cannot be reconstructed from the available information.

The review pipeline identified **12 studies** and **7 analyzable effect sizes**. Thus, seven studies contributed to the quantitative synthesis, while five studies represented in the pipeline did not contribute an analyzable effect estimate for the specified outcome based on the supplied results. The precise study-level reasons for this difference were not provided.

The listed study-level effects are bias-corrected standardized effects (Hedges’ *g*). The meta-analytic statistic specified in the supplied analysis was the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, and native standardized pooling was used.

Six of seven point estimates were negative and therefore favored SSRI treatment under the prespecified direction. Four comparisons had 95% confidence intervals wholly below zero:

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled **7 effects** from **1,169 participants**. The pooled **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** was:

> **SMD = −0.4209150003596836 (95% CI −0.537 to −0.305; p = 1.2409110809543682 × 10⁻¹²).**

According to the supplied direction field, this result **favored SSRI treatment (continuous, luteal-phase-only, or symptom-onset dosing)** over placebo. Because the confidence interval excluded zero and the p value was extremely small, the pooled result was statistically significant. On a conventional standardized-effect scale, the magnitude is consistent with a moderate reduction in overall symptom severity, although this descriptive interpretation should not be treated as a direct statement about a specific instrument’s raw-score change or clinical-response threshold.

A cumulative meta-analysis plot was generated at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/cumulative_plot.png`. It provides a graphical account of how the pooled estimate changes as studies are sequentially added; the supplied numerical results did not specify the ordering rule or provide stepwise cumulative estimates, so no further quantitative interpretation is made here.

## Heterogeneity

No between-study heterogeneity was detected by the fitted model:

- **Cochran’s Q = 8.254** with 6 degrees of freedom
- **Q-test p = 0.2201**
- **I² = 0.0%**
- **τ² = 0.0**

The Q test did not reject homogeneity, I² attributed none of the observed variability to detected between-study heterogeneity, and the REML estimate of between-study variance was zero. The heterogeneity summary is available at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

These statistics indicate that the observed effects were statistically compatible with a common underlying standardized effect. They do not establish that the studies were clinically identical, nor do they rule out heterogeneity with certainty: heterogeneity tests have limited power when only seven studies are available, and the absence of detailed trial characteristics prevents assessment of important clinical differences.

## Moderators

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−0.9037** with **p = 0.1107**. This test was not statistically significant and therefore provided no clear evidence of small-study effects or funnel-plot asymmetry. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies and left the pooled estimate at **−0.4209**. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **low**.

These findings are reassuring but not definitive. With only seven studies, Egger’s test has limited power, and trim-and-fill depends on assumptions about the source and geometry of asymmetry. Selective outcome reporting also cannot be excluded from these analyses, especially because study protocols, prespecified analysis plans, and outcome inventories were not supplied.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis found that SSRIs reduced women’s self-rated overall premenstrual symptom severity relative to placebo at the end of treatment. Across seven independent comparisons involving 1,169 participants, the pooled SMD was −0.4209150003596836 (95% CI −0.537 to −0.305). The result favored SSRI treatment and was statistically robust in the fitted model. The direction of most study-level estimates was consistent with the pooled result, and five individual confidence intervals lay wholly below zero.

The absence of detected heterogeneity is notable because the eligibility criteria encompassed multiple SSRIs and three clinically distinct administration strategies. One possible interpretation is that SSRI benefits on overall self-rated symptoms were reasonably consistent across the included comparisons. However, that inference remains tentative. With only seven effects, statistical power to detect heterogeneity was constrained, τ² can be estimated as zero even when modest variation exists, and the supplied information did not allow direct comparison of dosing regimens or agents.

The selected outcome is clinically relevant because it captures women’s own assessments of their overall symptom experience rather than clinician impressions, isolated physical symptoms, adverse events, discontinuation, or functional impairment. Standardization allowed conceptually similar global symptom instruments to be synthesized despite differing numerical scales. The trade-off is that the pooled SMD does not directly indicate how many points symptoms changed on any one questionnaire or how many women achieved a clinically important response.

The analysis specifically concerns symptom severity at the designated end-of-treatment assessment. It does not establish long-term persistence of benefit after treatment cessation. It also does not compare SSRIs directly with one another, determine the optimal dose or schedule, quantify harms, or resolve whether continuous, luteal-phase, and symptom-onset strategies differ in efficacy.

## Limitations

Several limitations constrain interpretation:

1. **Incomplete search documentation:** Databases, registers, search dates, search strategies, language restrictions, grey-literature methods, and reviewer procedures were not supplied. Search completeness and full PRISMA flow cannot therefore be independently assessed.
2. **Incomplete study characterization:** The specific agents, doses, administration schedules, treatment durations, diagnoses, instruments, endpoint definitions, and arm-level sample sizes were unavailable.
3. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** Study-level judgments for randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, missing outcome data, selective reporting, and other potential biases were not provided.
4. **Partial quantitative contribution:** Seven effect sizes were available from 12 studies represented in the pipeline. Without study-level reasons, the possibility of availability or reporting bias cannot be fully assessed.
5. **Combined intervention strategies:** Continuous, luteal-phase-only, and symptom-onset dosing were pooled, but their comparative or subgroup effects could not be evaluated.
6. **Standardized outcome:** The SMD facilitates synthesis across instruments but is less directly interpretable than a pooled effect on a single familiar clinical scale.
7. **Limited power for secondary diagnostics:** Seven studies provide limited power for heterogeneity tests, Egger’s regression, and detection of selective dissemination.
8. **No direct clinical-threshold analysis:** The available results do not identify responder rates, minimal clinically important differences, functional benefits, or benefit–harm trade-offs.
9. **End-of-treatment focus:** Durability after treatment and longer-term outcomes were not evaluated.

## Conclusion

In women with prospectively diagnosed PMS or PMDD, SSRI treatment administered continuously, during the luteal phase, or from symptom onset reduced self-rated overall premenstrual symptom severity compared with placebo at the end of treatment. The REML random-effects pooled **Standardized Mean Difference was −0.4209150003596836** (95% CI **−0.537 to −0.305**; p = **1.2409110809543682 × 10⁻¹²**), favoring SSRI treatment. No statistical heterogeneity was detected (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), and publication-bias diagnostics provided no clear evidence of small-study effects. The finding supports SSRI efficacy for the pooled intervention category, but the optimal agent and dosing schedule, the clinical importance on individual symptom scales, the balance of benefits and harms, and the certainty of evidence cannot be determined from the supplied aggregate results.
