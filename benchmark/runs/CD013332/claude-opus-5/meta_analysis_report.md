# Implantable Gentamicin–Collagen Sponge and Superficial Sternal Surgical Site Infection After Cardiac Surgery: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Superficial surgical site infection (SSI) of the sternotomy wound is a common complication of cardiac surgery, prolonging hospital stay and consuming substantial resources. An implantable gentamicin-impregnated collagen sponge placed in the presternal tissues at closure delivers high local antibiotic concentrations and has been proposed as an adjunct to systemic prophylaxis, but trial results have been discordant.

**Objective.** To determine whether, in adults undergoing cardiac surgery via median sternotomy, an implantable gentamicin–collagen sponge placed in the sternal wound at closure reduces the incidence of *superficial* sternal SSI compared with no topical antimicrobial (standard closure, placebo or usual care). Deep sternal wound infection/mediastinitis and combined total-SSI figures were explicitly outside the target outcome.

**Methods.** Randomized and quasi-randomized comparative trials reporting superficial sternal SSI events per randomized arm were eligible. Five trials met criteria and contributed five effect estimates. The pre-specified effect measure was the risk ratio (RR); pooling was performed on the native RR scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Because one trial reported its result only as an odds ratio, outcome estimates were partitioned by reported effect measure and analysed in separate groups rather than merged. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** The primary pooled analysis (superficial sternal SSI, RR-scale group) comprised k = 4 trials and 4,662 randomized participants. The pooled risk ratio was 0.604 (95% CI 0.344 to 1.061; p = 0.0793), a result that did not reach conventional statistical significance; the direction field for this analysis records the result as favouring **neither arm** (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure whose favourability depends on which event was counted). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant (Q = 12.837, df = 3, p = 0.005; I² = 71.59%; τ² = 0.224193 on the log scale, τ ≈ 0.474). A second outcome group, comprising the single trial (n = 720) that reported an odds ratio, was flagged as having insufficient data to pool; its standardized single-study estimate was g = −0.2545 and no pooled value could legitimately be computed for that group. Egger's regression showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept = −0.4235, p = 0.787) and trim-and-fill imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized estimate −0.2793), giving an overall publication-bias risk rating of "low".

**Conclusions.** Across four randomized trials in 4,662 adults, the gentamicin–collagen sponge was associated with a point estimate suggesting roughly a 40% relative reduction in superficial sternal SSI, but the confidence interval crossed the null and the analysis is formally inconclusive; the direction classification is "neither arm favoured". Substantial unexplained heterogeneity (I² = 71.59%) and the exclusion of one trial from the pool on effect-measure grounds further limit confidence. The evidence is insufficient to establish — or to exclude — a clinically important benefit of the sponge for superficial sternal SSI specifically.


## 1. Introduction

Median sternotomy remains the standard access route for the majority of adult cardiac surgical procedures. Despite systemic antibiotic prophylaxis, perioperative glycaemic control, decolonisation protocols and modern skin-preparation regimens, sternal wound complications continue to occur in approximately 1–8% of patients, depending on case mix and surveillance intensity. The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) distinguishes *superficial incisional* SSI — infection involving only the skin and subcutaneous tissue of the incision — from *deep incisional* and *organ/space* infection, the latter encompassing mediastinitis. The two strata differ materially in their pathophysiology, microbiology, treatment burden and prognosis: superficial infection is predominantly a skin-flora, soft-tissue event managed with dressings and oral or short-course intravenous antibiotics, whereas deep sternal wound infection carries attributable mortality and often requires debridement, sternal re-fixation or flap reconstruction.

This distinction is directly relevant to the rationale for topical antimicrobial prophylaxis. An implantable gentamicin-impregnated collagen sponge, laid in the presternal subcutaneous plane or between the sternal halves at closure, releases gentamicin at local tissue concentrations one to two orders of magnitude above those achievable systemically, with rapid decay and minimal systemic exposure. Because the drug is deposited precisely in the tissue compartment where superficial incisional infection arises, the superficial stratum is arguably where the largest relative effect should be expected. Yet the published trial literature has been dominated by analyses of deep sternal infection or of a composite "any sternal wound infection" endpoint, and the superficial-only estimates — which are often reported as secondary outcomes — have been pooled inconsistently or not at all.

The present systematic review and meta-analysis addresses a single, deliberately narrow question: **in adults undergoing cardiac surgery via sternotomy, does an implantable gentamicin–collagen sponge placed in the sternal wound at closure, compared with no topical antimicrobial, reduce the incidence of superficial surgical site infection?** Deep sternal wound infection, mediastinitis and combined total-SSI counts were not eligible as the target outcome.

## 2. Methods: Search and Screening

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they: (i) enrolled adults (≥18 years) undergoing cardiac surgery through a median sternotomy; (ii) randomized or otherwise prospectively allocated participants to an implantable gentamicin–collagen sponge placed in the sternal wound at the time of closure versus no topical antimicrobial (standard closure, placebo sponge, or usual care); (iii) reported the incidence of *superficial* sternotomy-wound surgical site infection as events and totals per allocated arm, defined either by CDC criteria or by the trial's own explicit definition of superficial sternal wound infection; and (iv) reported data sufficient to derive a risk ratio or an equivalent contrast.

Studies were ineligible if the only infection outcome reported was deep sternal wound infection/mediastinitis, or an undifferentiated composite of superficial plus deep infection, with no separately extractable superficial stratum. Narrative and systematic reviews, editorials, conference abstracts without extractable arm-level counts, and duplicate reports of an already-included trial were excluded.

### 2.2 Information sources and screening

Records were identified through electronic bibliographic searching and citation-chaining of prior reviews of local antibiotic prophylaxis in cardiac surgery, combining controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for the intervention (*gentamicin-collagen sponge*, *gentamicin implant*, *Collatamp*, *Garamycin sponge*, *Sulmycin implant*), the population (*cardiac surgery*, *sternotomy*, *coronary artery bypass*, *median sternotomy*) and the outcome (*surgical site infection*, *sternal wound infection*, *wound complication*). Titles and abstracts were screened against the eligibility criteria, and full texts of potentially eligible records were assessed. Reviews and duplicate trial reports were removed at the screening stage so that each randomized population contributed only once to any pooled estimate.

Screening yielded **five eligible comparative trials** contributing **five effect estimates** to the analytic dataset. The deterministic record retained for this analysis preserves the five included trials and their arm-level data but does not retain the intermediate database-level record counts; accordingly, no PRISMA flow-diagram numerator counts (records identified, screened, excluded by reason) are reported here, and none have been inferred. This is stated explicitly rather than reconstructed, in keeping with PRISMA 2020 item 16a.

### 2.3 Data extraction and effect-measure handling

For each trial, the number of superficial sternal SSI events and the number of randomized participants were extracted for both arms. The **pre-specified and authoritative effect measure for this review was the risk ratio (RR)**, consistent with the review question (incidence of a relatively uncommon binary event) and with the explicit analytic request governing this synthesis (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`).

One important handling decision follows from this. One included trial reports its superficial-SSI contrast explicitly as an **odds ratio** rather than a risk ratio. The analytic record flags this as an effect-measure conflict ("user requested RR, but the paper(s) explicitly report OR — user request kept as authoritative"). Rather than silently converting that trial's OR to an RR, or pooling incommensurable ratio measures, the synthesis partitioned the superficial-SSI estimates into two outcome groups keyed by reported effect measure. Each group is reported with its own k, pooled effect, confidence interval and heterogeneity; the groups are **not** merged into a single overall number.

### 2.4 Statistical analysis

Pooling was performed on the native RR scale (`computation_scale = RR`, `native_pooling_used = true`), i.e. log risk ratios were combined and the result back-transformed; the pooled statistic reported below is therefore a genuine **Risk Ratio (RR)**, not a standardized mean-difference surrogate. A random-effects model was fitted with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of τ². Heterogeneity was quantified by Cochran's Q with its p-value, the I² statistic, and τ² (and its square root τ) on the log-RR scale. Small-study effects and potential publication bias were examined using Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

For descriptive and diagnostic purposes, each trial's contrast was additionally expressed as a standardized effect (Hedges' g) with a 95% confidence interval; these standardized values follow a raw treatment-minus-control sign convention and are used in this report only for study-level description, funnel-plot diagnostics and the trim-and-fill sensitivity estimate. **Directionality of every pooled result is reported using the analysis's own `favors` field, not inferred from the sign or magnitude of the point estimate.**

## 3. Included Studies

Five trials were included, enrolling a combined 5,382 randomized participants across both effect-measure groups. All compared an implantable gentamicin-impregnated collagen sponge placed in the sternal wound at closure against no topical antimicrobial, with superficial sternotomy-wound SSI as the extracted outcome. Trial sizes ranged from 542 to 1,950 randomized participants (median 720), so the evidence base is dominated by two large trials (n = 1,950 and n = 1,502) that together account for 64% of all randomized participants.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity in the primary RR-scale analysis was **substantial and statistically significant**: Cochran's Q = 12.837 on 3 degrees of freedom, p = 0.005; I² = 71.59%; τ² = 0.224193 on the log-RR scale, implying τ ≈ 0.474.

Interpreted substantively, I² = 71.59% indicates that roughly 72% of the observed variability in effect estimates across the four pooled trials is attributable to real differences in underlying treatment effect rather than to within-study sampling error. Under common conventions this falls in the "substantial" to "considerable" band. A τ of 0.474 on the log scale means that the standard deviation of true log risk ratios across settings is itself about half a log unit — i.e. the plausible range of *true* effects across populations spans a multiplicative factor of roughly 2.5 in either direction from the central estimate. An approximate 95% prediction interval computed on the normal approximation spans roughly RR 0.20 to 1.79; with only four studies and a t-based correction it is far wider still (approximately 0.06 to 6.5). Either way, the prediction interval is so wide as to be uninformative about what effect a new trial in a new setting would observe. With k = 4, τ² is itself estimated with very poor precision, and these prediction-interval figures should be treated as illustrative rather than as quantitative guidance.

The most plausible drivers of the dispersion, in descending order of likely contribution, are:

1. **Differing definitions and surveillance intensity for "superficial" infection.** The superficial stratum is the most definition-sensitive of the SSI categories. Trials using active, protocol-driven wound review at fixed intervals will record substantially more superficial events than trials relying on passive reporting or on readmission-based ascertainment, and the mix of ascertainment methods across trials can change both the baseline rate and the measured relative effect.
2. **Baseline risk and case mix.** Relative effects of local antimicrobials commonly attenuate at low baseline event rates. Differences in the proportion of bilateral internal mammary artery harvests, diabetes prevalence, obesity, and urgency of surgery alter baseline superficial SSI risk and therefore the scope for benefit.
3. **Sponge placement technique and number of sponges.** Placement between the sternal halves versus in the presternal subcutaneous plane, and the use of one versus two sponges, alters the tissue compartment receiving high gentamicin concentrations — and the superficial compartment is not always the one targeted.
4. **Background systemic prophylaxis and local resistance patterns.** Where systemic prophylaxis is already highly effective against the prevailing skin flora, or where gentamicin resistance among coagulase-negative staphylococci is prevalent, the marginal benefit of local gentamicin shrinks.
5. **Blinding and outcome adjudication.** Unblinded wound assessment can bias the superficial endpoint in either direction, since the diagnosis rests substantially on clinical signs rather than on a microbiological gold standard.

Given k = 4, no formal subgroup or meta-regression analysis of these candidate explanations was statistically defensible, and none was performed. The heterogeneity is therefore reported as **substantial and unexplained**.

## 7. Moderator Analyses

No moderator analyses were conducted. With four trials in the primary pool, meta-regression would have at most two residual degrees of freedom per covariate, giving essentially no power to detect a moderator effect and a high risk of spurious findings; conventional guidance is that at least ten studies per covariate are required. Subgroup analyses by definition of superficial SSI, sponge placement, blinding status, background prophylaxis regimen or baseline event rate were likewise not estimable. The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) is presented descriptively — as a precision-versus-effect display — and not as a fitted meta-regression. The absence of moderator analysis means that the substantial heterogeneity documented in Section 6 remains entirely unexplained, which is itself an important finding for the interpretation of this evidence base.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry gave an intercept of **−0.4235** with **p = 0.787**, providing no evidence of asymmetry. The Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure imputed **zero** missing studies (k₀ = 0); the adjusted standardized estimate of **−0.2793** therefore reflects no adjustment to the observed configuration of studies. The overall publication-bias risk rating for this analysis is **low**.

The accompanying interpretation reads: *"Egger's regression test shows no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.787), indicating that effect sizes are distributed symmetrically with respect to their standard errors — i.e., no detectable small-study effect. Consistent with this, the trim-and-fill procedure imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the pooled estimate is unchanged after adjustment, supporting the overall 'low' risk-of-bias rating."* The same source adds an important caveat: *"these tests have limited statistical power, particularly when fewer than ~10 studies are pooled or when heterogeneity is high, so this result argues against detectable publication bias rather than conclusively ruling it out; findings are best corroborated by trial-registry/grey-literature searching."*

Both conditions of that caveat apply here with full force: only four studies were pooled, and I² was 71.59%. Egger's test is known to be both underpowered and poorly calibrated in this regime, and trim-and-fill performs unreliably when heterogeneity is high because it can mistake genuine between-study variation for asymmetry (or vice versa). The correct conclusion is therefore the narrow one: **no publication bias was detectable**, not that publication bias is absent. Given that superficial SSI is frequently a secondary endpoint in these trials, selective *outcome* reporting — the non-reporting of a disaggregated superficial stratum in trials whose superficial result was unremarkable — is a more plausible threat here than the non-publication of whole trials, and neither Egger's test nor trim-and-fill is capable of detecting it.

## 9. Discussion

This synthesis addressed a deliberately specific question: the effect of an implantable gentamicin–collagen sponge on *superficial* sternotomy-wound infection, as distinct from deep sternal infection or composite SSI counts. Four randomized trials in 4,662 adults yielded a pooled risk ratio of 0.604 (95% CI 0.344 to 1.061; p = 0.0793) — a point estimate consistent with a substantial, clinically meaningful relative reduction, but an interval that includes no effect and a direction classification of **neither arm favoured**. The honest summary is that this body of evidence is *inconclusive* for the superficial endpoint.

Three features of the result deserve emphasis.

First, **the point estimate and the inference diverge**. An RR of 0.604 would, if real, translate into a worthwhile absolute reduction: at a baseline superficial SSI incidence of 4%, it implies an absolute reduction of about 1.6 percentage points and a number needed to treat of roughly 63. But a confidence interval reaching 1.061 also admits no benefit at all, and with τ ≈ 0.474 the prediction interval for a future trial spans both appreciable benefit and appreciable harm. Clinicians and guideline developers should not convert the 39.6% relative reduction into a recommendation; the appropriate reading is that the sponge *may* reduce superficial sternal SSI, with the trial evidence currently unable to confirm or refute it.

Second, **the heterogeneity is the dominant analytic feature**, not a nuisance parameter. With Q = 12.837 (p = 0.005) and I² = 71.59%, the four trials are not estimating a single common effect. One large trial in the pool (g = −0.5946) and the second-largest (g = +0.0340) have non-overlapping intervals. When trials of comparable size and comparable nominal intervention disagree to that degree, the explanation usually lies in outcome definition and ascertainment rather than in the biology of drug delivery — and superficial incisional SSI is precisely the endpoint most vulnerable to definitional drift. Before another meta-analysis of this question is attempted, the field would benefit far more from standardised, blinded, CDC-anchored superficial-SSI adjudication than from additional trials.

Third, **the biological plausibility argument cuts in a specific direction**. Local gentamicin deposited in the presternal soft tissues should, in principle, act most directly on the compartment where superficial infection originates. The fact that the superficial estimate remains statistically inconclusive, while much of the literature's attention has centred on deep sternal infection, suggests either that the local gentamicin concentration achieved in the subcutaneous plane is insufficient against the prevailing flora (including gentamicin-resistant coagulase-negative staphylococci), that the sponge's placement in many trials targeted the retrosternal rather than the subcutaneous plane, or simply that the superficial endpoint has been measured too noisily for a true effect to emerge. These possibilities cannot be distinguished with k = 4.

Two further considerations bear on practice. The sponge is a foreign body; a minority of reports have raised the possibility that an implanted collagen matrix could itself serve as a nidus or provoke sterile wound discharge that is then classified as superficial infection — a mechanism that would bias the superficial endpoint specifically toward the null or toward harm. And the local use of an aminoglycoside at supratherapeutic tissue concentrations has antimicrobial-stewardship implications, including selection pressure for gentamicin resistance in skin flora, that a trial powered for infection incidence is not designed to capture. Neither consideration is quantified by the present analysis, but both are relevant to a decision that the present analysis cannot resolve on efficacy grounds alone.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

The following data-handling and validation issues arose in this synthesis and are stated plainly:

- **One trial was excluded from the primary pool on effect-measure grounds.** A conflict was recorded: the review's pre-specified and authoritative effect measure was the risk ratio, but one included trial (n = 720) reports its superficial-SSI contrast explicitly as an **odds ratio**. The user-specified RR was retained as authoritative, and rather than converting or silently merging incommensurable ratio measures, the superficial-SSI estimates were partitioned into two outcome groups keyed by reported measure. As a consequence, the primary pooled analysis rests on 4 of the 5 included trials and 4,662 of the 5,382 randomized participants.
- **One outcome group had insufficient data to pool.** The OR-scale group contains a single trial (k = 1, n = 720) and is flagged `insufficient_data = true`. No pooled effect, confidence interval or heterogeneity statistic is reported for that group, because none can be computed from one study. Only its standardized single-study estimate (g = −0.2545) is recorded.
- **Study-level standardized estimates and the pooled native-scale result use different sign conventions and are not directly commensurable.** The per-study Hedges' g values in Section 4 carry a raw treatment-minus-control convention; the pooled statistic is a back-transformed risk ratio. Readers should not attempt to reconcile the sign of the g values with the position of the RR relative to 1.0. The authoritative statement of which arm a result favours is taken from the analysis's `favors` field in every case, and for the primary analysis that field records **neither arm**.
- **Trim-and-fill output is reported on the standardized scale.** The adjusted value of −0.2793 is not a risk ratio and should not be read as one; because k₀ = 0, no adjustment to the pooled RR was made in any event.
- **Reviews and duplicate trial reports were removed at screening** so that no randomized population contributes more than once to a pooled estimate. Intermediate database-level screening counts were not retained in the analytic record, so no PRISMA flow-diagram numerators are reported; none have been reconstructed or estimated.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Small number of pooled studies.** With k = 4 in the primary analysis, τ² is imprecisely estimated, the random-effects confidence interval may be anticonservative, prediction intervals are unstable, and all small-study-effect diagnostics are underpowered.
2. **Substantial, unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 71.59% with a significant Q test, and no capacity to investigate sources via subgroup analysis or meta-regression. The pooled risk ratio should be read as a summary of discordant results rather than as an estimate of a common effect.
3. **Incomplete pooling of the eligible evidence.** The effect-measure conflict removed one trial (n = 720, 13.4% of randomized participants) from the primary pool. Had that trial been convertible and included, both the pooled estimate and the heterogeneity statistics would differ, in a direction that cannot be determined without re-analysis on a common scale.
4. **Outcome-definition heterogeneity for the superficial stratum.** "Superficial sternal wound infection" is applied inconsistently across cardiac-surgical trials, and ascertainment is highly sensitive to surveillance intensity, follow-up duration and blinding of wound assessors. This is the single most likely source of the observed dispersion and is not adjustable in the present data.
5. **No formal risk-of-bias assessment is reported at the domain level.** Trial-level judgements on randomization, allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessors and completeness of follow-up were not retained in the analytic record, so the certainty of evidence could not be graded domain by domain. Given that the superficial endpoint is clinically adjudicated, lack of assessor blinding is a particular concern.
6. **Publication and selective-reporting bias cannot be excluded.** The low risk rating reflects the absence of *detectable* asymmetry in a four-study funnel, not the demonstrated absence of bias. Selective non-reporting of a disaggregated superficial stratum is undetectable by these methods.
7. **No absolute-risk or safety synthesis.** Pooled baseline event rates, absolute risk differences, numbers needed to treat, and harms (wound discharge, seroma, re-exploration, aminoglycoside resistance selection) were not within the scope of the extracted dataset. The absolute-benefit figures cited in the Discussion are illustrative calculations from an assumed baseline rate, not pooled estimates.
8. **Scope restriction by design.** Deep sternal wound infection/mediastinitis and composite total SSI were explicitly excluded as target outcomes. The present result says nothing about the sponge's effect on deep infection, which is the outcome of greatest clinical consequence and on which the balance of the published literature has concentrated.

## 12. Conclusion

In adults undergoing cardiac surgery via median sternotomy, pooling four randomized trials comprising 4,662 participants yielded a pooled **risk ratio of 0.604 (95% CI 0.344 to 1.061; p = 0.0793)** for superficial sternotomy-wound surgical site infection with an implantable gentamicin–collagen sponge versus no topical antimicrobial. The point estimate is compatible with a clinically important ~40% relative reduction, but the confidence interval crosses the null, the result does not reach statistical significance, and the direction classification for this analysis records **neither arm as favoured**. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant (Q = 12.837, df = 3, p = 0.005; I² = 71.59%; τ² = 0.224193) and could not be explained, as k = 4 precluded moderator analysis. A second outcome group, comprising the one trial that reported an odds ratio (k = 1, n = 720), had insufficient data to pool and is reported as such. No publication-bias asymmetry was detectable (Egger's p = 0.787; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0), with the standard caveat that these tests are underpowered at this k and heterogeneity level.

The evidence is therefore **insufficient either to establish or to exclude a clinically important benefit** of the gentamicin–collagen sponge for superficial sternal SSI specifically. Routine adoption cannot be justified on the strength of this endpoint alone, nor can the intervention be dismissed. Resolving the question will require adequately powered trials that pre-specify superficial incisional SSI as a distinct endpoint using CDC criteria, employ blinded outcome adjudication, standardise sponge placement plane and dose, report arm-level event counts on a common ratio scale, and record gentamicin-susceptibility data for isolated organisms.
