# Local Implantable Gentamicin–Collagen Sponge for Prevention of Superficial Sternal Surgical-Site Infection After Cardiac Surgery: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Superficial surgical-site infection (SSI) after cardiac surgery through a sternotomy may cause substantial morbidity. This review evaluated whether placing an implantable gentamicin–collagen sponge in the sternal wound during closure reduces superficial SSI compared with no topical antimicrobial.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons involved adults undergoing cardiac surgery through a sternotomy, local implantation of a gentamicin-impregnated collagen sponge at closure, and a control receiving no topical antimicrobial; equivalent systemic prophylaxis was permitted. The target outcome was superficial sternal SSI as defined by each trial, excluding deep SSI, mediastinitis, and combined or total SSI. The supplied dataset identified 5 studies and 4 effect-size records. Three studies (3,994 participants) contributed to the random-effects Risk Ratio (RR) meta-analysis, estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). One additional 720-participant record was kept separate because the source reportedly expressed the effect as an odds ratio (OR), creating a measure-classification conflict, and it was insufficient for a separate pooled analysis.

**Results:** The pooled Risk Ratio was 0.642180418310201 (95% CI 0.304–1.356; p = 0.2456401185152552; k = 3; n = 3,994). The confidence interval included the null, and the supplied favorability classification was neither arm/undetermined. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 79.37%; τ² = 0.328172; Q = 11.959, p = 0.0025). The separate single-study group (n = 720) could not be pooled. Egger’s intercept was −0.5122 (p = 0.6669), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀ = 0); these diagnostics were classified as indicating low publication-bias risk, although their power was severely limited by the small evidence base.

**Conclusions:** The available pooled evidence does not establish that local gentamicin–collagen sponge implantation reduces superficial sternal SSI. The estimate is imprecise and highly heterogeneous. Interpretation is further constrained by unavailable arm-level event counts, incomplete study-level bibliographic and methodological information, one effect-measure conflict, and too few studies for dependable publication-bias or moderator analyses.

## Introduction

Median sternotomy is a common approach for cardiac surgery. Even when confined to skin and subcutaneous tissues, a superficial sternal surgical-site infection (SSI) can prolong recovery, increase antimicrobial use and health-care utilization, and potentially precede more serious wound complications. Standard prevention commonly includes systemic perioperative antibiotic prophylaxis and sterile operative technique. A gentamicin-impregnated collagen implant is intended to supplement these measures by delivering a high local antimicrobial concentration while the collagen carrier is absorbed.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among adults undergoing cardiac surgery through a sternotomy, what is the effect of placing an implantable gentamicin–collagen sponge locally in the sternal wound during closure, compared with no topical antimicrobial, on superficial sternal SSI?

The prespecified target was a single dichotomous outcome—superficial SSI of the sternotomy wound, as defined by each trial. Deep SSI, mediastinitis, and combined or total SSI outcomes were not eligible substitutes. For Risk Ratios (RRs), values below 1 would conventionally correspond to fewer superficial SSI events in the sponge group, whereas values above 1 would correspond to fewer events in the control group. Formal statements of favorability below follow the classification supplied by the analysis pipeline rather than being inferred independently from the numerical estimate.

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

The review question implies the following eligibility framework:

- **Population:** Adults undergoing cardiac surgery through a sternotomy.
- **Intervention:** Implantable gentamicin–collagen sponge, or gentamicin-impregnated collagen implant, placed locally in the sternotomy wound during closure.
- **Comparator:** No topical antimicrobial in the sternotomy wound. Usual care and equivalent systemic antibiotic prophylaxis were permitted.
- **Outcome:** Superficial SSI of the sternotomy wound, reported as event/no event according to each trial’s definition.
- **Excluded outcomes:** Deep sternal SSI, mediastinitis, and combined or total SSI counts.
- **Effect measure requested:** Risk Ratio.

## Meta-Analytic Results

### Main Risk Ratio group

Three studies involving 3,994 participants were pooled with a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** was **0.642180418310201** (95% CI **0.304–1.356**; **p = 0.2456401185152552**).

The 95% confidence interval crossed the null value of 1, and the result was not statistically significant at the conventional two-sided 0.05 level. In accordance with the supplied `favors` field, the pooled result was classified as favoring **neither arm (tie or undetermined)**. Thus, the synthesis does not provide conclusive evidence of a reduction in superficial SSI with the gentamicin–collagen sponge.

### Separate single-study group

One study involving 720 participants was assigned to a separate group because its source reportedly expressed the result as an OR, whereas the requested analysis measure was RR. The pipeline retained RR as authoritative and labeled the group **Risk Ratio (RR)**, but explicitly recorded this source-measure conflict. With **k = 1**, the group had insufficient data for meta-analysis; no pooled Risk Ratio, confidence interval, heterogeneity estimate, or pooled favorability judgment should be inferred.

The two groups were appropriately not merged into one overall estimate because doing so would obscure the effect-measure classification problem.

## Heterogeneity

For the three-study RR meta-analysis:

- **I² = 79.37%**;
- **τ² = 0.328172**;
- **Cochran’s Q = 11.959**; and
- **Q-test p = 0.0025**.

With k = 3, the Q statistic has 2 degrees of freedom. These results indicate substantial inconsistency and statistically detectable between-study heterogeneity. The pooled estimate should therefore be interpreted as an average across results that may differ materially rather than as a common effect expected in every setting.

Potential explanations could include differences in patients’ baseline infection risk, operation type or urgency, systemic prophylaxis, implant dose or placement, wound closure, superficial-SSI definitions, surveillance periods, and trial conduct. These explanations remain hypothetical because corresponding study-level covariates were not supplied.

## Moderators

No prespecified subgroup analysis or meta-regression result was provided. With only three studies in the main synthesis and one in the separate group, moderator analysis would in any case be underpowered and vulnerable to spurious ecological associations. No conclusion can be drawn about whether effects differ according to operation type, diabetes, obesity, systemic antibiotic regimen, sponge dose, closure technique, SSI definition, or follow-up duration.

## Publication Bias

The supplied small-study-effect diagnostics were:

- **Egger’s intercept = −0.5122**;
- **Egger’s test p = 0.6669**;
- **trim-and-fill k₀ = 0**; and
- **trim-and-fill diagnostic estimate = −0.2473** on the pipeline’s diagnostic scale.

Egger’s test was not statistically significant, and trim-and-fill did not impute missing studies. The pipeline therefore classified publication-bias risk as **low**. However, this should not be interpreted as proof that reporting bias is absent. Tests for funnel-plot asymmetry have very low power with such a small number of studies, and standard guidance generally discourages relying on them when fewer than approximately 10 studies are available (Sterne et al., 2011). The scale of the reported trim-and-fill diagnostic estimate was not confirmed as an RR and should not be interpreted as a clinically meaningful adjusted Risk Ratio.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

The random-effects synthesis of three studies and 3,994 participants produced a pooled Risk Ratio of 0.642180418310201, but its 95% CI was wide (0.304–1.356), included no effect, and corresponded to p = 0.2456401185152552. Following the pipeline’s formal classification, the pooled result favored neither arm/was undetermined. A further 720-participant study could not be combined because it formed a single, measure-conflicted group.

The apparent uncertainty is not explained solely by sampling error. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 79.37%; τ² = 0.328172), suggesting that the studies did not estimate a uniform effect. Consequently, even the average pooled result may have limited transportability to a particular cardiac-surgical setting.

### Clinical interpretation

The evidence does not establish that implanting a gentamicin–collagen sponge during sternotomy closure reduces superficial SSI relative to no topical antimicrobial. Nor does it establish equivalence or absence of benefit: the confidence interval is compatible with both a potentially meaningful reduction and an increase in risk. The intervention should therefore not be considered proven effective for this specific superficial-SSI outcome on the basis of the supplied synthesis alone.

The review question was deliberately restricted to superficial SSI. Results for deep sternal wound infection, mediastinitis, or composite SSI would answer different clinical questions and cannot be substituted, even if those outcomes were more frequently reported. Safety outcomes, antimicrobial resistance, gentamicin-related adverse effects, reoperation, mortality, resource use, and cost-effectiveness were outside the supplied analysis and should also inform clinical decisions.

### Relation to the evidence base

The evidence base is difficult to contextualize because trial citations and clinical characteristics were absent. In particular, it is unknown whether heterogeneity reflects differences in modern versus historical practice, systemic prophylaxis, patient selection, implant technique, outcome definitions, or risk of bias. Access to complete trial reports and extraction forms is necessary before drawing firm conclusions for guidelines or routine practice.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Several data constraints materially affect interpretation:

1. The pipeline listed 5 studies but only 4 effect-size records; no reason was supplied for the missing fifth effect.
2. Arm-specific superficial-SSI event counts and randomized denominators were unavailable, preventing independent reconstruction or verification of trial RRs.
3. The principal synthesis was explicitly labeled and natively pooled as Risk Ratio, but the supplied study-level entries were standardized values and were not independently reconcilable with event data.
4. One 720-participant report was said to provide an OR, creating a conflict with the requested RR classification. It was retained as a separate, unpooled group and was not merged with the three-study RR synthesis.
5. Study identities, outcome definitions, follow-up periods, and risk-of-bias assessments were unavailable.
6. Deep SSI, mediastinitis, and combined/total SSI figures were not used as substitutes for the target superficial-SSI outcome.

## Limitations

This report has important limitations:

- **Nonreproducible search:** Search sources, dates, strategies, restrictions, deduplication, and screening decisions were not supplied.
- **Incomplete PRISMA flow information:** Numbers of records identified, screened, excluded, and reasons for full-text exclusion could not be reported.
- **Missing trial-level event data:** Superficial-SSI events and arm-specific randomized totals—the core inputs for an RR meta-analysis—were unavailable.
- **Unverifiable outcome classification:** Trial definitions of superficial SSI and surveillance windows were not reported.
- **Effect-measure conflict:** One source reportedly used an OR but was classified under the requested RR framework; it was therefore not pooled with the main group.
- **Small evidence base:** Only three studies contributed to the pooled RR, limiting precision, heterogeneity assessment, moderator analysis, and publication-bias detection.
- **Substantial heterogeneity:** I² was 79.37%, reducing confidence that the pooled average represents a consistent intervention effect.
- **Risk of bias not assessed:** Randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, missing data, selective reporting, and other trial-level biases could not be evaluated.
- **No certainty rating:** A formal GRADE assessment was not possible from the supplied information.
- **Clinical outcomes beyond superficial SSI:** Harms, resistance, deep infection, mediastinitis, mortality, length of stay, and costs were not evaluated.

## Conclusion

Among adults undergoing cardiac surgery through a sternotomy, the available three-study random-effects synthesis did not establish a statistically significant effect of a locally implanted gentamicin–collagen sponge on superficial sternal SSI. The pooled Risk Ratio was **0.642180418310201** (95% CI **0.304–1.356**; p = **0.2456401185152552**; n = **3,994**), with substantial heterogeneity (**I² = 79.37%**). The supplied favorability classification was neither arm/undetermined. A separate 720-participant study had insufficient data for its own pooled analysis and could not be combined because of an effect-measure conflict.

Future updates should retrieve complete trial reports, verify superficial-SSI definitions, extract exact events and randomized totals by arm, reconcile OR and RR reporting from raw 2×2 data, assess risk of bias, and explore clinically justified sources of heterogeneity. Larger, well-reported trials using standardized superficial-SSI surveillance are needed before firm practice recommendations can be made.
