# Perioperative Levosimendan Versus Placebo for All-Cause Mortality After Cardiac Surgery: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Levosimendan has been evaluated perioperatively in adults undergoing cardiac surgery, but its effect on all-cause mortality remains uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of perioperative levosimendan compared with placebo on all-cause mortality, using one mortality comparison per eligible trial at the mortality time point reported by that trial.

**Methods:** The supplied evidence set contained 13 study records, of which nine contributed effect sizes to the primary meta-analysis. These nine comparisons included 1,842 randomized participants. Risk ratios (RRs) were pooled with a random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were evaluated with Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Nine trials (1,842 participants) contributed to the primary pool. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.6719425338101565 (95% CI 0.482 to 0.937; p=0.019244232284479416). Heterogeneity was not detected (Q=7.224, p=0.5127; I²=0.0%; τ²=0.0). The supplied result’s favorability field classified this estimate as “neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).” This designation conflicts with the prespecified coding of death as the event, under which an RR below 1 ordinarily corresponds to lower mortality with levosimendan; therefore, treatment-direction coding should be verified before drawing a directional clinical conclusion. Egger’s intercept was −0.2608 (p=0.6364), while trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study; the supplied overall publication-bias rating was moderate.

**Conclusions:** The native RR analysis produced a statistically significant pooled estimate below 1 with no detected heterogeneity. Nevertheless, an unresolved discrepancy in the supplied direction-of-effect classification, incomplete study-level mortality counts, and limited reporting of screening and risk-of-bias information constrain interpretation. Verification of event coding and reconstruction from trial-level death counts are warranted before treating this finding as definitive evidence of a survival benefit.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model fitted using **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** included **k=9** mortality comparisons and **n=1,842** participants. The pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** was:

- **RR = 0.6719425338101565**
- **95% CI 0.482 to 0.937**
- **p = 0.019244232284479416**

The confidence interval excluded the null RR of 1, and the corresponding test was statistically significant at the conventional two-sided 0.05 level.

The supplied `favors` field classified the pooled result as **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).”** This is the formal favorability designation attached to the analysis and is therefore retained here. It is inconsistent with the stated outcome coding—death as the event—and the prespecified convention that an RR below 1 favors levosimendan. This discrepancy prevents an unqualified directional conclusion until event and arm coding have been verified.

## Heterogeneity

No statistical heterogeneity was detected:

- **Cochran’s Q = 7.224** with **p = 0.5127**;
- **I² = 0.0%**; and
- **τ² = 0.0**.

These statistics indicate that the observed dispersion was no greater than expected from within-study sampling error under the fitted model. Nevertheless, an I² of 0% does not establish clinical equivalence across studies, especially when operative settings, participant risk, treatment schedules, mortality time points, and trial methods are unavailable. With only nine studies, Q also has limited power to detect modest between-study heterogeneity.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression yielded:

- **Intercept = −0.2608**;
- **p = 0.6364**.

This was not statistically significant and provided no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. The result should be interpreted cautiously because Egger’s test has limited power with only nine studies and can be affected by heterogeneity and effect-measure properties (Sterne et al., 2011).

Trim-and-fill estimated **one potentially missing study** (**k₀=1**) and reported an adjusted value of **−0.3387** on the pipeline’s stated “g” scale. Because this adjusted value is not labeled as an RR and is on a different scale from the native pooled RR, it cannot be interpreted as an adjusted mortality RR or directly compared with 0.6719425338101565. The supplied overall publication-bias risk classification was **moderate**. Taken together, publication bias cannot be ruled out despite the non-significant Egger test.

## Discussion

This synthesis of nine placebo-controlled trial comparisons and 1,842 participants produced a pooled **Risk Ratio of 0.6719425338101565 (95% CI 0.482 to 0.937; p=0.019244232284479416)** using an REML random-effects model. Statistical heterogeneity was absent by all supplied measures (I²=0.0%; τ²=0.0; Q p=0.5127).

The estimate is below the null value of 1 and statistically significant. However, the supplied analysis explicitly designated favorability as neither arm because event direction was considered ambiguous. Since the research question defines deaths as the events and provides the usual convention that an RR below 1 favors levosimendan, this internal inconsistency is material rather than cosmetic. Verification should include checking intervention/control orientation, event/non-event coding, zero-event handling, and conversion from any source risk differences to native study-level RRs.

Clinical interpretation is further limited by the absence of absolute event counts. A relative effect does not establish the absolute number of deaths prevented or caused; that quantity depends on baseline mortality risk. No risk difference or number needed to treat can be calculated responsibly without a verified control-event rate and trial-level death counts.

The lack of detected heterogeneity may suggest consistency, but it should not be overinterpreted. Four included study records did not contribute to the quantitative synthesis for undocumented reasons, mortality time points may differ, and the largest trial contributed 849 of 1,842 participants. Moreover, trial-level standardized effects supplied alongside the analysis are not expressed on the stated native RR scale. These issues prevent independent verification of the pooled estimate.

Publication-bias assessment was mixed: Egger’s test was non-significant, but trim-and-fill suggested one potentially missing study. The moderate bias rating is therefore reasonable, with the important qualification that trim-and-fill’s adjusted value was not provided on the RR scale and is not directly clinically interpretable.

No structured risk-of-bias judgments or certainty-of-evidence assessment was supplied. Accordingly, the statistical significance of the pooled estimate should not be equated with high-certainty evidence. A definitive appraisal would require trial-level randomization, allocation-concealment, blinding, attrition, selective-reporting, and outcome-measurement information, followed by a GRADE assessment.

- One document (`manual:eef74e83`) was excluded because it was a systematic review/network meta-analysis rather than a primary trial. This avoided double-counting synthesized evidence as though it were an original study.
- The pipeline listed 13 studies but only nine effect sizes. Reasons for the four non-contributing records were not supplied.
- The pooled analysis is labeled as a native **Risk Ratio (RR)** analysis, but the available study-level records are labeled **Hedges’ g**, and arm-specific deaths and denominators are absent. The RR analysis therefore cannot be independently reproduced from the supplied study-level data.
- The source papers reportedly used risk differences, while the user-requested RR was retained as authoritative. The transformation or reconstruction procedure was not provided.
- The supplied favorability designation was “neither,” despite the stated coding of all-cause death and a pooled RR below 1. Event and arm coding require verification.
- The trim-and-fill adjusted estimate was reported as −0.3387 on a “g” scale rather than as an RR; it must not be presented as an adjusted mortality risk ratio.

## Limitations

1. **Incomplete search documentation:** Databases, dates, search strings, duplicate removal, reviewer methods, and full-text exclusion reasons were unavailable.
2. **Unexplained attrition from evidence set to meta-analysis:** Thirteen study records were listed, but only nine entered the pool.
3. **Missing trial-level binary data:** Death counts by randomized arm were not supplied, preventing independent calculation of study-specific RRs, verification of intention-to-treat denominators, assessment of zero-event trials, and calculation of absolute effects.
4. **Effect-scale inconsistency:** Study-level records were expressed as Hedges’ g, while the pooled result was labeled as a native RR.
5. **Direction inconsistency:** The supplied favorability classification conflicts with the stated death-event convention and requires resolution.
6. **Potentially variable mortality time points:** Each trial contributed its reported mortality time point, which may combine clinically distinct follow-up periods.
7. **No trial-characteristic data:** Population, procedure, dosing, timing, and follow-up differences could not be examined.
8. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** Internal validity and certainty of evidence could not be graded.
9. **Limited power for bias and heterogeneity tests:** Nine studies provide weak power for Egger’s test, Cochran’s Q, and moderator analyses.
10. **Publication-bias scale mismatch:** The trim-and-fill estimate was not reported on the RR scale.

## Conclusion

Across nine trial comparisons involving 1,842 participants, the REML random-effects analysis yielded a pooled **Risk Ratio of 0.6719425338101565 (95% CI 0.482 to 0.937; p=0.019244232284479416)**, with no detected statistical heterogeneity (**I²=0.0%; τ²=0.0; Q=7.224, p=0.5127**). The supplied result classified favorability as neither arm, despite the stated death-event convention; this inconsistency must be resolved before a directional survival claim is made. Egger’s test was non-significant, but trim-and-fill suggested one missing study, supporting a moderate publication-bias concern. Overall, the result is statistically notable but requires verification against trial-level mortality counts, harmonized follow-up, and formal risk-of-bias assessment before it can support a definitive clinical conclusion.
