# High-dose calcium supplementation during pregnancy and preterm birth before 37 weeks: a dose-stratified systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** This review evaluated high-dose calcium supplementation during pregnancy, compared with placebo or no treatment, for preventing preterm birth before 37 completed weeks, restricted to the dose-stratified high-dose subgroup. Non-dose-stratified and timing-stratified (“started to take calcium”) outcomes were excluded.

**Methods:** The supplied evidence set contained 11 studies and seven eligible effect-size records. Six records (13,568 participants) were combined using a restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) random-effects model on the native risk-ratio scale. One additional record (524 participants) was kept separate because its source report explicitly used an odds ratio, although the requested measure was a risk ratio. Study-level event counts, arm denominators, bibliographic details, search sources, and risk-of-bias assessments were not supplied and therefore could not be reconstructed or invented.

**Results:** For the six-study risk-ratio group, the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.6890309824789995 (95% CI 0.453 to 1.047; p=0.08130690501603177). The supplied result classified the direction as neither arm favored/tie or undetermined. Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=86.42%; τ²=0.161749; Q=14.881, p=0.0109). The separate single-study group (n=524) had insufficient data for pooling; its pipeline-standardized value was −0.370701, but this is not a pooled RR and should not be interpreted as one. Egger’s intercept was −0.039 (p=0.0147), indicating funnel-plot asymmetry; trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀=0), with an adjusted standardized value of −0.312. Publication-bias risk was rated high.

**Conclusions:** The pooled estimate did not reach conventional statistical significance, its confidence interval included the null value of RR=1, and between-study heterogeneity was high. Funnel-plot asymmetry and incomplete reporting of trial-level events, study methods, and risk of bias further reduce confidence. The evidence does not establish a statistically conclusive effect of dose-stratified high-dose calcium supplementation on preterm birth before 37 weeks.

## Introduction

Preterm birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation is an important cause of neonatal morbidity and mortality. Calcium supplementation during pregnancy has been investigated as a preventive intervention, but estimates can differ according to calcium dose and the timing or basis of subgroup classification. Combining non-equivalent outcome definitions or subgroup analyses can therefore yield a clinically misleading summary.

This review addressed the following focused question: among pregnant participants, what is the effect of high-dose calcium supplementation, compared with placebo or no calcium supplementation/no treatment, on the dichotomous occurrence of preterm birth before 37 completed weeks of gestation in the **dose-stratified high-dose subgroup**? In accordance with the prespecified scope, the analysis excluded both the non-dose-stratified outcome and the timing-stratified outcome described as “started to take calcium.” The requested estimand was the Risk Ratio (RR), for which values below 1 would ordinarily correspond to fewer preterm births in the calcium group. For reporting the actual meta-analytic direction, however, this report follows the supplied result’s explicit classification of favorability.

Eligibility was restricted to:

- preterm birth before 37 completed weeks as a dichotomous outcome; and

The non-dose-stratified analysis and the timing-stratified (“started to take calcium”) analysis were excluded by design. ## Effect Sizes

The prespecified effect measure was the **Risk Ratio (RR)**. Native RR pooling was reported as having been used for the six-study principal group. The random-effects model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML).

The supplied data also contained standardized study-level fields labeled as Hedges’ g. Those values appear to be pipeline representations and are not numerically interchangeable with RRs. They were therefore not used here to reinterpret the native-scale pooled Risk Ratio (RR), calculate event rates, or infer absolute effects. In particular, no baseline control risk was provided, so absolute risk differences and numbers needed to treat cannot be calculated reliably.

A second group contained one study whose paper reportedly used an odds ratio even though RR was the user-requested measure. The pipeline retained the requested label “Risk Ratio (RR)” as authoritative but flagged this conflict. With only one study, no meta-analysis was performed for that group. Its supplied single standardized effect value was −0.370701; this is not a pooled RR and does not permit a native RR interpretation in the absence of the underlying event counts.

## Heterogeneity

For the six-study Risk Ratio (RR) meta-analysis:

- **I² = 86.42%**
- **τ² = 0.161749**
- **Cochran’s Q = 14.881**
- **Q-test p = 0.0109**

These results indicate substantial inconsistency among study estimates, with statistically detectable heterogeneity. The high I² means that the pooled average should not be treated as though it described a uniform intervention effect across all settings. The estimated between-study variance was also non-zero and appreciable on the model’s analysis scale.

## Publication Bias

The supplied publication-bias diagnostics were:

- **Egger’s intercept = −0.039**
- **Egger’s test p = 0.0147**
- **Trim-and-fill estimated missing studies, k₀ = 0**
- **Trim-and-fill adjusted standardized value = −0.312**
- **Overall publication-bias risk classification: high**

Egger’s test was statistically significant, indicating funnel-plot asymmetry and possible small-study effects or publication bias. Funnel asymmetry is not specific to publication bias and may also arise from genuine heterogeneity, methodological differences, or chance. This is especially important here because heterogeneity was high.

Trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies, but this does not negate the significant Egger result or rule out selective publication and reporting. Moreover, the reported trim-and-fill value is on a standardized-effect representation and should not be interpreted as an adjusted Risk Ratio (RR). Given the small number of pooled studies (k=6), asymmetry tests and trim-and-fill are intrinsically unstable. The supplied overall judgment of **high publication-bias risk** is therefore retained, with cautious interpretation.

## Discussion

This review focused narrowly on preterm birth before 37 completed weeks among pregnant participants assigned to high-dose calcium in a dose-stratified analysis. Six studies with 13,568 participants contributed to the native-scale REML meta-analysis. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.6890309824789995 (95% CI 0.453 to 1.047; p=0.08130690501603177). The interval crossed RR=1, and the result was not statistically significant at the 0.05 level. Consistent with the supplied directional classification, the synthesis is reported as favoring neither arm/tie or undetermined.

The uncertainty around the average estimate is compounded by substantial heterogeneity (I²=86.42%; Q p=0.0109). This suggests that differences among the included studies were important and that the average pooled estimate may not transfer directly to every clinical population. Without trial characteristics, arm-level events, and risk-of-bias information, the causes and implications of this variation cannot be resolved.

The evidence base also raised outcome-measure compatibility concerns. A seventh record with 524 participants was not pooled because the paper explicitly reported an odds ratio, conflicting with the requested RR. Keeping that record separate avoided mixing effect measures or treating an OR as though it were an RR. It also means that the principal pooled result does not summarize all 14,092 participants represented by the seven eligible effect records.

Publication-bias diagnostics warrant additional caution. Egger’s test suggested asymmetry (p=0.0147), whereas trim-and-fill imputed no studies. These findings are not mutually exclusive: trim-and-fill has limited power and strong assumptions, particularly with few studies and high heterogeneity. Neither method can determine whether selective publication actually occurred.

Clinically, the available synthesis does not establish a statistically conclusive reduction or increase in preterm birth before 37 weeks. A non-significant result should not be interpreted as proof of no effect. Rather, the compatible range represented by the confidence interval remains broad, and confidence in the pooled average is constrained by inconsistency, possible small-study effects, and incomplete reporting.

## Data Quality and Validation Notes

- The supplied pipeline listed 11 studies but only seven eligible effect-size records; reasons for the difference were not provided.
- Six records were pooled as native Risk Ratios, while one record was separated because its paper explicitly reported an odds ratio despite the requested RR.
- The one-study group had insufficient data for meta-analysis; no pooled value was invented.
- Trial-level event counts and randomized denominators by arm were absent, preventing independent calculation or verification of intention-to-treat RRs.
- The context supplied standardized study-effect fields in addition to a native RR meta-analysis. These standardized values were not substituted for the requested RR.
- The non-dose-stratified and timing-stratified (“started to take calcium”) outcomes were excluded and were not used to fill missing information.
- No author-level identifiers or trial reports were available to assess duplicate populations, overlapping reports, or selective outcome reporting.

## Limitations

First, the search and selection process was incompletely documented. Databases, dates, search strategies, deduplication procedures, reviewer processes, and exclusion reasons were unavailable, preventing full reproducibility and a complete PRISMA flow account.

Second, no study-level event counts or arm denominators were supplied. The requested extraction of preterm-birth events and total randomized in each arm could therefore not be reported or independently checked. This also precluded calculation of absolute risks and assessment of zero-event handling.

Third, bibliographic and clinical study characteristics were absent. The report cannot characterize participants, countries, intervention doses, treatment duration, adherence, baseline calcium intake, comparator implementation, or outcome-assessment methods.

Fourth, no structured risk-of-bias assessments were provided. Randomization, allocation concealment, masking, attrition, outcome measurement, selective reporting, and other potential biases remain unassessed.

Fifth, heterogeneity was high, yet moderator analyses were unavailable. The pooled RR should therefore be understood as an average across potentially diverse study effects rather than a universally applicable effect.

Sixth, publication-bias tests were based on few studies and may have been calculated from standardized effect representations rather than directly from log RRs. The significant Egger test and trim-and-fill result should therefore be interpreted as sensitivity signals, not definitive corrections to the pooled RR.

Finally, the single study with an outcome-measure conflict could not be converted to RR without event data. Excluding it from pooling preserved metric validity but reduced the comprehensiveness of the quantitative synthesis.

## Conclusion

In the dose-stratified high-dose calcium subgroup, six studies involving 13,568 participants yielded a pooled **Risk Ratio (RR) of 0.6890309824789995 (95% CI 0.453 to 1.047; p=0.08130690501603177)** for preterm birth before 37 completed weeks. The supplied direction classification was neither arm favored/tie or undetermined. Heterogeneity was substantial (**I²=86.42%**), and Egger’s test indicated funnel-plot asymmetry (**p=0.0147**), with publication-bias risk rated high. One additional study (n=524) had insufficient data for separate pooling and was not combined because of an explicit OR-versus-RR measure conflict.

Overall, the available evidence does not provide a statistically conclusive estimate of benefit or harm. Better reporting of trial-level events, randomized denominators, study characteristics, and risk of bias—and access to the original trial reports—would be required for a fully reproducible and clinically definitive assessment.
