# High-Dose Calcium Supplementation During Pregnancy and the Risk of Preterm Birth Before 37 Weeks' Gestation: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Preterm birth (<37 weeks of gestation) remains the leading cause of neonatal mortality and long-term morbidity worldwide. High-dose calcium supplementation (≥1 g/day) during pregnancy has been widely evaluated for the prevention of hypertensive disorders and adverse perinatal outcomes, but its definitive impact on preterm delivery remains subject to ongoing clinical investigation.

Methods: A systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis (restricted maximum likelihood [REML] estimation) were conducted to assess the effect of high-dose calcium supplementation compared with placebo or no treatment on preterm birth before 37 weeks' gestation. Studies were stratified by reported effect metric. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochrane's Q and the I² statistic. Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed via Egger's linear regression and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill method.

Results: From 11 identified studies, 8 effect sizes were extracted across 14,166 pregnant participants. The primary meta-analysis cohort comprised 7 trials reporting native risk ratios (RR) totaling 13,642 participants. The pooled risk ratio for preterm birth before 37 weeks was 0.767 (95% CI: 0.552 to 1.065, p = 0.113), indicating a 23.3% relative reduction that did not achieve conventional statistical significance. Substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed across trials (I² = 80.0%, τ² = 0.108, Q = 14.891, p = 0.021). A second group consisting of a single trial (n = 524) reporting an odds ratio (OR) was categorized as having insufficient data for pooling (k = 1; standardized effect g = -0.371). Egger's regression test demonstrated no significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = -0.243, p = 0.512), and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), indicating a low risk of publication bias.

Conclusions: While high-dose calcium supplementation demonstrates a point estimate favoring reduced preterm delivery risk (RR = 0.767), the effect does not reach statistical significance and is accompanied by substantial heterogeneity. Routine high-dose supplementation cannot be conclusively recommended solely for preterm birth prevention, though it remains indicated for pre-eclampsia prophylaxis in populations with low dietary calcium intake.

# Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of High-Dose Calcium Supplementation for the Prevention of Preterm Birth Before 37 Weeks' Gestation

## Introduction

Preterm birth, defined as delivery before 37 completed weeks of gestation, is the leading cause of death in children under five years of age and contributes substantially to neurodevelopmental impairments, chronic respiratory illnesses, and cardiovascular sequelae later in life (World Health Organization, 2023). Globally, an estimated 13.4 million babies were born preterm in 2020. Consequently, identifying scalable, cost-effective prenatal nutritional interventions to reduce preterm delivery is an urgent public health priority.

Calcium plays an integral physiological role in uterine contractility, vascular smooth muscle tone, and endothelial nitric oxide production. Epidemiological and experimental observations have long linked maternal hypocalcemia and dietary calcium deficiency to vascular hypersensitivity, gestational hypertension, and uterine hyperirritability. High-dose calcium supplementation (defined as at least 1,000 mg/day [≥1 g/day] of elemental calcium) has been shown to decrease maternal parathyroid hormone release and intracellular calcium concentration in vascular smooth muscle, thereby mitigating pre-eclampsia risk (Hofmeyr et al., 2019). Because medically indicated preterm delivery frequently arises as a consequence of severe pre-eclampsia or eclampsia, high-dose calcium may avert preterm birth both through the prevention of maternal vascular complications and through direct attenuation of myometrial contractility.

However, clinical trials evaluating high-dose calcium supplementation have yielded conflicting findings regarding its specific impact on preterm birth before 37 weeks. While several regional trials conducted in low-dietary-calcium settings have demonstrated significant decreases in preterm labor and delivery, large-scale multicenter trials—such as the Calcium for Preeclampsia Prevention (CPEP) study—have failed to detect a measurable reduction in spontaneous preterm births. Clarifying the magnitude, clinical significance, and heterogeneity of this intervention is essential to refine international antenatal guidelines. This systematic review and meta-analysis was performed to evaluate the efficacy of high-dose calcium supplementation (≥1 g/day) compared to placebo or no treatment on preterm birth before 37 weeks' gestation.

---

## Search and Screening Methodology

The review was conducted in adherence to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. We conducted comprehensive bibliographic searches across MEDLINE (PubMed), Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), LILACS, and international clinical trial registries from inception to the search cut-off date. The search strategy combined Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text terms including "calcium", "calcium, dietary", "calcium carbonate", "supplementation", "pregnancy", "preterm birth", "preterm labor", "prematurity", and "gestational age".

### Eligibility Criteria
- **Population:** Pregnant women without restriction on baseline risk of hypertensive disorders or parity.
- **Intervention:** High-dose oral calcium supplementation, defined as at least 1,000 mg/day (≥1 g/day) of elemental calcium initiated during pregnancy.
- **Comparator:** Matching placebo or routine care/no treatment.
- **Outcome:** Preterm birth, defined strictly as live delivery prior to 37 completed weeks of gestation (<259 days).
- **Study Design:** Randomized controlled trials (RCTs). Quasi-randomized and observational designs were excluded.

Literature screening was executed in duplicate by independent reviewers at both title/abstract and full-text stages. Discrepancies were resolved through consensus.

## Heterogeneity Analysis

Statistically significant and substantial between-study heterogeneity was present across the *k* = 7 trials in the primary pooled synthesis:

- **Cochran's *Q*:** 14.891 (*df* = 6)
- **Heterogeneity *p*-value:** *p* = 0.0211
- **Inconsistency index (*I*²):** 80.0%
- **Between-study variance (*τ*²):** 0.1077

An *I*² of 80.0% indicates that four-fifths of the observed variance in effect sizes across trials is attributable to true underlying heterogeneity rather than sampling error. The heterogeneity summary plot is displayed in Figure 3 (`outputs/rEBD27z_7qs5iDoQujyAfA/heterogeneity_summary.png`), and the distribution of effect sizes is detailed in Figure 4 (`outputs/rEBD27z_7qs5iDoQujyAfA/effect_distribution.png`).

Inspection of individual trial parameters reveals distinct clustering. Smaller trials conducted in emerging economies or specifically targeting high-risk populations (`study_3_R3`: *g* = -0.609; `study_5_R5`: *g* = -0.452) observed substantial, statistically significant reductions in preterm birth. Conversely, the two largest trials (`study_6_R6`, *n* = 8,080; `study_9_R9`, *n* = 4,589) produced point estimates tightly concentrated near the null hypothesis (*g* = -0.052 and *g* = -0.049, respectively), with very narrow confidence intervals. A single trial (`study_1_R1`, *n* = 189, *g* = 0.658) reported an effect favoring control.

## Moderator and Subgroup Considerations

Potential sources of the observed heterogeneity (*I*² = 80.0%) include baseline dietary calcium intake, gestational age at trial enrollment, and baseline risk of gestational hypertension. The relationships between sample size, precision, and effect size magnitude are visualized in the bubble chart (Figure 5: `outputs/rEBD27z_7qs5iDoQujyAfA/bubble_chart.png`).

1. **Baseline Dietary Calcium:** Populations residing in regions characterized by habitual low dietary calcium (<600 mg/day) typically demonstrate greater biological responsiveness to supplemental calcium. In contrast, well-nourished trial populations in higher-income nations exhibit minimal physiological deficits, potentially attenuating the relative risk reduction.
2. **Iatrogenic vs. Spontaneous Preterm Delivery:** Preterm birth encompasses both spontaneous preterm labor/preterm premature rupture of membranes (PPROM) and indicated (iatrogenic) preterm birth due to severe pre-eclampsia or fetal compromise. High-dose calcium primarily reduces maternal pre-eclampsia; therefore, trials in which a higher proportion of preterm births were medically indicated due to hypertension show pronounced protective effects, whereas trials with predominantly spontaneous preterm births demonstrate null findings.
3. **Study Size and Weight Distribution:** In random-effects REML modeling with *τ*² = 0.108, smaller studies receive greater relative weight than in fixed-effect models. However, the dominance of `study_6_R6` and `study_9_R9` (accounting for 12,669 of the 13,642 participants) anchors the pooled risk ratio closer to 1.0 (RR = 0.767).

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

During pipeline data validation and quality audit, the following structural considerations were identified:
1. **Effect Measure Segregation:** A conflict was detected wherein the meta-analysis protocol explicitly specified a native Risk Ratio (RR) scale, but one eligible publication (`study_7_R7`, *n* = 524) reported data exclusively as an Odds Ratio (OR). To preserve mathematical validity, this study was segregated into an independent stratum (`outcome_group_key: preterm birth before 37 weeks::measure=OR`) and retained as an unpooled single study (*k* = 1) rather than inappropriately combined with native risk ratios.
2. **Standardized Fallback Extractions:** For descriptive study-level comparisons across disparate metrics, Hedges' *g* was computed as a standardized fallback metric; however, native pooling via REML was confirmed and executed for the primary RR group (*k* = 7, *N* = 13,642).

## Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized data from randomized trials evaluating high-dose calcium supplementation (≥1 g/day) for the prevention of preterm birth before 37 weeks' gestation. In the primary pooled analysis of 7 trials comprising 13,642 women, high-dose calcium was associated with a point estimate of RR = 0.767 (95% CI: [0.552, 1.065]). Although this represents a 23.3% relative reduction in preterm deliveries, the wide confidence interval encompasses the null value of 1.0, and the result is not statistically significant (*p* = 0.113).

Our findings align with current World Health Organization (WHO) maternal health guidelines, which strongly recommend high-dose calcium supplementation during pregnancy for the prevention of pre-eclampsia in areas where dietary calcium intake is low, but do not designate preterm birth prevention as an independent primary indication (WHO, 2020). The high degree of statistical heterogeneity observed (*I*² = 80.0%, *p* = 0.021) reflects divergence between smaller single-center trials and massive multicenter studies. Smaller studies conducted in lower-middle-income countries frequently reported dramatic reductions in preterm birth, whereas large-scale trials (such as the CPEP study and the WHO multi-country trial) found effect estimates near unity.

Biologically, calcium supplementation lowers circulating parathyroid hormone and 1,25-dihydroxyvitamin D, thereby dampening intracellular calcium influx into smooth muscle cells. While this mechanism effectively prevents vascular spasms and hypertensive disorders, its capacity to prevent spontaneous labor in normotensive pregnancies appears limited. The observed clinical reductions in preterm birth in calcium trials may be largely driven by the prevention of indicated preterm deliveries necessitated by maternal pre-eclampsia.

---

## Limitations

Several methodological and clinical limitations must be considered:
1. **Residual Heterogeneity:** With an *I*² value of 80.0%, unmeasured clinical diversity—including variable baseline calcium intake, co-administration of vitamin D, and varying gestational ages at initiation (first vs. second trimester)—could not be completely resolved via subgroup analysis due to trial-level reporting constraints.
2. **Aggregation of Preterm Birth Phenotypes:** Most primary trials did not disaggregate spontaneous preterm birth from medically indicated preterm delivery. Consequently, the specific hypothesis that calcium preferentially prevents indicated preterm birth could not be directly isolated.
3. **Exclusion of Odd-Ratio Extractions from Primary Pool:** To maintain statistical integrity, one trial (`study_7_R7`, *n* = 524) could not be pooled into the primary risk ratio model due to metric discordance, reducing the cumulative sample available for native pooling from 14,166 to 13,642.
4. **Adherence and Tolerability:** High-dose calcium regimens (typically 1.5 to 2.0 g daily administered in divided doses) present adherence challenges due to bulky tablet size and gastrointestinal side effects (e.g., constipation, dyspepsia). Variable compliance across trial arms may have diluted the observed therapeutic effect.

---

## Conclusion

In summary, high-dose calcium supplementation (≥1 g/day) during pregnancy is associated with a non-significant trend toward reduced preterm birth before 37 weeks' gestation (pooled RR = 0.767, 95% CI: [0.552, 1.065], *p* = 0.113), accompanied by substantial heterogeneity (*I*² = 80.0%). There is no indication of publication bias (Egger's *p* = 0.512; trim-and-fill *k*₀ = 0). While high-dose calcium remains a cornerstone for pre-eclampsia prevention in low-calcium environments, existing evidence does not support its prescription solely for the prevention of preterm birth. Future clinical trials should stratify outcomes by spontaneous versus indicated preterm delivery and account precisely for baseline dietary calcium intake.
