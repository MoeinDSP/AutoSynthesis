# Combined Dietary and Physical Activity Interventions and Body Mass Index in Adolescents Aged 12–18 Years: A PRISMA-Compliant Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Short-Term Follow-Up Outcomes

## Abstract

**Background.** School-, community- and family-based programmes that combine a dietary component with a physical activity component are among the most widely deployed public-health responses to adolescent obesity. Whether such programmes produce a measurable change in body mass index (BMI) expressed on its native scale (kg/m²) — as distinct from BMI z-score or percentile — at the first assessment after the intervention ends remains uncertain.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of combined diet-plus-physical-activity interventions, compared with no intervention, usual curriculum or usual care, on BMI (kg/m²) in adolescents aged approximately 12–18 years at short-term follow-up (first post-intervention assessment, up to 12 months after the intervention finished).

**Methods.** A systematic search and screening pipeline identified 11 eligible trial reports. Effects were oriented as intervention minus control and pooled on the native mean-difference (MD) scale in kg/m² using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Effect sizes were extracted according to a pre-specified preference hierarchy (reported between-group difference with SE/CI/p-value; end-of-follow-up means, SDs and n per arm; change-from-baseline means and SDs per arm). Trials from which no standard error could be derived were excluded and are listed. Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Six trials (n = 1,250 adolescents) provided an analysable effect size on the BMI (kg/m²) scale. The pooled mean difference was 0.289 kg/m² (95% CI −0.512 to 1.090; p = 0.4796; k = 6). Per the direction field of the analysis, the point estimate **favours the control condition (no intervention, usual curriculum or usual care)**; however, the confidence interval comfortably spans the null, so the data are compatible with a clinically meaningful benefit, no effect, or a clinically meaningful disadvantage of the combined intervention. Heterogeneity was substantial: Q = 13.413 (df = 5), p = 0.0198, I² = 64.91%, τ² = 0.575753 (τ ≈ 0.759 kg/m²); the approximate 95% prediction interval spanned roughly −1.40 to +1.98 kg/m². Individual trial effects ranged from −0.153 to 0.547 on the pooled scale. Egger's regression indicated statistically significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = −0.3865, p = 0.0027), supporting a high risk of small-study effects; trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0) but reduced the adjusted estimate to 0.0807, and a null imputation under substantial heterogeneity should not be read as evidence of absence of bias.

**Conclusions.** There is no reliable evidence that combined diet-plus-physical-activity interventions reduce absolute BMI (kg/m²) in adolescents at short-term follow-up. The pooled estimate is imprecise, statistically non-significant, substantially heterogeneous, and compromised by significant funnel-plot asymmetry driven by small studies reporting the largest effects; the two largest trials in the evidence base point in the opposite direction to the pooled point estimate. Confidence in the body of evidence is low. Larger, adequately powered trials reporting BMI on its native scale with full variance information are required before any conclusion about direction or magnitude can be drawn.


## 1. Introduction

Adolescence is a critical window for the consolidation of weight status. Excess adiposity acquired between the ages of 12 and 18 years tracks strongly into adulthood and is associated with cardiometabolic risk, musculoskeletal morbidity, impaired psychosocial functioning and reduced quality of life. Because adolescents are reachable through institutional settings — schools, youth organisations, primary care, and the family unit — behavioural interventions delivered in these settings have become the dominant prevention and management strategy worldwide.

Theoretically, interventions that address *both* sides of the energy-balance equation should outperform single-component programmes: diet-only interventions may be offset by compensatory reductions in activity, and activity-only interventions may be offset by compensatory increases in energy intake. This rationale has driven the proliferation of "combined" or "multicomponent" diet-plus-physical-activity programmes. Yet the empirical literature evaluating these programmes is methodologically fragmented. Trials differ in setting (classroom curriculum, after-school club, community centre, family home), intensity (single workshops to multi-year curricula), delivery agent (teachers, researchers, peer leaders, dietitians, parents), and — critically for synthesis — in how the anthropometric outcome is reported.

The choice of outcome metric is not a technicality. Most paediatric obesity syntheses pool BMI z-score or BMI standard deviation score, because these are age- and sex-standardised and are considered more appropriate for growing children. However, BMI z-scores are compressed at the upper tail of the distribution, are reference-population dependent, and are poorly interpretable by clinicians, policymakers and families. Absolute BMI in kg/m² is the metric in which clinical thresholds, surveillance data and cost-effectiveness models are expressed. A synthesis restricted to the native kg/m² scale therefore answers a distinct and policy-relevant question — but it also means that many otherwise eligible trials cannot contribute, because they reported only standardised metrics.

This review was designed to address that specific question: **among adolescents aged approximately 12–18 years, what is the effect of school-, community- or family-based interventions containing both a dietary and a physical activity component, compared with no intervention, usual curriculum or usual care, on BMI expressed in kg/m² at the first assessment after the intervention ended (and no more than 12 months post-intervention)?**

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

This review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement. The research question, eligibility criteria, outcome definition, effect-size extraction hierarchy and analytic model were specified prior to data extraction. Because extraction and synthesis were executed through a structured, auditable pipeline, all decisions reported below (including exclusions at the effect-size derivation stage) are traceable to the pre-specified rules.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adolescents of approximately 12–18 years of age. Trials recruiting mixed paediatric samples were eligible where the sample mean age fell within, or immediately adjacent to, this band.

**Intervention.** Programmes containing **both** a dietary/nutritional component and a physical activity component, delivered in a school, community or family setting. Programmes with only one of the two components were not eligible.

**Comparator.** No intervention, usual curriculum, usual care, or attention-free control.

**Outcome.** Body mass index expressed in **kg/m²**. BMI z-score, BMI standard deviation score and BMI percentile were explicitly *not* eligible as the pooled outcome. The timepoint of interest was the first assessment after the end of the intervention, and no more than 12 months after the intervention finished ("short-term follow-up"). Waist circumference, percentage body fat, skinfolds, dietary intake, physical activity, fitness, and all other outcomes were extracted for descriptive purposes where available but were **not pooled**.

**Design.** Controlled trials with a concurrent comparison group.

### 2.3 Search and screening

Records were retrieved through the review pipeline's bibliographic search and passed through sequential title/abstract and full-text screening against the criteria above. Screening excluded: reviews, commentaries and other non-primary documents; duplicate reports of the same trial; studies in populations outside the target age band; single-component (diet-only or activity-only) interventions; studies without an eligible comparator; and studies reporting no eligible anthropometric outcome at an eligible timepoint.

**Eleven (11) trial reports** satisfied all eligibility criteria and were carried forward to data extraction (`pipeline_summary.n_studies` = 11).

### 2.4 Effect-size extraction hierarchy

Effects were oriented as **intervention minus control** and expressed as a mean difference (MD) in kg/m². The following pre-specified preference order was applied:

1. The trial's own reported between-group difference (adjusted or unadjusted), together with its standard error, 95% confidence interval (SE = CI width ÷ 3.92), or p-value plus sample size.
2. End-of-follow-up mean, SD and n per arm.
3. Mean change from baseline and its SD per arm.

Where no direct between-group difference was given, model-predicted or adjusted arm means were subtracted (intervention minus control). Where the number analysed for BMI was not reported, the number randomised or enrolled per arm was substituted and this substitution noted. **Trials from which no standard error could be derived by any of these routes were excluded from the pooled analysis and are listed as non-contributing.** For multi-arm trials, arms containing both a diet and a physical activity component were combined (or the principal combined arm used), and the shared control group was counted once to avoid unit-of-analysis error.

### 2.5 Statistical analysis

A random-effects model was fitted on the **native mean-difference scale** using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of the between-study variance τ². The analysis therefore reports a **pooled Mean Difference (MD)** in kg/m²; `native_pooling_used` = true, confirming that pooling was performed on the requested native scale rather than on a standardised fallback. The effect measure was set by explicit user specification (`effect_measure_source` = explicit_user_request) and no conflict with the extracted data was flagged (`effect_measure_conflict` = null).

Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q with k − 1 degrees of freedom, the I² statistic, and the between-study variance τ² (with τ reported on the kg/m² scale for interpretability). An approximate 95% prediction interval was derived to characterise the dispersion of true effects.

Small-study effects and potential publication bias were assessed using Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. Given k = 6, both procedures are acknowledged to be low-powered and are interpreted as exploratory signals rather than as definitive tests.

**Direction convention.** Pooled and study-level values retain the raw statistical sign convention (intervention minus control, as re-oriented by the pipeline). The arm favoured by the result is reported **exclusively from the analysis `favors` field**, not inferred from the sign of the point estimate.

## 3. Search and Screening Results

The screening cascade yielded **11 trial reports** meeting all eligibility criteria for population, intervention, comparator, outcome and timepoint.

Of these 11, **6 trials (54.5%) yielded an extractable effect size with a derivable standard error** on the BMI (kg/m²) scale and were included in the quantitative synthesis (`pipeline_summary.n_effect_sizes` = 6; meta-analysis `k` = 6). The remaining **5 trials (45.5%) were eligible but non-contributing**: no standard error could be derived for the BMI (kg/m²) outcome at the short-term timepoint through any step of the extraction hierarchy. The typical reasons for non-derivability in this literature are: reporting of BMI z-score or percentile only (which is outcome-ineligible for this synthesis); reporting of means without any dispersion measure, confidence interval or p-value; reporting of a between-group p-value as "ns" or "< 0.05" without an exact value; and reporting of graphical results only. In accordance with the protocol, these trials are counted in the review but excluded from pooling, and this exclusion is reported transparently rather than being bridged by imputation.

The total sample contributing to the pooled estimate was **n = 1,250 adolescents** across the six contributing trials.

Two structural features of this evidence base are immediately apparent and are central to interpreting the synthesis.

**First, the sample-size distribution is extremely skewed.** Analysable samples range from 33 to 559 participants — a 17-fold spread. Three trials are small (n = 33, 54, 81; 168 participants in total, 13.4% of the pooled sample) and three are moderately sized (n = 253, 270, 559; 1,082 participants, 86.6% of the pooled sample). Under a random-effects model with a large between-study variance, the three small trials receive weight far out of proportion to their 13.4% share of participants.

**Second, effect direction is systematically associated with trial size.** The three smallest trials (n = 33, 54, 81) all return positive point estimates (0.494, 0.175, 0.547), whereas the two largest trials (n = 559, 270) both return negative point estimates (−0.153, −0.137), and the third moderately sized trial (n = 253) returns a point estimate close to the null (0.078). Only one trial in the entire set — the smallest-but-one, `study_1_R1` (n = 81) — has a confidence interval that excludes zero. This pattern is the textbook signature of small-study effects and is formally corroborated by Egger's test (Section 8).

Trials varied in setting (school curriculum, community and family-based delivery), intensity and duration, as is characteristic of this field; however, the number of contributing trials (k = 6) is far too small to support formal subgroup analysis by any of these characteristics (Section 7).

## 5. Effect Sizes

All effects are expressed on the pre-specified native scale, pooled as a **Mean Difference (MD)** in kg/m² (`measure_label` = "Mean Difference (MD)"; `computation_scale` = MD; `native_pooling_used` = true). Each study-level value represents the intervention-minus-control contrast after orientation, with its 95% confidence interval derived from the trial's reported standard error, confidence interval width (SE = CI width ÷ 3.92), or p-value plus sample size, according to the extraction hierarchy.

Study-level effects span a range of **0.700 units** on the pooled scale, from −0.153 (`study_3_R3`) to 0.547 (`study_1_R1`). Precision, as indexed by confidence-interval width, varies more than four-fold: from 0.340 (`study_3_R3`, the largest trial) to 1.386 (`study_7_R7`, the smallest trial). Five of six confidence intervals include zero.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Results

### 6.1 Primary pooled estimate

Pooling the six trials under a REML random-effects model gave:

> **Pooled Mean Difference (MD) = 0.289 kg/m² (95% CI −0.512 to 1.090), p = 0.4796; k = 6 trials, n = 1,250 participants.**

Per the analysis `favors` field, the point estimate **favours the control condition (no intervention, usual curriculum or usual care)**. This labelling is taken directly from the pipeline's direction field and is not inferred from the sign of the estimate.

Three points must be stated with equal emphasis alongside that direction label:

1. **The result is not statistically significant.** With p = 0.4796 and a 95% confidence interval spanning from −0.512 to +1.090 kg/m², the data are fully compatible with the null hypothesis of no difference. The nominal direction of the point estimate therefore carries very little evidential weight on its own.
2. **The confidence interval is wide relative to any plausible clinical target.** Its width is 1.602 kg/m² — larger than the effect sizes typically considered meaningful for population-level adolescent obesity prevention (commonly of the order of 0.2–0.5 kg/m²). The interval is consistent with a sizeable effect in either direction.
3. **The point estimate is driven by small trials.** As Section 4 showed, the three smallest trials supply the positive signal while the two largest trials point the other way. Section 8 shows that this asymmetry is statistically significant and that bias-adjustment collapses the estimate towards the null.

### 6.2 Effect measure confirmation

The effect measure was pooled natively as requested. `native_pooling_used` = true and `effect_measure` = "MD" confirm that the reported pooled value of 0.289 is a **confirmed native-scale mean difference in kg/m²**, not a standardised fallback. It should be named a *pooled mean difference* and should **not** be described as Hedges' g, Cohen's d, or any other standardised metric, notwithstanding the internal `hedges_g` field names used by the pipeline's data structures.

### 6.3 Cumulative and precision-ordered synthesis

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) illustrates the instability of the pooled estimate as trials accrue. With only six contributing trials and τ² of 0.576, each added trial materially shifts both the point estimate and the interval; the cumulative estimate does not converge on a stable value. This is a direct visual expression of the fragility documented quantitatively in Sections 7 and 8.

The bubble plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) renders each trial's effect against its precision/sample size, with marker area proportional to weight. It makes visible the inverse relationship between trial size and effect magnitude that underpins both the heterogeneity finding and the significant Egger test.

### 6.4 Single-group analysis

The analysis comprised a single outcome–timepoint group (BMI in kg/m² at short-term follow-up). No `meta_analysis_groups` with multiple entries were present in the analysis object, so no separate per-group pooled estimates, confidence intervals or heterogeneity statistics are reported; all figures in this section pertain to the one defined synthesis.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant:

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 13.413 (df = 5) | Observed dispersion exceeds sampling error |
| p-value for Q | 0.0198 | Significant at α = 0.05 |
| I² | 64.91% | Substantial inconsistency |
| τ² | 0.575753 | Between-study variance |
| τ | ≈ 0.759 kg/m² | SD of true effects across studies |
| Approximate 95% prediction interval | ≈ −1.40 to +1.98 kg/m² | Range of plausible true effects in a new setting |

Roughly 65% of the observed variability in effect estimates reflects genuine differences in underlying effects rather than within-study sampling error. By conventional thresholds this falls in the "substantial" band (50–90%) and sits close to the boundary of "considerable". With k = 6, I² is itself estimated imprecisely and should be read as an indication of magnitude rather than a precise value.

The between-study standard deviation of true effects, τ ≈ 0.759 kg/m², is **more than two-and-a-half times the pooled point estimate of 0.289 kg/m²**. This ratio is the single most informative heterogeneity statistic in this review: the variation in effects *between* trials dwarfs the average effect itself. Correspondingly, the approximate 95% prediction interval — the interval within which the true effect of a new, comparable combined diet-plus-activity programme would be expected to fall — extends from roughly −1.40 to +1.98 kg/m². A future trial could plausibly show a benefit of nearly 1.4 kg/m², a disadvantage of nearly 2 kg/m², or anything in between. No defensible prediction about the effect of implementing such a programme in a new adolescent population can be made from this evidence base.

Heterogeneity diagnostics are summarised graphically in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

**Likely sources of heterogeneity.** Clinically, the contributing trials differ in setting (school-based curricular delivery versus community and family-based programmes), intervention intensity and duration, delivery agent, baseline weight status of the recruited sample (universal prevention versus targeted samples of adolescents with overweight/obesity), and the precise lag between intervention end and the "first post-intervention assessment" within the permitted 0–12-month window. Methodologically, they differ in the extraction route used (reported between-group difference versus arm means versus change scores), in whether adjusted or unadjusted contrasts were available, and in whether the analysed n for BMI was reported or substituted with the randomised n. Any of these could generate real differences in effect. Because k = 6, none of them can be tested.

## 8. Moderator Analyses

**No formal moderator or subgroup analyses were conducted, and none should be.** With six contributing trials, a meta-regression can accommodate at most one covariate while retaining any semblance of statistical validity, and the conventional minimum of approximately ten studies per covariate is not remotely met. Any subgroup analysis by setting (school versus community versus family), by intervention duration, by baseline weight status, or by extraction route would involve subgroups of one to three trials, would be severely underpowered to detect genuine effect modification, and would be highly susceptible to spurious findings.

The one structural pattern that is unambiguously present in the data — the inverse association between trial size and effect magnitude (Sections 4 and 5) — is best understood not as a substantive moderator but as a small-study effect, and is formally addressed in Section 9. The bubble plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) displays this precision–effect relationship descriptively; it should be read as an exploratory diagnostic of that asymmetry, not as evidence that sample size is a clinically meaningful effect modifier.

Identifying the clinical and methodological determinants of the substantial heterogeneity documented in Section 7 therefore remains an open question that this evidence base is too thin to answer.

## 9. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

| Diagnostic | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | −0.3865 |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.0027** |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate | 0.0807 |
| Overall risk rating | **High** |

**Egger's test indicates statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.0027).** This is consistent with small-study effects and is what drives the "high" risk rating assigned by the analysis. The asymmetry is plainly visible in the raw data: the three smallest trials (n = 33, 54, 81) report the three most positive estimates, whereas the two largest trials (n = 270, 559) report negative estimates. A p-value of 0.0027 from a test with only six data points is a notably strong signal, since Egger's regression is typically badly underpowered at this k — the asymmetry would have to be pronounced to be detected at all.

**The trim-and-fill result (k₀ = 0) does not contradict this finding.** The procedure imputes studies only when asymmetry falls on one specific side of the funnel, and it is known to be underpowered and unreliable in the presence of substantial heterogeneity — which, with I² = 64.91% and τ² = 0.576, is exactly the situation here. A null imputation must therefore **not** be read as evidence of absence of bias. Notably, the trim-and-fill adjusted estimate is 0.0807, approximately **72% smaller** than the unadjusted pooled value of 0.289 and essentially indistinguishable from zero — so even this conservative procedure, while imputing no studies, points towards an estimate at the null.

**Interpretation.** The pooled effect should be treated as **potentially inflated**. More robust sensitivity analyses — PET-PEESE regression-based adjustment, and selection or three-parameter models — would be warranted, although with k = 6 even these methods would be at the very edge of their applicability. Non-suppression explanations for the asymmetry must also be given serious consideration: genuine heterogeneity (small, intensive, closely supervised programmes in selected high-risk samples may truly produce larger effects than large, diffuse, universal school programmes), and study-quality gradients (smaller trials are, on average, less likely to have concealed allocation, blinded outcome assessors or complete follow-up) can each generate funnel asymmetry in the absence of any publication suppression. In this literature, where small intensive trials and large pragmatic trials differ systematically on both dimensions, all three mechanisms plausibly operate simultaneously.

Taken together with the non-significant pooled estimate and the wide prediction interval, the bias diagnostics reinforce the central conclusion: the observed pooled point estimate is not a trustworthy summary of the underlying effect.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 Principal findings

Across six controlled trials in 1,250 adolescents, combined diet-plus-physical-activity interventions produced a pooled mean difference in BMI of 0.289 kg/m² (95% CI −0.512 to 1.090; p = 0.4796) at short-term follow-up. The point estimate is labelled as favouring the control condition, but it is statistically non-significant, imprecise, substantially heterogeneous (I² = 64.91%; τ ≈ 0.759 kg/m²), and compromised by significant funnel asymmetry (Egger p = 0.0027) that reduces to near-zero (0.0807) under trim-and-fill adjustment. **The defensible conclusion is that this body of evidence does not establish an effect of combined diet-plus-activity interventions on absolute BMI in adolescents in either direction.** Claiming a benefit would be unsupported; claiming a harm would be equally unsupported.

### 10.2 Why absolute BMI may be a conservative outcome in adolescents

A central interpretive consideration is that absolute BMI (kg/m²) rises naturally through adolescence as lean mass, stature and pubertal development progress. A successful intervention may therefore attenuate the *rate* of BMI gain without producing an absolute reduction, and over a short follow-up window the resulting between-group difference on the kg/m² scale may be small and easily swamped by measurement variability and secular growth. This is precisely why many paediatric trials report BMI z-scores, which adjust for age and sex. The present review deliberately excluded z-scores, SD scores and percentiles in order to answer the question in the clinically and economically interpretable native metric — but the cost of that decision is a loss of sensitivity to intervention effects that are real but growth-masked, and a loss of nearly half the eligible trials (Section 3). A null or control-favouring result on the kg/m² scale should not be over-extrapolated to mean that such programmes have no effect on adiposity trajectories; it means that no reliable absolute BMI reduction was detectable here.

### 10.3 The decisive role of trial size

The most striking feature of this evidence base is that effect direction tracks trial size almost perfectly. The three trials with fewer than 100 participants all favour the intervention on the raw scale; the two largest trials (n = 559 and n = 270, together 66.3% of the pooled sample) both point the other way; the third moderately sized trial (n = 253) sits essentially at zero. Had the analysis been restricted to the three larger trials — which contribute 86.6% of participants — the pooled estimate would almost certainly have been close to or on the opposite side of the null relative to the reported 0.289. The random-effects model, by design, up-weights small studies when τ² is large, and here τ² = 0.576 is large enough that the three small trials (13.4% of participants) exert disproportionate leverage. This is a legitimate property of the model, not an error, but it makes the headline estimate highly sensitive to the inclusion of a handful of small, imprecise, and (per Egger) possibly selectively reported trials.

### 10.4 Comparison with the wider literature

The finding of a small, non-significant and heterogeneous effect of multicomponent behavioural interventions on adolescent anthropometry is broadly consistent with the wider evidence base. Systematic reviews of school-based obesity prevention have generally reported modest effects on standardised BMI metrics in younger children, with effects attenuating markedly in adolescent samples — a pattern attributed to reduced parental control over the food environment, increased autonomy, competing social priorities, and lower engagement with school-delivered curricula in older age groups. Adolescence is also the developmental period in which attrition from behavioural programmes is highest. The present analysis, restricted to the native kg/m² scale and to short-term follow-up, adds to this picture by showing that when effects are examined in absolute units, no reliable signal survives at all.

### 10.5 Implications for practice and policy

For practitioners and policymakers, the honest message is one of uncertainty rather than of futility. Nothing here demonstrates that combined diet-plus-activity programmes reduce adolescent BMI in absolute terms over the short term; the evidence is too sparse, too heterogeneous and too bias-prone to support that claim. Equally, nothing here demonstrates that they are ineffective or harmful — and such programmes may deliver benefits on outcomes outside the scope of this synthesis (dietary quality, physical activity levels, cardiorespiratory fitness, body composition, psychosocial wellbeing, health literacy), which were explicitly not pooled. Decisions to fund or de-fund such programmes should not be based on this pooled BMI estimate alone.

### 10.6 Implications for research

Three priorities follow. First, **trials must report BMI on the native kg/m² scale with complete variance information** (SDs or SEs, exact p-values, and the n actually analysed for the anthropometric outcome) in addition to any standardised metrics — the loss of 5 of 11 eligible trials to non-derivable standard errors is a wholly avoidable waste of research effort. Second, **the field needs fewer, larger trials**: given that τ ≈ 0.759 kg/m² and that plausible true effects are of the order of 0.2–0.5 kg/m², detecting an effect of this size requires sample sizes well beyond those of the small trials dominating the current random-effects weighting. Third, **prospective registration with pre-specified primary anthropometric outcomes** is essential, since the significant Egger asymmetry is consistent with selective reporting or selective publication of small positive trials.

## 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No formal `validation_flags` were attached to this analysis. Nonetheless, several data-quality features of the synthesis warrant plain statement, because they materially condition the interpretation of the pooled estimate:

- **Eligible but non-contributing trials.** Eleven trials met all eligibility criteria, but only six yielded an extractable effect size with a derivable standard error for BMI (kg/m²) at short-term follow-up. **Five eligible trials (45.5%) were excluded from pooling** because no SE could be derived through any step of the pre-specified extraction hierarchy. Their exclusion was protocol-driven, but it means the pooled estimate rests on just over half the identified evidence, and the excluded trials' results are unknown to the synthesis. If non-reporting of usable variance data were itself associated with result direction, this would constitute an additional, unquantifiable source of bias.
- **Outcome-scale restriction.** By design, BMI z-score, BMI standard deviation score and BMI percentile were ineligible. Trials reporting only these metrics could not contribute, which is a principal reason for the modest k.
- **Sample-size substitution.** Where the n analysed for BMI was not reported, the number randomised or enrolled per arm was used in place of the analysed n, in accordance with the protocol. This can modestly overstate precision for trials with appreciable attrition.
- **Mixed extraction routes.** Effect sizes were derived via different routes across trials (reported between-group difference, end-of-follow-up arm means and SDs, or change-from-baseline means and SDs), and in some cases by subtracting model-predicted or adjusted arm means. Mixing adjusted and unadjusted contrasts introduces an additional, non-quantified source of between-study inconsistency that contributes to the observed τ².
- **Effect measure.** The effect measure was pooled natively as a Mean Difference in kg/m² (`native_pooling_used` = true), as explicitly specified. The reported pooled value is a confirmed native-scale result, not a standardised fallback. Internal field names in the analysis object referencing "hedges_g" are data-structure artefacts and do **not** indicate that a standardised metric was pooled.
- **Multi-arm handling and double-counting.** Where trials had multiple eligible arms, diet-plus-activity arms were combined (or the principal combined arm used) and the shared control group was counted once, so no control group contributes twice to the synthesis. Duplicate reports of the same trial and non-primary documents (reviews, commentaries) were removed at screening.
- **Non-pooled outcomes.** Waist circumference, body fat, dietary intake, physical activity and all other outcomes were not pooled and are not represented anywhere in the quantitative results.
- **Bias diagnostics are flagged as high risk.** The publication-bias module returned an overall risk rating of **high**, driven by Egger's p = 0.0027. This rating is reported as given and is not softened by the null trim-and-fill imputation.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small number of contributing trials (k = 6).** This is the overriding limitation. It renders τ² imprecisely estimated, I² unstable, the confidence interval wide, moderator analysis impossible, and all bias diagnostics underpowered.
2. **Substantial unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 64.91% and τ ≈ 0.759 kg/m² — the latter being more than 2.5× the pooled estimate — mean that no single summary effect adequately represents the included trials. The approximate prediction interval (≈ −1.40 to +1.98 kg/m²) is so wide as to be uninformative for planning.
3. **Significant small-study effects and high bias risk.** Egger's p = 0.0027, with a bias-adjusted estimate of 0.0807, indicates that the pooled value may be inflated by selective publication, selective reporting, or quality gradients correlated with trial size.
4. **Fragility of the point estimate.** The direction of the pooled estimate is driven by three trials contributing only 13.4% of participants, while the two largest trials (66.3% of participants) point the other way. The cumulative analysis shows no convergence.
5. **Loss of 45.5% of eligible trials to non-derivable standard errors**, as detailed in Section 11.
6. **Outcome-scale insensitivity.** Absolute BMI (kg/m²) may under-detect genuine effects on adiposity trajectory during a period of active growth; a parallel synthesis on BMI z-score was outside this review's scope and might yield a different picture.
7. **Short follow-up window only.** By design, only the first post-intervention assessment within 12 months was eligible. Nothing can be said about effects beyond one year post-intervention, where either decay or delayed emergence of benefit is plausible.
8. **No formal risk-of-bias assessment at the individual-trial level is reported here**, so the contribution of allocation concealment, blinding, attrition and selective outcome reporting to the observed heterogeneity and asymmetry cannot be partitioned.
9. **Heterogeneous intervention content.** "Combined diet plus physical activity" subsumes programmes differing by orders of magnitude in intensity, duration, setting and delivery agent; pooling them presumes an exchangeability that is clinically questionable.
10. **Anonymised trial identifiers.** Contributing trials are labelled by pipeline record identifiers rather than author-year citations, which limits independent verification of individual extractions.

---

## 13. Conclusion

In six controlled trials comprising 1,250 adolescents aged approximately 12–18 years, combined dietary and physical activity interventions delivered in school, community or family settings yielded a pooled mean difference in BMI of **0.289 kg/m² (95% CI −0.512 to 1.090; p = 0.4796)** at the first post-intervention assessment within 12 months. Per the analysis direction field, the point estimate favours the control condition — but the result is statistically non-significant, the confidence interval spans effects of meaningful magnitude in both directions, heterogeneity is substantial (Q = 13.413, df = 5, p = 0.0198; I² = 64.91%; τ² = 0.575753), and Egger's test shows significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = −0.3865, p = 0.0027) with a bias-adjusted estimate of 0.0807, essentially at the null.

**There is therefore no reliable evidence that these programmes reduce absolute BMI in adolescents at short-term follow-up, and no reliable evidence that they increase it.** Overall confidence in the body of evidence is **low**, downgraded for imprecision, inconsistency and high risk of small-study/publication bias. The field requires larger, pre-registered, adequately powered trials that report BMI on its native kg/m² scale with complete variance information and specify the number analysed for the anthropometric outcome, before any directional conclusion about combined diet-plus-physical-activity interventions and adolescent BMI can responsibly be drawn.
