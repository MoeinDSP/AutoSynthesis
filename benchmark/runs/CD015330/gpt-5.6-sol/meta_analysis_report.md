# Short-term effects of combined dietary and physical activity programmes on adolescent BMI: a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Combined dietary and physical activity programmes are widely used to prevent or reduce excess weight among adolescents, but their short-term effect on body mass index (BMI) remains uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate, among adolescents aged approximately 12–18 years, the effect of school-, community-, or family-based combined dietary and physical activity interventions versus no intervention, usual curriculum, or usual care on BMI (kg/m²) at the first post-intervention assessment occurring no more than 12 months after programme completion.

**Methods:** Eleven studies were represented in the supplied evidence set, but only four yielded effect-size records and three contributed to the requested native-scale BMI meta-analysis. Effects were oriented as intervention minus control, so a negative mean difference (MD) represented lower BMI with intervention. Native BMI effects were pooled using a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were examined using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill, although the very small number of pooled studies makes these analyses unstable.

**Results:** The primary analysis included 3 studies and 835 participants. The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was 0.026102272396397397 kg/m² (95% CI −1.203 to 1.255; p=0.9667892657794981), favoring no intervention, usual curriculum, or usual care according to the supplied direction classification. Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=76.71%; τ²=0.784871 [kg/m²]²; Q=7.742, df=2, p=0.0208). Four standardized study records were available, but one study was excluded from native MD pooling because raw inputs needed to establish an MD were absent. Egger’s intercept was −0.4583 (p=0.0223), while trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀=0) and produced a standardized estimate of −0.0579. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as high.

**Conclusions:** The available native-scale evidence did not show a statistically detectable short-term effect of combined dietary and physical activity programmes on adolescent BMI. The estimate was highly imprecise, heterogeneity was substantial, and only three studies could be pooled. Consequently, neither meaningful benefit nor harm can be excluded, and the publication-bias findings require caution because formal asymmetry tests are unreliable with so few studies.

## Introduction

Adolescent overweight and obesity are important public-health concerns because elevated adiposity during adolescence can persist into adulthood and is associated with adverse cardiometabolic and psychosocial outcomes. Schools, communities, and families provide practical settings for interventions that address both energy intake and physical activity. Combined programmes may act through complementary pathways, but variation in intervention content, intensity, setting, duration, adherence, and participant characteristics can lead to inconsistent effects.

This review addressed the following question: among adolescents aged approximately 12–18 years, what is the short-term effect of combined dietary and physical activity interventions delivered through school-, community-, or family-based programmes, compared with no intervention, usual curriculum, or usual care, on BMI in kg/m² at the first assessment after intervention completion and no more than 12 months after completion?

The prespecified effect was the raw between-group Mean Difference (MD), calculated as intervention minus control. Accordingly, an MD below zero indicates lower BMI and favors the combined intervention, whereas an MD above zero indicates lower BMI in the control group and favors no intervention, usual curriculum, or usual care.

## Search/Screening

### Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies evaluated adolescents aged approximately 12–18 years and compared a combined dietary and physical activity programme delivered in a school, community, or family setting against no intervention, usual curriculum, or usual care. The outcome had to be BMI expressed in kg/m² at the first assessment after intervention completion, provided that the assessment occurred no more than 12 months after completion.

BMI z-scores, BMI percentiles, waist circumference, body-fat measures, dietary intake, physical activity, and other outcomes were not eligible substitutes for BMI in kg/m². Where several eligible short-term assessments were available, the intended rule was to select the first assessment after intervention completion. For studies with several eligible combined-intervention groups, the intended approach was to combine groups using standard methods where possible or use the prespecified/main intervention group while counting a shared control group only once.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood produced a pooled **Mean Difference (MD)** of **0.026102272396397397 kg/m²** (95% CI **−1.203 to 1.255**; p=**0.9667892657794981**; k=**3**; total n=**835**).

According to the supplied `favors` classification, this result favors **no intervention, usual curriculum, or usual care**. Nevertheless, the point estimate is extremely close to zero, and the confidence interval crosses the null value by a wide margin. Thus, the analysis provides no statistically detectable evidence of a short-term between-group difference in BMI. The interval remains compatible with both a potentially meaningful reduction and a potentially meaningful increase in BMI under intervention.

A cumulative meta-analysis plot was generated at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`. Because study ordering criteria were not supplied and only three native-scale studies were pooled, the cumulative pattern should be regarded as descriptive rather than as evidence of temporal stabilization.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- Cochran’s Q = **7.742**, with 2 degrees of freedom and p=**0.0208**;
- I² = **76.71%**;
- τ² = **0.784871 (kg/m²)²**.

The Q test rejects the hypothesis that all observed differences are attributable solely to sampling error at the conventional 0.05 threshold. I² indicates that a large proportion of the observed variation was associated with between-study heterogeneity rather than within-study sampling error. The estimated between-study standard deviation is √0.784871 ≈ **0.885929 kg/m²**, which is large relative to the pooled point estimate.

Potential clinical and methodological sources include differences in setting, programme intensity and duration, participant baseline BMI, age distribution, comparator conditions, follow-up timing, adherence, analysis method, and cluster adjustment. These explanations could not be tested using the supplied aggregate data.

## Publication Bias

The supplied small-study-effect assessment reported an Egger intercept of **−0.4583** with p=**0.0223**, indicating statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry under that model. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀=0** missing studies and reported a standardized adjusted estimate of **−0.0579**. The supplied overall assessment classified publication-bias risk as **high**.

These findings are not mutually exclusive: trim-and-fill’s failure to impute studies does not exclude selective publication or other small-study effects. However, both procedures are particularly unstable with very few studies. With only three studies in the native MD meta-analysis—and only four standardized effect records overall—Egger’s regression has inadequate power and can also produce misleading significance, while funnel-plot geometry and trim-and-fill are poorly identified. The trim-and-fill value is a standardized estimate and must not be interpreted as an adjusted BMI difference in kg/m². Therefore, the reported asymmetry warrants caution but does not establish publication bias as its cause.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

The random-effects synthesis found essentially no average short-term difference in adolescent BMI between combined dietary and physical activity programmes and control conditions. The pooled MD was 0.026102272396397397 kg/m², with a 95% confidence interval from −1.203 to 1.255 and p=0.9667892657794981. Although the supplied direction field classifies the point estimate as favoring control, its magnitude is negligible and the estimate is highly uncertain.

Substantial heterogeneity limits interpretation of the average effect. The pooled estimate may summarize interventions, populations, and implementation conditions with meaningfully different effects. One small study (`study_7_R7`, n=33) had a comparatively large positive standardized estimate and wide confidence interval, while the largest available standardized record (`study_3_R3`, n=549) had a negative estimate with a confidence interval excluding zero. These contrasting standardized results are consistent with the presence of between-study variation, although they cannot be directly substituted for the native MDs.

### Clinical and policy interpretation

The findings should not be interpreted as proof that combined interventions are ineffective. A non-significant pooled result does not demonstrate equivalence, particularly when the confidence interval is wide and heterogeneity is high. The evidence instead indicates that the average short-term BMI effect remains uncertain. Programmes may also affect health behaviors, fitness, cardiometabolic outcomes, or psychosocial outcomes without producing an immediate change in BMI, but those outcomes were outside the scope of this review and were not pooled.

For decision-makers, the present results do not provide a precise quantitative basis for expecting a short-term BMI reduction from combined programmes as a single broad category. Future implementation and evaluation should define intervention components and intensity clearly, measure adherence and reach, use appropriately adjusted analyses for cluster-randomized designs, and report BMI in kg/m² alongside age- and sex-standardized measures.

### Relation to the review question

The analysis directly targeted BMI in kg/m² at the first eligible post-intervention assessment within 12 months, rather than mixing BMI with z-scores, percentiles, waist circumference, or body composition. This preserves clinical interpretability of the pooled unit. Conversely, it reduced the amount of poolable evidence: only three of the eleven represented studies contributed to the primary synthesis.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

- The evidence set contained **11 studies**, but only **4 effect-size records** were available and only **3 studies** could be included in native MD pooling.
- `study_4_R4` was excluded from the native MD analysis because raw inputs required to confirm or calculate a BMI MD were absent. Its standardized effect was not treated as a raw BMI difference.
- The primary meta-analysis was confirmed as native pooling on the requested MD scale. Standardized Hedges’ g records were reported separately and were not relabeled as MDs.
- The supplied data did not provide study-level native MDs, standard errors, extraction sources, or calculation details; these values could not be independently verified.
- The supplied data did not state whether any BMI analysis counts were replaced by randomized or enrolled counts. Such substitutions therefore cannot be identified at study level.
- Search documentation, full-text exclusion reasons, duplicate reports, outcome-classification decisions, and trial-report linkage information were not available. The possibility of unrecognized duplicate populations cannot be assessed.
- No study-level risk-of-bias judgments were supplied.
- Egger’s test, trim-and-fill, and the supplied “high” publication-bias classification are based on an extremely small evidence base and should be interpreted cautiously.
- The trim-and-fill estimate of −0.0579 is standardized and is not a native-scale BMI MD.

## Limitations

First, only three studies and 835 participants contributed to the requested native-scale analysis, providing limited precision and unstable estimates of heterogeneity. Second, eight of the eleven represented studies did not contribute to the primary meta-analysis; seven lacked effect-size records, and one additional study lacked raw information for native MD pooling. If availability of BMI data was related to study findings, outcome-reporting bias could result.

Third, the study-level raw MDs and their standard errors were not supplied, preventing independent verification of effect calculations, adjustment status, handling of baseline imbalance, clustering, missing data, or sample-size substitutions. Fourth, detailed intervention, population, comparator, and follow-up characteristics were unavailable, precluding investigation of substantial heterogeneity. Fifth, no risk-of-bias assessment was provided, so certainty cannot be evaluated fully. Sixth, the search and selection process was incompletely documented, limiting reproducibility and preventing construction of a complete PRISMA flow account. Seventh, publication-bias methods are not dependable with three or four studies, notwithstanding the statistically significant supplied Egger result.

Finally, the analysis concerns only short-term BMI in kg/m². It cannot determine longer-term sustainability, effects on BMI z-score or adiposity, behavioral outcomes, fitness, cardiometabolic health, adverse effects, equity, or cost-effectiveness.

## Conclusion

Across three studies involving 835 adolescents, combined dietary and physical activity interventions produced a pooled short-term **Mean Difference (MD) of 0.026102272396397397 kg/m²** compared with no intervention, usual curriculum, or usual care (95% CI −1.203 to 1.255; p=0.9667892657794981). The supplied direction classification favored the control condition, but the point estimate was near zero and statistically inconclusive. Substantial heterogeneity (I²=76.71%; τ²=0.784871; Q=7.742, p=0.0208), incomplete availability of native BMI effects, absent study-level risk-of-bias information, and unstable publication-bias diagnostics substantially weaken confidence in the result. Better-reported and adequately powered trials, followed over longer periods and analyzed on clearly specified native outcome scales, are needed to determine whether particular combined-programme designs meaningfully alter adolescent BMI.
