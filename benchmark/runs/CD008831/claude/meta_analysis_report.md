# Probiotics versus placebo for the prevention of grade 2 or higher radiation-induced diarrhoea in patients receiving radiotherapy: a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Radiation-induced diarrhoea is among the most common and most burdensome acute toxicities of pelvic and abdominal radiotherapy, frequently prompting treatment interruption, dose reduction and unplanned hospital contact. Probiotic supplementation has been proposed as a low-cost, low-risk strategy to preserve mucosal barrier integrity and stabilise the intestinal microbiome during irradiation, but trial results have been inconsistent.

**Objective.** To determine whether probiotic supplementation during radiotherapy (with or without concurrent chemotherapy), compared with placebo, reduces the incidence of grade 2 or higher (moderate-to-severe) diarrhoea graded with RTOG, CTCAE, WHO or comparable toxicity criteria.

**Methods.** A systematic search of the assembled evidence base was screened against prespecified PICO criteria; eligible records were placebo-controlled randomised trials in patients with cancer receiving radiotherapy that reported the number or proportion of patients experiencing grade ≥2 diarrhoea. Four studies entered the review and three contributed analysable dichotomous data (n = 194 randomised participants). Effects were pooled on the native risk-ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** The pooled risk ratio for grade ≥2 diarrhoea with probiotics versus placebo was **RR = 0.535 (95% CI 0.204 to 1.400; p = 0.2025; k = 3; n = 194)**. The effect measure was the Risk Ratio (RR) and native-scale pooling was confirmed (native_pooling_used = true). Although the point estimate corresponds to an approximate 46% relative reduction in moderate-to-severe diarrhoea, the confidence interval comfortably includes the null and the automated direction classification for this result is **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**; the analysis therefore does not establish superiority of either arm. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant (Q = 12.88, df = 2, p = 0.0016; I² = 84.5%; τ² = 0.584066), driven by directional disagreement: two trials reported large benefits of probiotics (standardized effect estimates −1.130, 95% CI −1.661 to −0.598, n = 63; and −0.894, 95% CI −1.500 to −0.287, n = 46), while the largest trial reported a non-significant effect in the opposite direction (+0.228, 95% CI −0.199 to 0.655, n = 85). Publication bias risk was assessed as low: Egger's test was non-significant (intercept = 2.9787, p = 0.1206) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted log-scale estimate −0.5811, essentially unchanged from the primary result).

**Conclusions.** Current placebo-controlled randomised evidence is insufficient to conclude that probiotics reduce the incidence of grade ≥2 radiation-induced diarrhoea. The pooled point estimate is compatible with a clinically meaningful halving of risk, but also with a 40% relative increase, and heterogeneity is high enough (I² = 84.5%) that a single summary effect should be interpreted with considerable caution. Adequately powered, strain-specified, placebo-controlled trials using harmonised toxicity grading are required before probiotics can be recommended as standard supportive care during pelvic or abdominal radiotherapy.

**Registration.** Not registered; conducted as a retrospective synthesis of an assembled evidence corpus.

## 1. Introduction

Acute gastrointestinal toxicity is the dose-limiting side effect of pelvic and abdominal radiotherapy. Between 50% and 80% of patients receiving radiotherapy to fields encompassing bowel loops develop diarrhoea during treatment, and a clinically important minority experience moderate-to-severe (grade ≥2) diarrhoea requiring antidiarrhoeal medication, parenteral support, treatment interruption or dose modification. Grade ≥2 diarrhoea is therefore a well-established threshold outcome: it marks the transition from a nuisance symptom to a toxicity that alters oncological management and patient quality of life, and it is the grading cut-point most consistently reported across Radiation Therapy Oncology Group (RTOG), Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events (CTCAE) and World Health Organization (WHO) toxicity scales.

The pathophysiology of radiation enteritis involves crypt stem-cell depletion, villous blunting, loss of tight-junction integrity, mucosal inflammation and, increasingly recognised, radiation-induced dysbiosis of the intestinal microbiota. This mechanistic picture provides a biologically coherent rationale for probiotic supplementation: live micro-organisms — most commonly *Lactobacillus*, *Bifidobacterium* and multi-strain formulations such as VSL#3 — may competitively exclude pathobionts, enhance short-chain fatty acid production, upregulate tight-junction proteins and modulate mucosal immune signalling. Probiotics are inexpensive, orally administered, and have a favourable safety profile in immunocompetent patients, making them an attractive candidate for routine supportive care if efficacy can be demonstrated.

Several randomised placebo-controlled trials have tested this hypothesis, with conflicting conclusions. Some report marked reductions in diarrhoea incidence and severity; others find no benefit, or benefit confined to lower-grade (grade 1) symptoms. Previous narrative and systematic reviews have been hampered by pooling across heterogeneous outcome definitions — mixing any-grade diarrhoea, mean stool frequency, severity scores and grade-specific incidence — which obscures whether probiotics prevent the clinically decisive moderate-to-severe events.

This review addresses a single, narrowly specified question: **in people with cancer receiving radiotherapy, with or without concurrent chemotherapy, do probiotics compared with placebo reduce the incidence of grade 2 or higher diarrhoea?** By restricting the outcome to the dichotomous incidence of grade ≥2 events graded on a recognised toxicity scale, and by pooling on the native risk-ratio scale, we aim to provide a directly interpretable estimate of clinical benefit or its absence.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

This review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement. The synthesis was performed retrospectively on an assembled evidence corpus and was not prospectively registered; this is acknowledged as a limitation (Section 11).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria (PICO)

| Domain | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Adults with a histologically or cytologically confirmed malignancy receiving radiotherapy, with or without concurrent chemotherapy, to a field including abdominal or pelvic bowel |
| **Intervention** | Probiotic supplementation (single- or multi-strain live micro-organisms) administered orally during the radiotherapy course |
| **Comparator** | Placebo (matched, inactive) |
| **Outcome** | Incidence — number or proportion of patients — experiencing grade 2 or higher diarrhoea during or shortly after radiotherapy, graded with RTOG, CTCAE, WHO or comparable criteria. Reports of "moderate-to-severe" radiation-induced diarrhoea were accepted where an explicit grading threshold was stated |
| **Study design** | Randomised controlled trials |

**Exclusions:** non-randomised or single-arm studies; active-comparator-only designs without a placebo arm; trials reporting only any-grade diarrhoea, continuous severity scores or stool-frequency means without a grade ≥2 dichotomy; narrative reviews, systematic reviews, editorials and conference abstracts without extractable 2×2 data; duplicate reports of the same trial population.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

The evidence corpus was screened for records describing probiotic interventions in irradiated oncology populations with diarrhoea outcomes. Titles and abstracts were screened against the PICO frame, and potentially eligible records were assessed in full text. Reference lists of retrieved reviews were hand-searched for additional candidate trials. Where multiple publications reported the same randomised cohort, only the report providing the most complete grade ≥2 diarrhoea data was retained, to avoid double-counting participants.

### 2.4 Outcome definition and effect measure

The primary outcome was the **incidence of grade ≥2 diarrhoea** (binary: event / no event per randomised patient). The prespecified effect measure was the **Risk Ratio (RR)**, selected by explicit analytical request (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`); no conflict between the requested and detected measures was registered. Native-scale pooling on the RR metric was confirmed (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the summary statistic reported below is a genuine risk ratio and not a standardized-mean-difference fallback. By the convention adopted here, **RR < 1 indicates fewer grade ≥2 diarrhoea events in the probiotic arm**.

For descriptive, per-study display purposes the pipeline also reported standardized effect estimates for each contributing trial; these are presented in Section 4 alongside the native pooled result. They are **not** Hedges' *g* interpretations of the pooled analysis, and the pooled summary must be named and read as a **pooled risk ratio**.

### 2.5 Synthesis methods

Effects were combined using a **random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of the between-study variance τ², which is appropriate when clinical and methodological diversity across trials makes a common true effect implausible. Heterogeneity was characterised using Cochran's **Q** statistic with its associated p-value, the **I²** proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity, and **τ²** as an absolute measure of dispersion on the analysis (log-RR) scale. Thresholds of I² ≈ 25%, 50% and 75% were interpreted as low, moderate and substantial heterogeneity respectively, recognising that I² is imprecisely estimated when the number of studies is small.

Small-study effects and potential publication bias were examined with **Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry** and the **Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill** procedure. Both were interpreted conservatively given the known low power of these methods when k < 10.

Prespecified moderator analyses (tumour site, concurrent chemotherapy, probiotic formulation, dose, duration, grading scale) were to be explored by meta-regression and subgroup analysis if the number of contributing studies permitted.

All analyses were conducted within the automated meta-analytic pipeline; generated figures are referenced by path in the sections below.

## 3. Search and screening results

Screening proceeded through the standard PRISMA stages: identification of records, removal of duplicate reports of the same trial cohort, title/abstract screening, full-text assessment against the PICO criteria, and inclusion in quantitative synthesis.

- **Studies included in the systematic review: 4**
- **Studies contributing an analysable effect size to the meta-analysis: 3**
- **Participants contributing to the pooled analysis: 194**

Records were excluded at full text principally because they were not placebo-controlled, because diarrhoea was reported only as an any-grade or continuous outcome without a grade ≥2 incidence threshold, or because they were secondary/overlapping reports of an already-included randomised cohort.

One of the four studies retained in the systematic review (the record occupying position 3 in the included-study sequence) did **not** yield an extractable grade ≥2 diarrhoea effect size and therefore contributes to the narrative review only; it is excluded from all quantitative results. This discrepancy between the number of included studies (4) and the number of pooled effect sizes (3) is stated explicitly here and again in Section 10 rather than being reconciled silently.

A PRISMA-style flow of counts is summarised as: 4 studies assessed as eligible for the review → 3 studies with extractable grade ≥2 diarrhoea dichotomous data → 3 effect sizes pooled (n = 194).

## 4. Included studies and effect sizes

Three randomised, placebo-controlled trials contributed quantitative data. Because the source corpus did not carry bibliographic identifiers for each trial, studies are referred to by their analytic labels.

**Table 1. Studies contributing to the quantitative synthesis.**

| Study label | n (analysed) | Per-study standardized effect estimate | 95% CI | Direction of per-study estimate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | 63 | −1.129627 | [−1.661, −0.598] | Fewer grade ≥2 diarrhoea events with probiotics; CI excludes zero |
| study_2_R2 | 46 | −0.893796 | [−1.500, −0.287] | Fewer grade ≥2 diarrhoea events with probiotics; CI excludes zero |
| study_4_R4 | 85 | +0.227984 | [−0.199, 0.655] | Point estimate in the opposite direction (more events with probiotics); CI includes zero |
| **Total** | **194** | — | — | — |

Two features of this evidence base are immediately consequential for the synthesis.

**First, the trials disagree in direction.** The two smaller trials (n = 63 and n = 46) each produced large effect estimates favouring probiotics, with confidence intervals excluding the null. The largest trial (n = 85, 44% of all pooled participants) produced a small estimate in the opposite direction whose confidence interval spans the null. This is the classic configuration in which a random-effects pooled estimate sits between irreconcilable extremes and its confidence interval widens to accommodate them.

**Second, the relationship between study size and effect magnitude is monotone and inverse** — the smallest trial yields the largest apparent benefit, the largest trial yields none. This pattern is consistent with (though not diagnostic of) small-study effects, and is examined formally in Section 8.

## 5. Meta-analytic result

A single outcome group was analysed (no `meta_analysis_groups` with multiple entries were present), so one pooled result is reported.

**Primary outcome — incidence of grade ≥2 diarrhoea, probiotics versus placebo:**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Risk Ratio (RR)** — native-scale pooling confirmed |
| Number of studies (k) | 3 |
| Participants (n) | 194 |
| **Pooled risk ratio** | **0.535** |
| 95% confidence interval | **0.204 to 1.400** |
| p-value | **0.2025** |
| Model | Random effects, REML |
| τ² | 0.584066 |
| I² | 84.5% |
| Cochran's Q (df = 2) | 12.88, p = 0.0016 |
| Direction classification (`favors`) | **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"** |

The pooled risk ratio of **0.535 (95% CI 0.204–1.400)** corresponds, at the point estimate, to a 46.5% relative reduction in the incidence of moderate-to-severe diarrhoea among patients receiving probiotics. However, the result is **not statistically significant (p = 0.2025)** and the interval is wide: the data are compatible with anything from an approximately 80% relative reduction in grade ≥2 diarrhoea to a 40% relative *increase*. In accordance with the pipeline's direction field, this result is classified as favouring **neither arm** — it should be read as an inconclusive comparison rather than as evidence of probiotic benefit, and equally not as evidence of harm.

It is worth stating explicitly what the point estimate does and does not license. A pooled RR of 0.535 is a clinically attractive number; if it were precise, it would represent a halving of the most management-relevant gastrointestinal toxicity of pelvic radiotherapy and would plausibly change practice. But with k = 3, n = 194 and I² = 84.5%, the point estimate is a weakly identified central tendency across three mutually inconsistent trials, not a stable estimate of a common treatment effect. The honest summary of this analysis is **"direction uncertain, magnitude unknown, heterogeneity substantial."**

No subsidiary outcome groups or timepoints were available for separate pooling; consequently no group-specific estimates beyond the one above are reported, and none have been invented.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **substantial and statistically significant**:

- **Cochran's Q = 12.88** on 2 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.0016** — the null hypothesis of a common underlying effect is firmly rejected, which is notable given that Q is typically underpowered with only three studies.
- **I² = 84.5%** — approximately 85% of the observed variability in effect estimates reflects genuine between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. This falls well above the conventional 75% threshold for substantial heterogeneity.
- **τ² = 0.584066** on the analysis (log-RR) scale, implying a between-study standard deviation of roughly 0.76 in log-RR units. Translated into ratio terms, plausible true effects across settings span roughly a fivefold range, which is why the 95% confidence interval for the summary effect remains wide despite the pooled point estimate being far from 1.0.

The source of this heterogeneity is transparent from Table 1: two trials show large protective effects and the third shows none. With k = 3 there is no statistical means of adjudicating between them, and no basis for declaring any one trial an outlier on statistical grounds. Clinically, candidate explanations include differences in probiotic strain composition, dose (colony-forming units per day) and duration; differences in irradiated volume and total dose; the presence or absence of concurrent chemotherapy, which independently raises baseline diarrhoea risk; differences in primary tumour site (e.g. cervical/endometrial versus rectal versus prostate pelvic fields); divergent baseline event rates in the placebo arms, which strongly affect the achievable risk ratio; and non-equivalence between RTOG, CTCAE and WHO grade 2 definitions.

Given I² = 84.5%, the random-effects summary should be interpreted as the average of a genuinely dispersed distribution of effects rather than as an estimate of a single shared effect. A prediction interval for the effect in a new, exchangeable trial would be wider still than the reported confidence interval and would comfortably include both appreciable benefit and appreciable harm; with only three studies, τ² is itself estimated with very poor precision, so such an interval is not reported as a numeric quantity here.

## 7. Moderator analyses

Moderator and subgroup analyses were prespecified for tumour site, concurrent chemotherapy, probiotic strain/formulation, daily dose, duration of supplementation and toxicity grading instrument.

**None of these analyses could be performed.** With only three contributing effect sizes, any meta-regression would have at most one residual degree of freedom, and any subgroup split would compare groups of one or two studies. Such analyses are uninterpretable and prone to spurious findings; conventional guidance requires approximately ten studies per covariate before meta-regression is advisable. Accordingly, no moderator coefficients, subgroup estimates or interaction tests are reported, and the substantial heterogeneity documented in Section 6 remains statistically unexplained.

A descriptive bubble plot relating study precision to effect magnitude is provided at **`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`**. It is presented purely as a visual aid; it carries no inferential weight and should not be read as a fitted meta-regression.

## 8. Publication bias and small-study effects

Two complementary assessments were conducted.

- **Egger's regression test for funnel-plot asymmetry:** intercept = **2.9787**, **p = 0.1206**. The intercept is substantially displaced from zero — the direction and magnitude are consistent with the inverse size–effect pattern noted in Section 4 — but the test does not reach statistical significance at conventional thresholds.
- **Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill:** **k₀ = 0** studies imputed. The adjusted estimate on the log scale was **−0.5811**, which back-transforms to a risk ratio of approximately 0.56 — essentially unchanged from the primary pooled RR of 0.535. No correction for suppressed small studies was indicated by the algorithm.

**Assessed risk of publication (small-study) bias: low.**

As the pipeline interpretation notes: *"Egger's regression test showed no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.1206), and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), meaning the pooled estimate was unchanged after adjustment. Together these converge on a low assessed risk of publication (small-study) bias for this meta-analysis. That said, these tests have limited statistical power — particularly when the number of included studies is small or heterogeneity is substantial — so a non-significant result should be read as an absence of detectable asymmetry rather than positive proof that no bias exists."*

This caveat deserves emphasis rather than footnoting. With **k = 3** and **I² = 84.5%**, both conditions that most degrade the performance of asymmetry tests are present simultaneously. Egger's test is essentially uninformative at this k, and trim-and-fill cannot reliably detect suppression in a three-study funnel. The non-significant Egger p-value of 0.1206 coexists with a large intercept (2.9787) and with a visually monotone inverse relationship between sample size and apparent benefit — a configuration that, in a larger evidence base, would raise genuine concern about small-study effects, selective outcome reporting or early-stopping optimism in the two small positive trials. The formal classification of "low risk" is therefore reported as computed, but it should be understood as *not detectably biased* rather than *demonstrably unbiased*.

## 9. Discussion

This meta-analysis of three placebo-controlled randomised trials (n = 194) found that probiotic supplementation during radiotherapy was associated with a pooled risk ratio of **0.535 (95% CI 0.204–1.400; p = 0.2025)** for the incidence of grade ≥2 diarrhoea. The analysis does not demonstrate a benefit of probiotics, and the direction classification for the result is **"neither"** — a statistically inconclusive comparison.

**Interpreting a non-significant result with an attractive point estimate.** It would be a misreading of these data to conclude that probiotics are ineffective. A pooled RR of 0.535 is the kind of effect that, if real, would matter a great deal clinically; what the analysis establishes is that the available randomised evidence is too sparse and too inconsistent to distinguish a halving of risk from no effect at all. Absence of evidence is not evidence of absence, and with 194 participants across three trials the analysis is severely underpowered to detect even moderate reductions in a toxicity whose placebo-arm incidence varies widely across radiotherapy settings. Conversely, the upper confidence bound of 1.400 means the data cannot exclude a modest increase in moderate-to-severe diarrhoea, so an unqualified claim of safety-plus-benefit is not supported either.

**The heterogeneity is the main finding.** With I² = 84.5% and Q p = 0.0016, the trials are not estimating the same thing. Two small trials report large protective effects with confidence intervals excluding the null; the largest trial reports a null-to-slightly-adverse effect. Mechanistically plausible reasons for genuine effect modification are abundant: probiotic efficacy is strain-, dose- and viability-dependent, and a formulation delivering high-dose multi-strain *Lactobacillus*/*Bifidobacterium* through the full radiotherapy course is not pharmacologically equivalent to a low-dose single-strain product started late. Baseline risk also matters: in cohorts receiving concurrent chemotherapy or large-volume pelvic fields, placebo-arm grade ≥2 diarrhoea incidence can exceed 40%, while in conformal prostate radiotherapy it may be under 10% — and risk ratios computed across such different baselines are not interchangeable quantities. Variation in grading instrument (RTOG versus CTCAE versus WHO) adds further measurement heterogeneity at precisely the grade 1/grade 2 boundary that defines this outcome.

**Relationship to prior literature.** Earlier reviews of probiotics in radiation-induced gastrointestinal toxicity have generally reported favourable pooled estimates, but most pooled across outcome definitions — combining any-grade diarrhoea, mean stool frequency and severity scores — and many included non-placebo-controlled comparisons. Restricting the synthesis to the placebo-controlled, grade ≥2 incidence question, as done here, yields a markedly less certain picture. This pattern is informative in itself: it suggests that apparent benefits reported in the broader literature may be concentrated in milder symptomatology or in less rigorously controlled designs, and that the evidence specifically addressing prevention of clinically consequential, treatment-altering diarrhoea remains thin.

**Clinical implications.** At present, probiotics cannot be recommended as evidence-based standard supportive care for the prevention of moderate-to-severe radiation-induced diarrhoea. They remain a biologically rationalised, low-cost, generally well-tolerated candidate intervention whose efficacy for this specific endpoint is unproven. Clinicians considering probiotics in immunocompetent patients may reasonably view them as low-risk, but should not substitute them for established measures (dietary modification, antidiarrhoeal agents, bowel-sparing planning techniques) or present them to patients as proven prophylaxis. Caution remains warranted in neutropenic, heavily immunosuppressed or central-line-bearing patients, in whom probiotic-associated bacteraemia and fungaemia have been reported.

**Research implications.** The field needs fewer, larger, better-specified trials rather than more small ones. Priorities are: (i) adequately powered trials — on the order of several hundred patients per arm for a realistic 25–35% relative risk reduction at plausible baseline rates; (ii) complete specification of strain, dose in CFU/day, product viability and timing relative to the radiotherapy course; (iii) stratification or restriction by irradiated site and concurrent chemotherapy, the two dominant determinants of baseline risk; (iv) harmonised grading with a single instrument (CTCAE) and prospective, protocol-specified grade ≥2 incidence as the primary endpoint; and (v) prospective registration with published statistical analysis plans to mitigate selective outcome reporting. Mechanistic co-endpoints — faecal microbiome sequencing, calprotectin, intestinal permeability — would help explain why effects differ so markedly between trials.

## 10. Data quality and validation notes

No formal `validation_flags` were returned for this synthesis. The following data-integrity observations are nonetheless stated plainly, because they bear directly on how the results should be read:

1. **Four studies were included in the review but only three contributed an effect size.** The pipeline recorded `n_studies = 4` and `n_effect_sizes = 3`, and the contributing study labels are `study_1_R1`, `study_2_R2` and `study_4_R4` — the third included record did not yield extractable grade ≥2 diarrhoea data and is absent from every quantitative result. All pooled statistics in this report therefore rest on **three** trials and **194** participants, not four trials.
2. **The pooled analysis is on the native risk-ratio scale.** `native_pooling_used = true` and `measure_label = "Risk Ratio (RR)"`; the effect measure was set by explicit analytical request (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`) with no recorded conflict. The summary statistic is a **pooled risk ratio**, not Hedges' *g*, and is named as such throughout.
3. **Per-study values in Table 1 are standardized effect estimates** supplied for display alongside the native pooled RR. They are not on the same scale as the pooled risk ratio and the two should not be compared numerically.
4. **Only one outcome group was available.** No multi-group or multi-timepoint structure was present, so no group-specific pooled effects were withheld as `insufficient_data` — but equally, no separate estimates for alternative diarrhoea definitions or timepoints exist in this analysis.
5. **Overlapping reports.** Where multiple publications described the same randomised cohort, only one was retained, so no participant contributes more than once to the pooled estimate.
6. **Risk-of-bias assessment at the individual-study level** (randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding integrity, attrition, selective reporting) was not available in machine-extractable form for this corpus and could not be tabulated; the certainty of evidence discussion below should be read with that gap in mind.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base.** Three trials and 194 participants provide minimal power. The analysis is far more likely to miss a true moderate effect than to detect one, and all precision-dependent statistics (τ², I², Egger's test, trim-and-fill) are unstable at this k.
2. **Substantial unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 84.5% with Q p = 0.0016 and directional disagreement between trials means the pooled RR summarises a dispersed distribution rather than a common effect. The summary should not be transported confidently to any specific clinical setting.
3. **No moderator analysis possible.** Strain, dose, duration, tumour site, concurrent chemotherapy and grading instrument could not be examined; the drivers of heterogeneity remain hypothetical.
4. **Imprecision.** The 95% CI (0.204–1.400) spans both substantial benefit and modest harm. On GRADE-type reasoning, certainty of evidence for this outcome would be rated low to very low, driven by serious imprecision and serious inconsistency, with possible further downgrading for risk of bias given that individual-study bias domains could not be assessed.
5. **Publication-bias assessment is weakly informative.** The formal classification is low risk, but with k = 3 and high heterogeneity neither Egger's test (intercept 2.9787, p = 0.1206) nor trim-and-fill (k₀ = 0) has meaningful power. The monotone inverse size–effect pattern across the three trials is a residual concern that the statistics cannot resolve.
6. **Outcome measurement heterogeneity.** Grade ≥2 is defined slightly differently across RTOG, CTCAE and WHO scales, and the grade 1/2 boundary is partly subjective and assessor-dependent; non-differential misclassification would tend to attenuate any true effect.
7. **Incomplete study-level reporting.** The source corpus did not supply bibliographic identifiers for each trial, so included studies are referenced by analytic label and their individual characteristics (strain, dose, tumour site, radiotherapy dose) could not be tabulated.
8. **One included study contributed no data**, reducing the review's quantitative base below its nominal size (see Section 10).
9. **No prospective registration or protocol**, and no duplicate independent data extraction with formal inter-rater agreement, raising the possibility of selection or extraction error.
10. **Safety outcomes were not synthesised.** This review addresses efficacy for a single toxicity endpoint only and makes no quantitative statement about probiotic-related adverse events, including rare infectious complications in immunocompromised hosts.

---

## 12. Conclusion

Across three placebo-controlled randomised trials comprising 194 patients receiving radiotherapy with or without concurrent chemotherapy, probiotic supplementation yielded a **pooled risk ratio of 0.535 (95% CI 0.204 to 1.400; p = 0.2025)** for the incidence of grade 2 or higher diarrhoea, pooled natively on the risk-ratio scale using a REML random-effects model. The result is statistically inconclusive and is classified as favouring **neither arm**. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial and significant (I² = 84.5%; τ² = 0.584066; Q = 12.88, df = 2, p = 0.0016), with two small trials reporting large benefits and the largest trial reporting none. Publication bias was formally assessed as low risk (Egger's intercept 2.9787, p = 0.1206; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted log-scale estimate −0.5811), but these tests carry little power at k = 3.

The practical conclusion is one of genuine uncertainty rather than negative evidence: the point estimate is compatible with a clinically important halving of moderate-to-severe radiation-induced diarrhoea, and equally compatible with no effect or a modest increase. Probiotics should not currently be recommended as standard prophylaxis for grade ≥2 radiation-induced diarrhoea, and should not be dismissed. Resolving the question will require adequately powered, strain- and dose-specified, placebo-controlled trials with harmonised CTCAE grading, prospective registration, and stratification by irradiated site and concurrent chemotherapy — not further small trials of heterogeneous formulations.
