# Probiotics for Prevention of Grade 2 or Higher Diarrhoea During Cancer Radiotherapy: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis

## Abstract

## Abstract

**Background:** Diarrhoea is a clinically important toxicity of radiotherapy, particularly when abdominal or pelvic fields are treated and when chemotherapy is administered concurrently. This review evaluated whether probiotics, compared with placebo, reduce the incidence of grade 2 or higher diarrhoea among people receiving radiotherapy, with or without concurrent chemotherapy.

**Methods:** The supplied evidence set contained four studies, of which three contributed effect estimates to the quantitative synthesis. The three included estimates represented 279 participants. Because the intended native effect measure could not be calculated from the available study-level data, effects were pooled as a standardized fallback using Hedges' g rather than as a confirmed native-scale hazard ratio. A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were examined using Egger's test and trim-and-fill, with strong caution because only three studies were pooled.

**Results:** Individual Hedges' g estimates were −1.129627 (95% CI −1.661 to −0.598; n=63), −0.893796 (95% CI −1.500 to −0.287; n=46), and −0.203663 (95% CI −0.505 to 0.098; n=170). The pooled Hedges' g was −0.7015991672759259 (95% CI −1.286 to −0.117; p=0.018677536077011014; k=3; n=279), favoring probiotics as specified by the analysis. Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=78.24%; τ²=0.206132; Q=10.707, p=0.0047). Egger's intercept was 0.6409 (p=0.0129), while trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀=0) and left the pooled estimate unchanged at −0.7016. Publication-bias risk was classified as high.

**Conclusions:** The available synthesis suggests that probiotics may reduce grade 2 or higher diarrhoea during radiotherapy, but confidence in this finding is limited by substantial heterogeneity, only three analyzable studies, possible small-study or publication bias, and use of a standardized fallback effect because the intended native effect measure was not recoverable. The result should not be interpreted as a pooled hazard ratio or as a directly interpretable absolute or relative reduction in diarrhoea risk.

## Introduction

Radiotherapy can cause clinically consequential gastrointestinal toxicity, especially when treatment fields include the abdomen or pelvis. Grade 2 or higher diarrhoea generally reflects toxicity requiring meaningful clinical management and may contribute to dehydration, impaired quality of life, treatment interruptions, dose modification, or additional supportive care. Concurrent chemotherapy may further increase gastrointestinal toxicity.

Probiotics have been proposed as a preventive strategy through effects on intestinal microbial composition, epithelial barrier integrity, and inflammatory signaling. The relevant clinical question is whether administering probiotics during radiotherapy, with or without concurrent chemotherapy, reduces the incidence of grade 2 or higher diarrhoea compared with placebo.

This report synthesizes the supplied study-level results. Its interpretation distinguishes the clinical binary outcome of interest from the statistical scale actually available for pooling. In particular, the intended native effect measure was not recoverable from the supplied raw inputs, and the analysis therefore used a standardized fallback effect.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A REML random-effects model pooled three studies comprising 279 participants. The pooled Hedges' g was **−0.7015991672759259** (95% CI **−1.286 to −0.117**; p=**0.018677536077011014**). According to the supplied `favors` field, this result favors **probiotics administered during radiotherapy, with or without chemotherapy**.

The confidence interval excluded the null on the standardized scale. Nevertheless, this apparent benefit should be interpreted cautiously. The effect measure was not confirmed on the native outcome scale and instead represents a standardized fallback. Therefore, the analysis does not establish the relative or absolute reduction in the incidence of grade 2 or higher diarrhoea. Its bedside interpretability is correspondingly limited.

Only one meta-analysis group was supplied, so no distinct outcome or time-point groups required separate pooling.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- **Cochran's Q:** 10.707
- **Q-test p value:** 0.0047
- **I²:** 78.24%
- **τ²:** 0.206132

The Q test indicates that dispersion exceeded that expected from sampling error alone, and I² suggests that a large proportion of observed variability was attributable to between-study differences. The relatively stronger standardized effects in the two smaller studies and the weaker estimate in the largest study are a notable feature of the data. However, the small number of studies prevents reliable identification of the cause.

Potential sources of heterogeneity could include cancer site, radiation field or dose, concurrent chemotherapy, baseline diarrhoea risk, probiotic strain and dose, adherence, toxicity grading system, and follow-up duration. These possibilities are clinically plausible but could not be tested with the supplied information. ## Moderators

No valid moderator or subgroup analysis could be performed. Only three studies contributed to the meta-analysis, and no usable study-level covariates were supplied. Meta-regression with such a small evidence base would be severely underpowered and prone to unstable or spurious findings. The available bubble chart (`outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/bubble_chart.png`) should therefore be regarded as descriptive rather than as evidence of a moderator effect.

Important prespecified moderators for future analyses would include pelvic versus non-pelvic radiotherapy, concurrent chemotherapy, probiotic species or multistrain formulation, probiotic dose, treatment duration, baseline gastrointestinal risk, and the toxicity instrument and ascertainment time point.

## Publication Bias

Egger's regression produced an intercept of **0.6409** with **p=0.0129**, indicating statistical funnel-plot asymmetry under the supplied analysis. The trim-and-fill procedure estimated **k₀=0** missing studies and produced an adjusted Hedges' g of **−0.7016**, identical to the rounded unadjusted estimate.

The publication-bias risk was classified as **high**. The significant Egger result is consistent with possible small-study effects or publication bias, while the lack of imputed studies from trim-and-fill does not remove that concern. The two methods assess related but nonidentical features and can disagree. Moreover, with only three studies, both Egger's test and trim-and-fill are highly unreliable: asymmetry may reflect heterogeneity, clinical differences, selective reporting, chance, or true effect modification by study size rather than publication bias alone. Accordingly, the exact Egger p value should not be treated as definitive proof of publication bias, and trim-and-fill should not be viewed as evidence that bias is absent.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

In this evidence set, probiotics were associated with a statistically significant standardized pooled effect favoring probiotics for prevention of grade 2 or higher diarrhoea during radiotherapy. The random-effects pooled Hedges' g was −0.7015991672759259 (95% CI −1.286 to −0.117; p=0.018677536077011014). However, the result was based on only three studies and 279 participants, with substantial heterogeneity (I²=78.24%) and high publication-bias risk.

The individual estimates were not consistent in magnitude. The two smaller studies reported comparatively large standardized effects with confidence intervals excluding the null, whereas the largest study reported a smaller effect with a confidence interval crossing the null. This pattern contributes both to the observed heterogeneity and to concern about small-study effects.

### Clinical interpretation

The analysis is directionally compatible with a protective effect of probiotics, but the magnitude is not directly translatable into a relative or absolute reduction in diarrhoea incidence. Although the clinical outcome is binary, the supplied data could not support native pooling as an HR or another binary effect measure. The standardized effect should therefore be considered supportive but indirect evidence rather than a precise estimate of clinical event reduction.

Clinical decisions should also account for intervention-specific considerations not captured here, including probiotic strain, dose, product quality, timing relative to radiotherapy, host immune status, central venous access, and infection risk. A pooled result across potentially different probiotic preparations does not establish interchangeability among products.

### Certainty and implications

A formal GRADE assessment was not supplied and cannot be completed reliably without study-design and risk-of-bias details. Nonetheless, several features would reduce confidence in the estimate: unexplained substantial inconsistency, imprecision associated with a three-study evidence base, possible reporting or small-study bias, missing study-level methodological information, and indirectness introduced by use of a standardized fallback rather than a native binary effect measure.

Future trials should use rigorous allocation concealment and blinding, preregister grade 2 or higher diarrhoea as an outcome, identify the toxicity grading system and assessment window, report complete event counts by randomized group, and provide strain-specific probiotic information. Reporting event counts would permit pooling with an interpretable risk ratio, odds ratio, or risk difference. Studies should also report adverse events, treatment interruptions, rescue medication, adherence, and results stratified by concurrent chemotherapy where appropriate.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

- Four studies were present in the pipeline, but only three contributed quantitative effect sizes; the reason the fourth study was not analyzable was not supplied.
- The intended effect measure was listed as HR, but **0/3** contributing rows contained the raw inputs needed for HR estimation. All three were therefore pooled as Hedges' g on a standardized fallback scale.
- `native_pooling_used` was false. The pooled value is not a confirmed native-scale result and must not be reported or interpreted as a hazard ratio.
- Study-level outcome definitions, time points, raw event counts, and methodological characteristics were unavailable, preventing independent confirmation that every analyzed outcome was strictly equivalent to grade 2 or higher diarrhoea.
- No information was supplied about duplicate publications, linked reports, excluded reviews, or trial reports removed to avoid double-counting. These issues therefore could not be independently assessed.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations. First, only three studies and 279 participants contributed to the pooled analysis, limiting precision and the reliability of heterogeneity and publication-bias assessments. Second, heterogeneity was substantial and could not be explained through subgroup analysis or meta-regression. Third, the intended native HR could not be computed; the standardized fallback limits clinical interpretation for a binary event outcome. Fourth, publication-bias risk was rated high, although formal asymmetry methods are themselves unstable with k=3. Fifth, one of four pipeline studies did not contribute an effect, and the reason was unavailable, creating potential availability or selective-reporting concerns.

Sixth, no study-level characteristics or risk-of-bias judgments were provided. The synthesis therefore cannot evaluate randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, incomplete outcome data, selective reporting, or other biases. Seventh, search and screening details were absent, so the completeness and reproducibility of evidence identification cannot be assessed. Finally, raw event counts were unavailable, preventing calculation of clinically interpretable absolute effects, baseline-risk-specific benefits, or numbers needed to treat.

## Conclusion

The available random-effects meta-analysis found a pooled Hedges' g of −0.7015991672759259 (95% CI −1.286 to −0.117; p=0.018677536077011014), favoring probiotics for reducing grade 2 or higher diarrhoea among people receiving radiotherapy, with or without chemotherapy. This finding is provisional. Substantial heterogeneity, high publication-bias risk, only three analyzable studies, and reliance on a standardized fallback rather than a confirmed native effect measure materially limit confidence and clinical interpretability. Larger, well-reported placebo-controlled trials with complete group-specific event counts are needed before a precise relative or absolute preventive effect can be established.
