# Cerebrolysin or Cortexin for All-Cause Mortality After Acute Ischaemic Stroke: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Randomised Placebo-Controlled Trials

## Abstract

### Background
Cerebrolysin and Cortexin have been evaluated as neuroprotective treatments for acute ischaemic stroke, but their effect on all-cause mortality remains uncertain.

### Objective
To determine whether Cerebrolysin or Cortexin, administered alone or in addition to otherwise comparable care, reduces all-cause mortality by the longest reported trial follow-up compared with saline placebo in adults with acute ischaemic stroke.

### Methods
Randomised, saline-placebo-controlled trials were eligible. The intended outcome was death from any cause, using deaths and analysed denominators when clearly reported and randomised denominators otherwise, at each trial's longest mortality follow-up. Four studies (1,381 participants) contributed four effects. Risk ratios (RRs) were pooled with a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were explored using Egger's test and trim-and-fill. Reporting was constrained by the absence of study citations, arm-level event counts, follow-up times, and risk-of-bias assessments in the supplied results.

### Results
Across four studies, the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.8828932285343211 (95% CI 0.581 to 1.342; p=0.5596401702148754). In accordance with the supplied direction classification, this result favored neither arm and was compatible with both a potentially important mortality reduction and an increase in mortality. Heterogeneity was not detected (Q=0.257, df=3, p=0.9679; I²=0.0%; τ²=0.0). Egger's intercept was 0.2443 (p=0.0439), and trim-and-fill estimated two potentially missing studies. The supplied publication-bias classification was high risk, although only four studies were available and these diagnostics are unreliable at such a small k.

### Conclusions
The available aggregate result does not demonstrate that Cerebrolysin or Cortexin reduces all-cause mortality after acute ischaemic stroke. The confidence interval is wide enough to include clinically meaningful benefit and harm. Apparent absence of heterogeneity should not be interpreted as equivalence across trials, and possible small-study effects further reduce confidence. Complete trial-level mortality counts, follow-up definitions, intervention identities, and risk-of-bias information are needed for a definitive assessment.

## Search/Screening

### Eligibility criteria

The review question was framed as follows:

- **Population:** adults with acute ischaemic stroke.
- **Intervention:** Cerebrolysin or Cortexin, administered alone or as an add-on to usual care or another co-intervention.
- **Comparator:** saline placebo with otherwise comparable usual care or co-intervention.
- **Study design:** randomised, placebo-controlled trials.
- **Outcome:** all-cause death during trial follow-up, measured at the longest mortality follow-up reported by each trial (for example, day 90 or trial end).

Deaths from any cause were eligible regardless of whether they appeared in efficacy, safety, or adverse-event sections. Stroke-specific death, functional status, and other non-mortality outcomes were not acceptable substitutes. The intended denominator rule was to use analysed denominators when clearly reported and randomised group sizes otherwise. For a trial containing multiple eligible treatment-dose groups and a shared placebo group, treatment deaths and denominators were to be combined while the placebo group was counted once. Trials with no deaths in either group were to be retained and flagged even when they did not individually estimate a conventional RR.

> **RR = 0.8828932285343211 (95% CI 0.581 to 1.342; p=0.5596401702148754).**

The null hypothesis of RR=1 was not rejected. Following the supplied `favors` field, the pooled result favored **neither** treatment nor placebo. The interval estimate is imprecise: it remains compatible with a potentially meaningful reduction in all-cause mortality as well as a potentially meaningful increase. Thus, statistical non-significance should not be interpreted as evidence of no effect or as proof of equivalence.

## Heterogeneity

No statistical heterogeneity was detected:

- **Cochran's Q = 0.257** with **3 degrees of freedom**;
- **Q-test p=0.9679**;
- **I²=0.0%**; and
- **τ²=0.0** under REML.

The heterogeneity summary figure is available at:

These statistics indicate that the observed between-study dispersion did not exceed that expected from sampling error in this dataset. They do not establish that the studies were clinically or methodologically identical. With only four studies, both Q and τ² have limited ability to detect genuine heterogeneity, and I² is estimated imprecisely. In addition, clinically relevant differences in agent, dose, treatment timing, concomitant care, stroke severity, and follow-up cannot be examined because those characteristics were not supplied.

No prediction interval was reported. One should not be reconstructed from rounded aggregate values alone, particularly with only four studies and τ² estimated at the boundary of zero.

## Publication Bias

The supplied small-study-effect analyses reported:

- **Egger's intercept = 0.2443**;
- **Egger's-test p=0.0439**;
- **trim-and-fill estimated missing studies (k₀) = 2**; and
- **trim-and-fill output = 0.1136**, labelled `trim_fill_g` in the source results.

Egger's test was statistically significant at the 0.05 level, indicating funnel-plot asymmetry and possible small-study effects. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **high**. Trim-and-fill estimated two potentially missing studies.

These findings warrant caution but do not prove publication bias. Egger's regression is generally not recommended as a dependable diagnostic with so few studies; with **k=4**, its type I error, power, and stability are problematic. Funnel asymmetry can also arise from chance, clinical heterogeneity, methodological differences, selective outcome reporting, or an association between study precision and underlying effect. Likewise, trim-and-fill relies on assumptions that may fail in sparse or heterogeneous datasets.

The trim-and-fill value of **0.1136** is explicitly labelled as a g output, not an RR. It therefore cannot be interpreted as an adjusted mortality RR or directly contrasted with the pooled RR of 0.8828932285343211. No funnel-plot path was supplied.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This synthesis of four randomised saline-placebo-controlled studies and 1,381 participants found no statistically conclusive reduction in all-cause mortality with Cerebrolysin or Cortexin. The pooled Risk Ratio was 0.8828932285343211 (95% CI 0.581 to 1.342; p=0.5596401702148754), and the supplied directional classification favored neither arm. The confidence interval crosses the null and encompasses effects in both directions.

Observed statistical heterogeneity was zero, but this should be interpreted cautiously. Four studies provide limited information about between-study variability, and the absence of trial characteristics prevents evaluation of potentially important clinical differences. The largest study accounted for 1,070 of 1,381 participants (77.5% of the total sample), indicating that the evidence base is numerically dominated by one trial even though random-effects weighting is not determined by sample size alone.

Possible small-study effects were detected by Egger's test, and trim-and-fill imputed two studies. However, both procedures are particularly fragile with four studies. The high publication-bias classification should therefore be understood as a warning about the evidence base rather than as definitive proof that unpublished trials exist.

### Clinical interpretation

The analysis does not establish a mortality benefit. At the same time, failure to reject the null does not establish absence of benefit, absence of harm, or equivalence to saline placebo. The 95% CI is too broad for such conclusions. Clinical decisions should therefore not rely on this mortality estimate in isolation and should consider the totality of benefits, harms, treatment burden, costs, and the certainty of evidence for other outcomes.

Cerebrolysin and Cortexin are distinct interventions. Combining them may be clinically questionable if their compositions, mechanisms, dosing, or evidence bases differ materially. The provided aggregate data did not permit agent-specific analyses. Consequently, the pooled result should not be assumed to apply equally to each product.

### Methodological interpretation

Use of RR is appropriate for a binary mortality outcome and follows the explicit review request. REML is a standard estimator for random-effects meta-analysis, but estimation of τ² is uncertain with four studies. Hartung–Knapp or other small-sample confidence-interval methods were not reported, and no sensitivity analysis using alternative heterogeneity estimators or zero-event methods was available.

There is also a reported measure conflict in the analysis metadata: the user-requested RR was retained even though one or more papers were described as explicitly reporting hazard ratios (HRs). RRs and HRs estimate different quantities and should not ordinarily be pooled as if interchangeable. The result is acceptable only if the RR was independently calculated from arm-level cumulative death counts at the specified follow-up rather than copied or transformed from an HR. Because those arm-level data were not supplied, this cannot be confirmed.

### Certainty of the evidence

A formal GRADE assessment could not be completed. Randomisation supports an initially higher level of evidence, but confidence would potentially be reduced by unassessed trial-level risk of bias, imprecision, suspected publication bias, inability to verify outcome and denominator definitions, and possible indirectness from combining two distinct treatments. The evidence should therefore be regarded as uncertain rather than as demonstrating mortality equivalence.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

- A systematic review/meta-analysis (`manual:8bde092c`) was excluded from the primary-study set because it was a secondary evidence synthesis, not a new randomised trial report. This exclusion prevents double-counting secondary evidence as primary data.
- The analysis metadata records a conflict between the requested **RR** and HRs reportedly presented in one or more source papers. The requested RR was treated as authoritative, but the absence of arm-level deaths and denominators prevents validation of its derivation.
- Individual study outputs were supplied as Hedges' g, whereas the native pooled mortality result was an RR. These scales were kept conceptually separate; the standardized values were not presented as individual mortality RRs.
- The supplied data do not identify zero-event or double-zero trials. Compliance with the prespecified zero-event handling rule therefore cannot be checked.
- Trial citations, mortality timepoints, intervention identities, and arm-level counts were unavailable, preventing independent confirmation that each effect represented all-cause mortality at the longest reported follow-up.

## Limitations

1. **Sparse evidence base:** Only four studies were included, limiting precision, heterogeneity estimation, subgroup analysis, and assessment of small-study effects.
2. **Missing arm-level data:** Death counts and denominators were unavailable, so individual RRs, weighting, zero-event handling, and pooled calculations could not be independently reproduced.
3. **Incomplete study identification:** Authors, publication years, and full citations were not supplied. Study reports cannot therefore be independently checked from the information provided.
4. **Unverified outcome timing:** Exact mortality follow-up times were absent, so use of the longest available follow-up cannot be confirmed.
5. **Unclear intervention composition:** It was not reported which studies evaluated Cerebrolysin and which evaluated Cortexin, precluding agent-specific inference.
6. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** Trial-level methodological quality and selective reporting could not be evaluated.
7. **Effect-measure inconsistency:** Study-level values were presented as standardized effects, while the primary model used RR; one or more papers reportedly presented HRs. These discrepancies complicate auditability.
8. **Publication-bias diagnostics are unreliable at k=4:** The statistically significant Egger result and trim-and-fill imputations may be unstable.
9. **No robust sensitivity analyses:** Alternative random-effects methods, fixed-effect analysis, leave-one-out analysis, zero-event strategies, agent-specific analyses, and risk-of-bias restrictions were not reported.
10. **No formal certainty rating:** The information required for a defensible GRADE assessment was incomplete.
11. **Incomplete PRISMA accounting:** Search methods and screening counts were not provided, so search reproducibility and a full PRISMA flow diagram were not possible.

## Conclusion

Among adults with acute ischaemic stroke, the available randomised placebo-controlled evidence does not demonstrate that Cerebrolysin or Cortexin reduces all-cause mortality by the longest trial follow-up. Across four studies and 1,381 participants, the pooled **Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.8828932285343211 (95% CI 0.581 to 1.342; p=0.5596401702148754)**, with no detected statistical heterogeneity (**I²=0.0%; τ²=0.0; Q=0.257, p=0.9679**). The supplied direction classification favored neither arm.

The result remains compatible with clinically meaningful benefit and harm. Possible funnel asymmetry, missing trial-level mortality data, unclear follow-up definitions, unassessed risk of bias, and inability to separate Cerebrolysin from Cortexin substantially limit confidence. A definitive assessment requires transparent reporting of trial citations, treatment-specific arm-level deaths and denominators, mortality follow-up times, zero-event handling, and prespecified risk-of-bias and sensitivity analyses.
