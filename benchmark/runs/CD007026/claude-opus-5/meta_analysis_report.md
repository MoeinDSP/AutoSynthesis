# Cerebrolysin or Cortexin versus Placebo for All-Cause Mortality in Adults with Acute Ischaemic Stroke: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Randomised Placebo-Controlled Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Cerebrolysin and Cortexin are porcine/bovine brain-derived peptide preparations marketed as neuroprotective or "neurotrophic" add-on therapies for acute ischaemic stroke. Their effect on hard clinical endpoints — in particular all-cause death — remains uncertain, and most prior syntheses have concentrated on functional-outcome scales rather than mortality.

**Objective.** To determine whether Cerebrolysin or Cortexin, given as add-on therapy or alone, reduces all-cause mortality compared with placebo (saline) in adults with acute ischaemic stroke, pooled as a risk ratio from randomised placebo-controlled trials.

**Methods.** A systematic search of randomised placebo-controlled trials of Cerebrolysin or Cortexin in acute ischaemic stroke was conducted, followed by document-type gating to exclude reviews, meta-analyses and duplicate reports of the same trial. The prespecified outcome was the number of participants who died from any cause out of the number randomised, at the longest follow-up for which mortality was reported in each trial (e.g. day 90 or end of trial). Deaths reported only within safety or adverse-event sections were eligible. Stroke-related-death-only counts and functional scales (modified Rankin Scale, NIHSS, Barthel Index) were not used. Where a trial contributed several active dose arms, events and participants were summed across dose arms and compared once with the single placebo arm. Risk ratios were pooled on the native ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, τ² and I². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Four randomised placebo-controlled trials contributing four independent effect estimates and 1,380 randomised participants met the inclusion criteria. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for all-cause death was **0.882 (95% CI 0.580 to 1.340; p = 0.555; k = 4; n = 1,380)**, a result that **favours neither arm** — it is essentially a statistical tie, with a confidence interval compatible with both a clinically worthwhile 42% relative reduction in death and a 34% relative increase. Between-study heterogeneity was nil (Q = 0.256, df = 3, p = 0.968; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), indicating that the four trials were statistically consistent with a single common risk ratio. Egger's regression showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept −0.0221, p = 0.5357) and trim-and-fill imputed one hypothetically missing study (k₀ = 1, adjusted standardized estimate −0.0627), but the overall publication-bias risk was nonetheless rated **moderate** because these tests have very low power at k = 4. One Russian-language document was excluded at the document-type gate as a systematic review/meta-analysis rather than a primary trial.

**Conclusions.** In this synthesis of four randomised placebo-controlled trials, Cerebrolysin/Cortexin neither reduced nor increased all-cause mortality in acute ischaemic stroke to a statistically detectable degree. The point estimate is nominally below 1 but the interval is wide and uninformative about clinically important differences in either direction; the evidence base is small, dominated by a single large trial, and does not support a mortality benefit. Adequately powered trials with mortality as a prespecified endpoint remain necessary.

## 1. Introduction

Acute ischaemic stroke remains among the leading causes of death and acquired adult disability worldwide. Beyond reperfusion therapies (intravenous thrombolysis and endovascular thrombectomy) and organised stroke-unit care, no pharmacological agent has established a reliable effect on survival. Against this background, a family of brain-derived peptide preparations — most prominently **Cerebrolysin**, a porcine-brain peptide/amino-acid hydrolysate, and **Cortexin**, a bovine/porcine cortical polypeptide preparation — has been marketed, largely in Central and Eastern Europe and parts of Asia, as "neurotrophic" or neuroprotective adjunctive therapy for acute cerebral ischaemia. Proposed mechanisms include neurotrophic-factor-like signalling, reduction of excitotoxicity and apoptosis, and promotion of neuroplasticity.

Previous evidence syntheses of these agents have focused predominantly on functional and neurological outcome scales — the modified Rankin Scale (mRS), National Institutes of Health Stroke Scale (NIHSS) and Barthel Index — and on composite "global outcome" endpoints. Mortality, by contrast, is frequently reported only incidentally, often within the safety or adverse-event sections of trial reports rather than as a prespecified efficacy endpoint. This creates two problems. First, a treatment that appears to improve a rating scale may do so by shifting survivors across adjacent categories without affecting survival at all. Second, for a biologic product administered intravenously to acutely ill patients, death from any cause is the single most important and least manipulable safety signal. A synthesis dedicated specifically to **all-cause death**, extracted on an intention-to-treat denominator of randomised participants, therefore addresses a distinct and clinically decisive question.

The present systematic review and meta-analysis asks: **in adults with acute ischaemic stroke, does Cerebrolysin (or Cortexin), given as add-on therapy or alone, reduce all-cause mortality during trial follow-up compared with placebo (saline), when pooled as a risk ratio from randomised placebo-controlled trials?** By convention adopted throughout this report, a **risk ratio below 1 indicates fewer deaths in the Cerebrolysin/Cortexin arm**, i.e. lower all-cause mortality with active treatment; a risk ratio above 1 indicates the opposite.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Reporting standard and protocol

The review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement. The research question, eligible comparison, outcome definition, extraction rules for multi-arm trials, and the effect measure (risk ratio) were specified in advance of analysis; the effect measure was fixed by explicit prespecification rather than being chosen post hoc from the data (`effect_measure_source: explicit_user_request`).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults (≥18 years) with acute ischaemic stroke, diagnosed clinically and/or radiologically, enrolled within the acute phase as defined by each trial.

**Intervention.** Cerebrolysin or Cortexin, administered intravenously (or as otherwise specified by the trial), either as add-on to standard care or as monotherapy, at any dose and for any treatment duration.

**Comparator.** Placebo, operationalised as saline (physiological/normal saline infusion) with otherwise identical background care.

**Study design.** Randomised, placebo-controlled trials. Non-randomised studies, single-arm series, trials with an active (non-placebo) comparator only, narrative and systematic reviews, meta-analyses, protocols, editorials and duplicate publications of an already-included trial were ineligible as primary data sources.

**Outcome.** All-cause death (mortality from any cause) at the **longest follow-up reported for mortality in each trial** (e.g. day 90, day 30, or end of trial), extracted as the **number of participants who died out of the number randomised** in each arm.

### 2.3 Prespecified outcome-extraction rules

The following rules were fixed before extraction and applied uniformly:

1. **Safety-section deaths are included.** Deaths reported only in safety or adverse-event tables — not as an efficacy outcome — were extracted and counted. Restricting to efficacy-reported deaths alone would have introduced selective-reporting bias, since mortality is rarely a prespecified efficacy endpoint in this literature.
2. **Cause-specific death counts are excluded.** Counts restricted to "stroke-related death" or "death due to index stroke" were not used, because adjudication of attribution is non-standardised across trials and is susceptible to differential classification. Only all-cause counts were eligible.
3. **Functional scales are not surrogates for death.** mRS, NIHSS and Barthel Index results were not converted into, or substituted for, mortality data, even where an mRS score of 6 was reported, unless the trial itself reported the explicit number of deaths.
4. **Multi-arm trials are collapsed once.** Where a trial included more than one active dose arm against a single placebo arm, events and participants were **summed across the active dose arms** and compared once with the placebo arm. This preserves a single independent comparison per trial and avoids unit-of-analysis error from repeated use of the same control group.
5. **Randomised denominators.** Where the number analysed was not reported, the number randomised to each group was used as the denominator, approximating an intention-to-treat analysis.
6. **Zero-event trials retained and flagged.** Trials in which no deaths occurred in either arm were retained in the review and explicitly flagged; such trials carry no information for a ratio measure and contribute no weight to a risk-ratio synthesis, but their retention documents the full evidence base rather than silently discarding it.

### 2.4 Search and screening

Electronic bibliographic sources covering the biomedical and Russian-language regional literature were searched for randomised placebo-controlled trials of Cerebrolysin or Cortexin in acute ischaemic stroke, with no language restriction applied at the screening stage. Retrieved records were screened by title and abstract and then at full text against the criteria in §2.2.

Retained full-text documents then passed through an explicit **document-type gate**, in which each record was classified as a primary randomised trial report, a secondary synthesis (systematic review/meta-analysis/narrative review), or a duplicate/companion report of an already-included trial. Only primary randomised trial reports were permitted to contribute data to the quantitative synthesis. The outcome of this gate is reported in full in §9 (Data Quality & Validation Notes).

### 2.5 Data extraction

For each included trial the following were extracted: intervention agent (Cerebrolysin or Cortexin), dose and schedule, add-on versus monotherapy design, comparator (saline placebo), number randomised per arm, number of deaths per arm, the timepoint corresponding to the longest mortality follow-up, and the location of the mortality data within the report (efficacy versus safety section). Multi-arm trials were collapsed per rule 4 above at the point of extraction.

### 2.6 Statistical analysis

The **Risk Ratio (RR)** of all-cause death (Cerebrolysin/Cortexin versus placebo) was the effect measure, computed and pooled **on its native ratio scale** — that is, log-risk ratios were pooled and the result back-transformed, with no conversion to or from a standardized mean difference for the primary estimate (`native_pooling_used: true`; `computation_scale: RR`). This is important for interpretation: the headline figure in this report is a genuine risk ratio, not a standardized effect size relabelled as a ratio.

Pooling used a **random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of the between-study variance τ². Heterogeneity was characterised by Cochran's Q with its degrees of freedom and p-value, τ², and I² (the proportion of total variability attributable to between-study differences rather than within-study error).

Small-study effects and potential publication bias were assessed with **Egger's regression test** of funnel-plot asymmetry and the **Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill** procedure. These diagnostics, together with the per-study forest display of standardized estimates, operate on a standardized (Hedges' g) representation of each comparison; this is a secondary analytic scale used for bias diagnostics only and does not alter the native-scale pooled risk ratio.

Prespecified moderator analyses (agent, dose, add-on versus monotherapy, follow-up length, trial size) were to be explored by subgroup comparison or meta-regression only if the number of trials and the amount of residual heterogeneity made such analysis interpretable.

## 3. Search and Screening Results

Screening proceeded from database records through title/abstract assessment to full-text review and then to the document-type gate. Of the full-text documents reaching the gate, **one record was excluded as a secondary synthesis**: a Russian-language presentation of a Cochrane-style systematic review of Cerebrolysin for acute ischaemic stroke, which described systematic searching of CENTRAL/MEDLINE/EMBASE, predefined inclusion criteria, dual independent study selection, risk-of-bias assessment, Review Manager analysis, and tables of "characteristics of excluded studies" (22 trials) and "characteristics of included studies" (one eligible trial). Because no new primary data were collected in that document, it was excluded from the primary-study set to prevent double-counting of trial-level data already captured (or already judged ineligible) elsewhere (see §9).

After screening and gating, **four randomised placebo-controlled trials** were included in the quantitative synthesis, contributing **four independent effect estimates** and **1,380 randomised participants** in total.

Two features of this evidence base are immediately consequential for interpretation.

First, the sample is **highly concentrated**: a single trial (`study_4_R4`, n = 1,069) contributes 77.5% of all randomised participants, while the three remaining trials together contribute 311 participants (22.5%). Any pooled estimate will therefore be substantially a restatement of that one large trial, modestly tempered by three small ones.

Second, the smallest trial (`study_2_R2`, n = 46) is an order of magnitude smaller than the largest and carries by far the widest confidence interval. Trials of this size cannot meaningfully estimate a mortality risk ratio in a population whose background 90-day case fatality is typically in the single-digit-to-low-double-digit percentages; they contribute little weight but do contribute to the apparent spread of estimates in graphical displays.

Per the prespecified rule, any trial with zero deaths in both arms was retained in the review and flagged; such comparisons contribute no information to a risk-ratio pooling and were handled by the standard approach for non-informative strata within the ratio-scale model.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Study-level estimates are displayed in the forest plot at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`, with the distribution of effects shown at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`.

All four standardized study-level estimates lie on the same side of the null (range −0.4008 to −0.0099), and **all four confidence intervals cross zero** — that is, no single included trial independently demonstrated a statistically significant difference in all-cause death between Cerebrolysin/Cortexin and placebo. The three largest trials cluster tightly between g = −0.0099 and g = −0.0820; the only estimate appreciably distant from this cluster is the smallest trial (g = −0.4008, n = 46), whose interval [−0.985, 0.183] is wide enough to be consistent with the cluster. This pattern — consistent sign, consistently null individually, and a magnitude gradient inversely related to trial size — is the classic configuration in which a small nominal "benefit" in the pooled estimate is driven more by imprecise small studies than by the large one.

Readers should note the scale distinction maintained throughout this report. The per-study values in Table 1 and in the forest plot are **standardized (Hedges' g) representations** retained for graphical display and for the bias diagnostics in §8. The **primary pooled result in §6 is a native-scale Risk Ratio**, computed by pooling log-risk ratios directly from the event counts and randomised denominators. The two scales carry opposite sign conventions for "benefit" (a negative g and an RR below 1 both indicate fewer deaths on active treatment), which is why direction is reported below using the analysis's own explicit direction field rather than inferred from any sign.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

The single prespecified synthesis pooled all four trials on the outcome of **all-cause death at the longest mortality follow-up reported in each trial**. No separate outcome/timepoint groups were defined: the analysis comprises one `meta_analysis` entry, so a single pooled estimate is reported, and there are no additional groups requiring independent k, effect, interval and heterogeneity statistics.

**Table 2. Primary pooled result — all-cause death, Cerebrolysin/Cortexin vs saline placebo**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Risk Ratio (RR)** (native-scale pooling confirmed) |
| Pooled estimate | **RR = 0.882** |
| 95% confidence interval | **0.580 to 1.340** |
| p-value | **0.555** |
| Trials (k) | **4** |
| Participants (n) | **1,380** |
| Model | Random effects, REML |
| τ² | 0.0 |
| I² | 0.0% |
| Cochran's Q | 0.256 (df = 3), p = 0.968 |
| Direction | **Favours neither arm** — essentially a tie; for a ratio measure, favourability also depends on which event was counted |

The pooled **Risk Ratio (RR) for all-cause death was 0.882 (95% CI 0.580–1.340; p = 0.555)** across 4 trials and 1,380 randomised participants. Although the point estimate lies nominally below 1 — corresponding to a 12% lower relative risk of death with Cerebrolysin/Cortexin — the result is **not statistically significant** and, per the analysis's explicit direction field, **favours neither arm**: it is effectively a statistical tie.

The confidence interval is the substantive finding. It spans 0.580 to 1.340, meaning the data are simultaneously compatible with a **42% relative reduction** in all-cause death and a **34% relative increase**. On a plausible placebo-arm 90-day case fatality of, say, 10%, this interval corresponds to absolute effects ranging from roughly 4.2 fewer deaths per 100 treated to roughly 3.4 more deaths per 100 treated. An interval of that width does not exclude either a clinically worthwhile survival benefit or a clinically important survival harm. The correct summary is therefore **not** "Cerebrolysin/Cortexin does not affect mortality" but rather "these four trials, together, cannot determine whether Cerebrolysin/Cortexin affects mortality in either direction."

The effect measure was explicitly prespecified as a risk ratio and was pooled natively on the ratio scale; this pooled value is a confirmed native-scale risk ratio and not a standardized fallback. It should not be described as a Hedges' g.

A cumulative meta-analysis, ordered by accrual of evidence, is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; it illustrates the stabilisation of the pooled estimate once the large trial (`study_4_R4`, 77.5% of participants) enters the synthesis, after which further accumulation produces no material movement in either the point estimate or the direction of the result.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **absent by every available metric**:

- **Cochran's Q = 0.256** on 3 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.968**
- **I² = 0.0%**
- **τ² = 0.0**

Graphical heterogeneity diagnostics are provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

A Q statistic of 0.256 against an expectation of 3 under homogeneity (p = 0.968) indicates that the observed dispersion among the four trial estimates is *smaller* than would be expected from within-study sampling error alone. With τ² estimated at exactly zero, the REML random-effects model collapses numerically onto its fixed-effect counterpart, and the pooled risk ratio of 0.882 is best read as an estimate of a single common effect shared by these four trials rather than as the mean of a distribution of trial-specific effects.

Three cautions attach to this apparent perfection of agreement. First, **I² is uninformative when the constituent trials are individually uninformative.** Four trials whose confidence intervals are all wide and all overlap the null will necessarily appear homogeneous, because sampling error dominates and leaves little residual variance to attribute to between-study differences. I² = 0% here is better understood as "no heterogeneity *detectable*" than as "no heterogeneity *present*".

Second, **Q has very low power at k = 4.** The test is known to be insensitive with few studies, so p = 0.968 provides essentially no assurance that clinically meaningful variation — for instance between Cerebrolysin and Cortexin, between dose regimens, or between 30-day and 90-day mortality ascertainment — is truly absent.

Third, a τ² of exactly zero at the boundary of the parameter space can be an artefact of REML estimation with few studies, and confidence intervals around τ² under these conditions would be extremely wide. Reporting τ² = 0 without this caveat would overstate the certainty of homogeneity.

Nevertheless, the practical consequence is favourable for interpretation: the four trials tell a consistent story, and the pooled null result is not the product of mutually cancelling opposite-direction findings.

## 8. Moderators

Prespecified moderators of interest were the agent administered (Cerebrolysin versus Cortexin), dose and treatment duration, add-on versus monotherapy design, the length of mortality follow-up (e.g. day 30 versus day 90 versus end of trial), and trial size.

**No moderator analysis was performed, and none would have been interpretable.** With k = 4 trials and a single prespecified comparison per trial, there are insufficient degrees of freedom to fit meta-regression models: a single covariate would be estimated from four data points, and any subgroup split would produce strata of one to three trials. In addition, with τ² = 0.0 and I² = 0.0% there is **no between-study variance for a moderator to explain** — a meta-regression would, by construction, have nothing to account for. Any subgroup difference emerging from such an analysis would be a chance artefact of sampling error, not a signal.

The relationship between trial precision and effect magnitude is displayed descriptively at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`. That display is presented as exploratory and hypothesis-generating only; it shows the configuration already described in §5 — the numerically largest apparent benefit arising in the smallest trial (n = 46) and the three larger trials clustering near the null — which is the visual hallmark of a small-study effect rather than evidence of a genuine modifier. No causal or subgroup claim should be drawn from it.

Because moderator effects could not be assessed, the pooled estimate should be read as applying to the mixed population of trials as enrolled, without any warrant for extrapolating to specific agents, doses, timings or follow-up windows.

## 9. Publication Bias

Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure, both applied on the standardized analytic scale.

**Table 3. Small-study-effect diagnostics**

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | −0.0221 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.5357 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 1 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted standardized estimate | −0.0627 |
| Overall publication-bias risk rating | **Moderate** |

Egger's regression test showed **no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept = −0.0221, p = 0.5357)**, and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed only **one** hypothetically missing study (k₀ = 1), yielding an adjusted standardized estimate of −0.0627 — very close to the unadjusted synthesis. Taken at face value, these diagnostics suggest that small-study effects are minimal and that the pooled estimate is unlikely to change materially after adjustment for them.

However, the overall risk of publication bias was nonetheless rated **moderate**. The reason is power, not contradiction: with only four studies, both Egger's test and trim-and-fill are severely underpowered, and conventional guidance discourages formal funnel-plot asymmetry testing below roughly ten studies altogether. A non-significant Egger's test under these conditions is weak evidence *about* bias rather than evidence of its *absence*. The finding must therefore be reported as **"no detectable publication bias"** rather than "no publication bias" — a distinction that matters particularly here, given that this literature spans regional and Russian-language publication venues where small negative or neutral trials of a nationally marketed product may be least likely to appear in indexed, English-language sources, and given that mortality in several of these trials is reported only incidentally within safety sections and is thus additionally exposed to selective-reporting as well as selective-publication pressures.

A further structural consideration reinforces caution. Because one trial supplies 77.5% of the participants, the funnel plot is dominated by a single high-precision point against three low-precision points; in such a configuration asymmetry statistics are dominated by the behaviour of the small studies and have little leverage to detect any systematic suppression. The moderate rating is the appropriate summary.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis of four randomised placebo-controlled trials, comprising 1,380 randomised adults with acute ischaemic stroke, found a pooled **Risk Ratio for all-cause death of 0.882 (95% CI 0.580–1.340; p = 0.555)** for Cerebrolysin/Cortexin versus saline placebo. The result **favours neither arm** and is, statistically, a tie.

Three points deserve emphasis.

**First, a non-significant point estimate below 1 is not a hidden benefit.** It is tempting to read RR = 0.882 as "a 12% mortality reduction that merely failed to reach significance". That reading is not supported. The confidence interval extends to 1.340, so the data are equally compatible with a one-third relative *increase* in deaths. In an evidence base this small, a point estimate displaced modestly from the null is exactly what random sampling variation produces, and the observed displacement is driven disproportionately by the smallest trial in the set (n = 46, standardized estimate −0.4008). The honest summary is indeterminacy, not under-powered benefit.

**Second, the consistency across trials is reassuring but not probative.** Heterogeneity was nil (Q = 0.256, p = 0.968; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), so the four trials are not telling conflicting stories that happen to average to zero. This increases confidence that the pooled estimate faithfully represents what these trials observed. But the trials were consistent largely because each was individually uninformative about mortality; consistency among imprecise studies is not evidence of a precisely estimated common effect.

**Third, the result sits in tension with the more optimistic literature on functional outcomes.** Prior syntheses of Cerebrolysin have often concentrated on mRS, NIHSS and Barthel Index endpoints, where favourable shifts have sometimes been reported. The present analysis, which deliberately excludes functional scales and cause-specific death counts and uses only all-cause death on randomised denominators, finds no corresponding signal on survival. Discrepancies of this kind are common and have a benign and a worrying explanation. The benign one is that a neuroprotective or neuroplasticity-promoting agent might improve function among survivors without altering who survives. The worrying one is that scale-based outcomes are more susceptible to measurement, assessment and reporting flexibility than counting deaths is, so an agent may appear effective on soft endpoints while leaving the hardest endpoint untouched. The present data cannot distinguish these, but they do establish that the case for these agents cannot currently rest on survival.

For clinical practice, these findings provide no basis for administering Cerebrolysin or Cortexin with the expectation of reducing death after acute ischaemic stroke. Equally, they do not demonstrate a mortality hazard. For research, the implication is that mortality should be prespecified, adjudicated and reported as an efficacy outcome in future trials of these agents — not relegated to safety tables — and that any trial intending to address survival would need to be an order of magnitude larger than most of the trials contributing here.

## 11. Data Quality & Validation Notes

Automated validation raised one flag during pipeline execution, reported here in full and without softening.

**Document-type gate — one record excluded as a secondary synthesis (severity: warning; subject: `manual:520b5582`).** A Russian-language document was classified as a *systematic review or meta-analysis* and excluded from the primary-study set. It is a Russian-language presentation of a Cochrane-style systematic review of Cerebrolysin for acute ischaemic stroke: it describes systematic searching of CENTRAL/MEDLINE/EMBASE and other sources, predefined inclusion criteria, dual independent study selection, risk-of-bias assessment, analysis in Review Manager, and contains "characteristics of excluded studies" (22 trials) and "characteristics of included studies" tables for the single eligible trial. **No new primary data were collected in that document.** Its exclusion is correct and necessary: including it would have double-counted trial-level data and would have mixed a secondary synthesis into a primary-trial meta-analysis. Readers should note, however, that the existence of that review — which itself judged only one trial eligible while excluding 22 — independently corroborates that the pool of methodologically eligible randomised placebo-controlled evidence in this field is small.

Additional data-quality points arising from the prespecified extraction rules, recorded here for transparency:

- **Deaths extracted from safety sections.** Mortality data were eligible even when reported only within safety or adverse-event sections rather than as efficacy outcomes. This was a deliberate anti-selective-reporting measure, but it means that some included counts were not subject to the adjudication, blinding or follow-up-completeness safeguards normally applied to prespecified efficacy endpoints.
- **Cause-specific deaths excluded.** Stroke-related-death-only counts were not used; where a trial reported only attributed deaths, those figures did not contribute.
- **Functional scales not substituted.** mRS, NIHSS and Barthel Index data were never converted into mortality estimates.
- **Multi-arm trials collapsed once.** Where a trial had multiple active dose arms, events and participants were summed across dose arms and compared a single time against the one placebo arm, avoiding unit-of-analysis error from repeated control-group use. A consequence is that dose cannot be examined as a moderator within such trials.
- **Randomised denominators used where analysed N was unreported**, approximating intention-to-treat but potentially differing from the trial's own analysis population.
- **Zero-event trials retained and flagged** rather than deleted; such comparisons contribute no information to a risk-ratio pooling.

No outcomes failed classification, no effect measures required separate pooling onto incompatible scales, and no additional trial reports were excluded for double-counting beyond the single secondary-synthesis record described above. The primary effect measure was prespecified and pooled natively, so no standardized fallback was substituted for the headline estimate.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small number of trials (k = 4).** This is the dominant limitation. It renders heterogeneity testing underpowered, publication-bias testing near-uninformative, and moderator analysis impossible, and it widens the pooled confidence interval to the point of clinical ambiguity.
2. **Extreme concentration of information in one trial.** A single trial contributes 1,069 of 1,380 participants (77.5%). The pooled risk ratio is therefore largely that trial's result with modest adjustment, and the synthesis provides much less independent replication than "four randomised trials" might suggest.
3. **Wide and clinically uninformative confidence interval.** The interval 0.580–1.340 does not exclude either a 42% relative reduction or a 34% relative increase in all-cause death. The analysis is an inconclusive test, not a demonstration of equivalence, and should not be cited as evidence of safety-equivalence on mortality.
4. **Mortality frequently a non-prespecified endpoint.** Several contributing counts derive from safety or adverse-event reporting rather than prespecified efficacy analysis, raising the possibility of incomplete ascertainment, inconsistent follow-up for vital status, and differential reporting across trials.
5. **Non-uniform follow-up horizons.** The outcome was defined as the longest mortality follow-up reported in each trial, which varies between trials (e.g. day 30 versus day 90 versus end of trial). Pooling risk ratios across differing time horizons assumes approximate proportionality of mortality risk over those windows, which is unverified here and could not be tested as a moderator.
6. **Two distinct agents treated as one intervention class.** Cerebrolysin and Cortexin are different preparations with different compositions and dosing. Combining them is a prespecified clinical-class decision, but it is a source of conceptual heterogeneity that the data were too sparse to interrogate.
7. **Dose and regimen heterogeneity obscured by arm-collapsing.** Summing across dose arms in multi-arm trials, while methodologically necessary to avoid double-counting controls, forecloses any dose–response examination.
8. **Residual publication and language-bias risk rated moderate.** Although Egger's test (p = 0.5357) and trim-and-fill (k₀ = 1) detected nothing, their power at k = 4 is minimal, and the field's substantial regional and non-English publication footprint makes undetected selective publication a live concern.
9. **Risk of bias within trials not incorporated into the pooled estimate.** Trial-level methodological quality (allocation concealment, blinding integrity of a saline comparator against a peptide infusion, completeness of vital-status follow-up) was considered qualitatively but could not be used for sensitivity analysis or quality-weighted pooling at this k.
10. **Boundary estimate of τ².** The τ² estimate of exactly zero sits at the parameter boundary and, with four studies, is accompanied by a very wide (unreported) uncertainty interval; apparent homogeneity should not be over-read.
11. **No individual-participant data.** All analyses are aggregate; no adjustment for baseline stroke severity, age, time to treatment or reperfusion co-therapy was possible, and these are strong determinants of post-stroke mortality.

---

## 13. Conclusion

Across four randomised placebo-controlled trials and 1,380 randomised adults with acute ischaemic stroke, Cerebrolysin or Cortexin compared with saline placebo yielded a pooled **Risk Ratio for all-cause death of 0.882 (95% CI 0.580 to 1.340; p = 0.555)**, with no detectable between-study heterogeneity (Q = 0.256, df = 3, p = 0.968; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). The result **favours neither treatment nor placebo**; the nominal 12% relative reduction is not statistically significant, and the confidence interval remains compatible with both a substantial survival benefit and a substantial survival harm.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore one of **unresolved uncertainty rather than demonstrated absence of effect**. There is currently no randomised evidence that Cerebrolysin or Cortexin reduces all-cause mortality after acute ischaemic stroke, and clinical use cannot be justified on survival grounds. Nor is there evidence of a mortality hazard. Given that three of the four trials are small, that one trial supplies more than three-quarters of the data, and that mortality is often captured only incidentally in safety reporting, the question will only be settled by adequately powered randomised trials in which all-cause death is prespecified, completely ascertained and transparently reported as a primary or key secondary outcome.
