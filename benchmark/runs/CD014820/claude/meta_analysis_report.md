# Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents for the Prevention of Acute Kidney Injury in Patients at Risk: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) have pleiotropic, non-haematopoietic properties — anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, antioxidant and pro-angiogenic — that have been proposed to confer tissue protection in the kidney. Numerous randomised controlled trials (RCTs) have therefore tested whether a short course of epoetin alfa/beta/zeta, darbepoetin alfa, methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta (CERA) or a related agent can prevent acute kidney injury (AKI) in high-risk clinical settings, but individual trials have been small and their results discordant.

**Objective.** To determine whether ESAs, compared with placebo or usual care without an ESA, reduce the incidence of AKI in adults and children at risk of AKI (cardiac surgery, critical illness or trauma, contrast exposure, or kidney transplantation).

**Methods.** A systematic search and screening pipeline identified 18 eligible randomised trial records, of which 15 contributed a quantitative effect size for the outcome "number of participants who developed AKI". AKI was accepted as defined by each trial, preferring consensus criteria in the order KDIGO > AKIN > RIFLE, otherwise the trial's stated primary creatinine- or eGFR-based definition; dialysis requirement, continuous creatinine values and undefined "renal dysfunction" were not accepted. In multi-arm trials only the ESA and control arms were used, with multiple ESA dose arms combined. The pre-specified effect measure was the risk ratio (RR), pooled on the native binary scale by random-effects meta-analysis with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** Fourteen trials enrolling 4,012 randomised participants contributed to the native-scale pooled analysis (one further trial, study_6, n = 603, was excluded from native RR pooling because raw event counts were not recoverable). The pooled risk ratio for development of AKI with ESA versus placebo/usual care was **RR = 0.909 (95% CI 0.811 to 1.019; p = 0.101)**. Per the analysis direction field, this result **favours neither arm** — it is statistically indistinguishable from the null, with a confidence interval that includes both a clinically meaningful 19% relative risk reduction and a 2% relative risk increase. Between-study heterogeneity was absent: **Q = 17.416 (df = 13), p = 0.181; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0**. Egger's test was borderline (intercept = 0.3713, p = 0.054), while trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0, adjusted standardized effect = −0.0344); overall publication-bias risk was rated **moderate**.

**Conclusions.** Across 14 randomised trials and 4,012 participants, short-course ESA administration did not significantly reduce the incidence of AKI in patients at risk. The point estimate is compatible with a modest benefit, but the 95% confidence interval also includes no effect and a small harm, and the evidence base remains underpowered for a plausible effect of this magnitude. Heterogeneity was nil, indicating that the discordance between individual trial reports is adequately explained by sampling error alone. These data do not support the routine use of ESAs for AKI prophylaxis; adequately powered trials using contemporary KDIGO criteria, with careful attention to thrombotic safety, would be required to resolve the residual uncertainty.


## 1. Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) complicates up to one in five hospital admissions and a far higher proportion of admissions to intensive care, cardiac surgical units and trauma services. Even mild, transient elevations in serum creatinine are independently associated with prolonged length of stay, incident and progressive chronic kidney disease, and excess short- and long-term mortality. Despite three decades of translational effort, no pharmacological agent has been shown convincingly to prevent AKI in any high-risk population, and management remains confined to haemodynamic optimisation, avoidance of nephrotoxins and renal replacement therapy once injury is established.

Erythropoietin and its analogues represent one of the most extensively investigated candidate nephroprotective therapies. Beyond the canonical erythropoietin receptor (EpoR) homodimer that drives erythropoiesis, erythropoietin binds a heteromeric EpoR/β-common receptor complex expressed on renal tubular epithelium, endothelium and infiltrating leucocytes. Engagement of this "tissue-protective receptor" in preclinical models of ischaemia–reperfusion injury, sepsis and nephrotoxin exposure attenuates tubular apoptosis, dampens NF-κB-driven inflammatory signalling, reduces oxidative stress, preserves peritubular capillary density and accelerates tubular regeneration. These findings prompted a substantial clinical trial programme in which erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) — epoetin alfa, beta and zeta, darbepoetin alfa, and methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta (CERA) — have been administered peri-operatively in cardiac surgery, at presentation in critical illness and major trauma, before iodinated contrast exposure, and at induction in deceased-donor kidney transplantation.

The resulting literature is heterogeneous in population, agent, dose, timing and, critically, in the definition of AKI itself, which has evolved from arbitrary creatinine thresholds through RIFLE and AKIN to the current KDIGO criteria. Individual trials have typically enrolled fewer than 200 participants and have reported effects ranging from a halving of AKI incidence to an apparent increase. Previous syntheses have been limited by the inclusion of surrogate endpoints (dialysis requirement, continuous creatinine trajectories, composite "renal dysfunction") that do not correspond to a participant-level AKI diagnosis, and by the merging of ESA-treated participants across unrelated outcome constructs.

This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore addresses a single, tightly specified question: **in people at risk of AKI, do ESAs, compared with placebo or usual care without an ESA, reduce the number of participants who develop AKI?** The outcome is strictly participant-level AKI, defined by consensus criteria wherever available, with dialysis need and undefined renal dysfunction explicitly excluded.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

This review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement. The analysis pipeline, outcome definition, effect measure and direction of benefit were specified before synthesis. The effect measure (risk ratio) was fixed by explicit a-priori request (`effect_measure_source: explicit_user_request`) rather than being chosen post hoc on the basis of the data.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults or children at risk of AKI by virtue of a defined exposure or clinical state: cardiac surgery (with or without cardiopulmonary bypass), critical illness or major trauma, administration of iodinated contrast media, or kidney transplantation.

**Intervention.** Any erythropoiesis-stimulating agent — epoetin alfa, epoetin beta, epoetin zeta, darbepoetin alfa, methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta (CERA), or another ESA — administered by any route, dose or schedule with prophylactic intent.

**Comparator.** Placebo or usual care without an ESA.

**Outcome.** The number of participants who developed AKI (events) out of the number randomised to each arm (intention-to-treat denominator; the number analysed where that was all that was reported), ascertained during the trial's own follow-up period. AKI was accepted as defined by each trial, with a pre-specified hierarchy of preference: KDIGO > AKIN > RIFLE > the trial's stated primary definition (e.g. a specified rise in serum creatinine or fall in eGFR). Participants, not episodes, were counted. **Not accepted as AKI:** need for dialysis or renal replacement therapy, continuous serum creatinine values, and undefined or composite "kidney dysfunction". Trials reporting zero AKI events in both arms were retained with their arm sizes; trials not reporting an AKI outcome at all were excluded.

**Design.** Randomised controlled trials. In multi-arm trials, only the ESA and control arms were used; where more than one ESA dose arm existed, events and participants were summed across ESA arms to avoid unit-of-analysis error from repeated use of a shared control group.

### 2.3 Information sources and screening

Records were retrieved through the systematic search and screening pipeline and passed through sequential gates: document-type classification (to remove narrative reviews, editorials, protocols and duplicate reports of the same trial), eligibility screening against the criteria above, and outcome extraction against the AKI definition hierarchy. Eighteen trial records survived screening and were judged eligible for the review. Of these, 15 yielded an extractable participant-level AKI effect size; three eligible records did not report the AKI outcome in an extractable participant-level form and therefore contributed to the narrative synthesis only. A single document-type classification warning was raised during screening and is reported transparently in Section 9.

### 2.4 Data extraction

For each contributing trial, the following were extracted: number of participants randomised to the ESA and control arms, number of participants in each arm meeting the trial's AKI definition, the AKI definition used, the clinical setting, the ESA agent and cumulative dose, and the follow-up window for outcome ascertainment. Effect sizes were computed on the native binary scale wherever raw numerators and denominators were recoverable.

### 2.5 Statistical analysis

The primary synthesis pooled risk ratios on the native binary scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of the between-study variance τ². Native (raw-count) pooling was successfully used for the primary analysis (`native_pooling_used: true`), so the reported pooled statistic is a genuine **Risk Ratio (RR)** and not a standardized surrogate. One trial (study_6, n = 603) could not be included in native RR pooling because raw event inputs were not recoverable from the available report; it therefore appears in the effect-size table on the standardized scale but is excluded from the pooled RR, giving k = 14 for the primary analysis.

For descriptive and graphical purposes, every contributing trial was additionally expressed on a common standardized scale (Hedges' *g* equivalent), in which, by the pre-specified direction convention for this review, a **positive** value corresponds to RR < 1 — that is, fewer participants developing AKI in the ESA arm. Readers should note that this display convention is the inverse of the raw ratio scale, on which values **below 1** indicate fewer events with ESA. The pooled RR itself retains the raw statistical sign convention (treatment relative to control), and the direction of benefit is reported using the analysis `favors` field rather than being inferred from the sign of the point estimate.

Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q (with k − 1 degrees of freedom), the I² statistic and τ². Small-study effects and potential publication bias were assessed with Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. Figures generated were a forest plot, a cumulative meta-analysis plot, an effect-size distribution plot, a precision–effect bubble chart and a heterogeneity summary panel; file paths are given in the relevant sections.

## 3. Search and Screening

The screening pipeline yielded **18 randomised trial records** meeting the population, intervention and comparator criteria. Of these:

- **15 records (83.3%)** reported a participant-level AKI outcome that satisfied the pre-specified definition hierarchy and contributed a computable effect size;
- **3 records (16.7%)** were eligible on population and intervention but did not report AKI as a participant count meeting the definitional requirements (e.g. reporting only continuous creatinine trajectories, dialysis requirement, or an undefined composite of renal dysfunction) and were therefore carried in the review but not in the synthesis;
- of the 15 trials with an effect size, **14** supplied raw numerators and denominators permitting native risk-ratio computation and entered the primary pooled analysis; **1** (study_6) did not and was excluded from native RR pooling.

The primary meta-analysis therefore comprises **k = 14 trials** and **N = 4,012 randomised participants**. Excluding study_6 removed 603 participants from an otherwise available pool of 4,615.

One record (`7dfc6693_cc13574`, internal subject `manual:2e13ab6a`) could not be confidently classified by document type and was retained as an input on the basis that the curation mode in use trusts user curation by default. This is documented in Section 9.

A PRISMA flow of records is implicit in the above counts: 18 eligible trial reports → 15 with extractable AKI outcomes → 14 in the native-scale quantitative synthesis.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Per-trial effect sizes are shown in the table in Section 4 and displayed graphically in the forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) and the effect distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`).

On the standardized display scale (positive = fewer AKI events with ESA, i.e. RR < 1), **eight of 15 trials (53.3%)** produced a point estimate favouring ESA and **seven (46.7%)** a point estimate favouring control — a near-perfect split consistent with an underlying null or near-null effect. Only four trials had confidence intervals excluding zero: study_3 (+0.617, 95% CI 0.291 to 0.944) and study_15 (+0.384, 95% CI 0.222 to 0.545) in the direction of benefit, and study_2 (−0.725, 95% CI −1.130 to −0.321), study_4 (−0.808, 95% CI −1.292 to −0.324) and study_5 (−0.713, 95% CI −1.189 to −0.236) in the direction of harm — five "statistically significant" trials in total, three of them pointing in opposite directions to the other two.

A structural feature of the dataset deserves emphasis. The trials with the largest apparent effects in **either** direction are without exception small: study_4 (n = 71), study_5 (n = 72), study_2 (n = 100) and study_3 (n = 151) generated the four most extreme estimates, whereas the four largest trials (study_12, n = 1,302; study_10, n = 923; study_6, n = 603; study_15, n = 600) generated estimates of +0.031, +0.057, +0.042 and +0.384 respectively — three of them within 0.06 of the null. This precision–magnitude gradient is visible in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) and is the classic signature of either sampling noise in underpowered trials or small-study effects (see Section 8). Notably, study_15 is the one large trial with a confidently non-null estimate in the direction of benefit, and it exerts appreciable leverage on the pooled result.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

A single outcome construct was analysed (participant-level incidence of AKI), so one pooled estimate is reported; the context data contain no `meta_analysis_groups` requiring separate synthesis by outcome or timepoint.

| 95% confidence interval | **0.811 to 1.019** |

The pooled risk ratio of **0.909 (95% CI 0.811 to 1.019, p = 0.101)** does not reach conventional statistical significance. In accordance with the analysis direction field, this result is classified as **favouring neither arm** — the estimate is, for practical purposes, a tie, and the confidence interval is compatible both with a 19% proportional reduction in the number of participants developing AKI and with a 2% proportional increase. Because the pre-specified effect measure was the risk ratio and native pooling on raw counts was achieved, this is a **confirmed native-scale result** rather than a standardized fallback; the Hedges' *g* values in Section 4 are a display convenience only and should not be quoted as the headline finding.

The forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) shows the diamond straddling the line of no effect with its right-hand limit only marginally beyond unity. The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`), in which trials are added sequentially, is informative: the running estimate is unstable and swings across the null while only the early small trials are included, then converges steadily towards the null-adjacent region as the large trials enter, and has been essentially flat thereafter. There is no evidence that the accumulating evidence is trending towards significance; rather, it is converging on a small, uncertain, probably clinically unimportant effect.

Because τ² was estimated at exactly zero, the random-effects solution coincides with the fixed-effect solution, and the 95% prediction interval for the effect in a new trial is effectively identical to the confidence interval — i.e. a future trial of similar design would be expected to produce a true effect close to RR ≈ 0.91, assuming exchangeability with the included trials.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **absent**:

- **Cochran's Q = 17.416** on **13 degrees of freedom**, **p = 0.181** — no statistically detectable excess dispersion beyond sampling error;
- **I² = 0.0%** — none of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to true between-study differences;
- **τ² = 0.0** — the estimated between-study variance in true log risk ratios is nil.

This is a striking and important result given the apparent discordance of the individual trials described in Section 5. Three trials reported statistically significant harm and two statistically significant benefit, yet the formal heterogeneity statistics indicate that this pattern is fully consistent with chance variation around a common underlying effect. The explanation lies in the width of the small trials' confidence intervals: with 60 to 151 participants and AKI event counts in the low tens, the sampling distribution of each estimate is broad enough to accommodate the observed spread without invoking true effect modification. The heterogeneity summary panel (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`) and the effect distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`) both show a unimodal, approximately symmetric spread of estimates with no distinct subpopulation of trials.

Two caveats temper the reassurance offered by I² = 0%. First, Q and I² have low power to detect heterogeneity when the number of trials is modest (k = 14) and the component studies are imprecise; a zero I² should be read as "no heterogeneity detected" rather than "heterogeneity excluded". Second, the exact-zero τ² means the random-effects model collapses to the fixed-effect model, which assumes a single common true effect across cardiac surgery, critical illness, contrast exposure and transplantation — a clinically strong assumption that the data cannot refute but also cannot positively support.

## 8. Moderators

No formal moderator (subgroup or meta-regression) analysis could be fitted in this synthesis, and this should be stated plainly rather than glossed. There are two reasons.

First, and decisively, **τ² = 0.0 and I² = 0.0%**: there is no residual between-study variance for a moderator to explain. Meta-regression on a dataset with zero estimated heterogeneity cannot, by construction, identify an effect modifier; any apparent association would be an artefact of within-study sampling error.

Second, the structured context data for this analysis contain no coded trial-level covariates (clinical setting, ESA agent, cumulative dose, timing relative to insult, AKI definition vintage, baseline haemoglobin, or risk-of-bias domain), so the covariates most plausibly related to effect modification could not be entered into a model even descriptively.

What is available is the **precision–effect bubble chart** (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`), which plots each trial's effect estimate against its weight/precision. As noted in Section 5, this visualisation shows a clear funnel-shaped relationship: the most extreme estimates in both directions come from the smallest trials, while the four largest trials cluster tightly near the null (with the partial exception of study_15). Sample size is therefore the only "moderator" with a visible relationship to effect magnitude in these data, and its relationship is one of dispersion rather than of systematic direction — precisely the pattern expected under a true null or near-null effect with imprecise component studies, and the pattern that also drives the borderline Egger's test discussed next.

Clinically important questions that these data cannot answer include: whether ESAs behave differently in ischaemia–reperfusion-dominated settings (cardiac surgery, transplantation) versus toxic/septic settings (contrast, critical illness); whether a high single pre-insult dose outperforms divided dosing; and whether the apparently discrepant results of trials using legacy creatinine thresholds versus KDIGO criteria reflect differential outcome ascertainment. These remain hypotheses for future individual-participant-data synthesis.

## 9. Publication Bias

Small-study effects were assessed by two complementary methods, which gave **discordant** results.

**Egger's regression test** produced an intercept of **0.3713** with **p = 0.054**. This is a borderline result that falls just short of the conventional 0.05 threshold. It indicates *suggestive but not statistically confirmed* funnel-plot asymmetry consistent with possible small-study effects — that is, a tendency for smaller trials to report systematically different (in this dataset, more extreme) estimates than larger trials.

**Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill** imputed **k₀ = 0** missing studies, with an adjusted standardized effect of **−0.0344**. Because no studies were imputed, the procedure implies that the pooled estimate is unlikely to change materially after adjustment for hypothetical unpublished results.

The composite publication-bias risk is therefore rated **moderate**. This rating reflects precisely the discordance between the two methods: the pooled estimate appears reasonably robust to trim-and-fill adjustment, but the near-threshold Egger asymmetry — combined with the limited statistical power of Egger's test and the well-documented insensitivity of trim-and-fill when the number of studies is small — means publication bias cannot be confidently ruled out. The findings should accordingly be interpreted with some caution.

Several additional considerations are relevant. The asymmetry signal in this dataset is bidirectional (small trials deviate from the null in both directions), which is more characteristic of sampling noise and heterogeneous outcome ascertainment in underpowered studies than of classical selective non-publication of null results. Conversely, a genuine mechanism for asymmetry does exist in this literature: small single-centre trials of a mechanistically attractive agent with a "positive" creatinine signal may have been preferentially published or preferentially reported with a favourable AKI definition. The exclusion of study_6 (n = 603) from native pooling for want of raw event counts also removes one large, near-null trial from the funnel, which mechanically exaggerates the apparent influence of small studies on the asymmetry tests. Finally, three eligible trials reported no extractable AKI outcome at all; if their non-reporting of AKI was outcome-dependent, this constitutes selective outcome reporting that neither Egger's test nor trim-and-fill can detect.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

The analysis pipeline raised the following validation flags, reported here in full and without softening:

1. **Document-type classification warning (severity: warning).** At the document-type gate, the record `7dfc6693_cc13574` (internal subject `manual:2e13ab6a`) could not be confidently classified as to document type. It was nevertheless retained as an input because the curation mode in operation trusts user curation by default. Consequently, one included record has not been machine-verified as a primary randomised trial report rather than, for example, a secondary analysis, conference abstract or review; readers should regard the inclusion of this record as resting on manual curation alone.

2. **One trial excluded from native effect-measure pooling.** The pre-specified effect measure was the risk ratio, and native binary-scale pooling was used. However, **study_6 (n = 603) was excluded from native RR pooling because it lacked the raw inputs (event counts and denominators) required to compute a risk ratio**. It appears in the effect-size table on the standardized scale only. The primary pooled analysis therefore rests on k = 14 of the 15 trials with effect sizes, and on 4,012 of 4,615 participants (86.9%). Because study_6's standardized estimate (+0.042) sits very close to the null and the trial is large, its omission is unlikely to have shifted the pooled point estimate materially, but it does reduce precision and distorts the funnel plot used for bias assessment.

3. **Eligible trials without an extractable AKI outcome.** Of the 18 eligible trial records, three did not report the outcome in the required participant-level form (AKI events / randomised participants under an accepted definition) and were excluded from the synthesis. Surrogates such as dialysis requirement, continuous creatinine values and undefined "kidney dysfunction" were not accepted in their place, in accordance with the pre-specified outcome definition.

4. **Definitional heterogeneity within the accepted outcome.** AKI was accepted as defined by each trial under a preference hierarchy (KDIGO > AKIN > RIFLE > trial-specific creatinine/eGFR threshold). The pooled estimate therefore averages across somewhat different operationalisations of the same clinical construct. No trials were excluded on this basis, and no measure-level conflict other than the study_6 exclusion was flagged.

No duplicate trial reports requiring de-duplication to prevent double-counting were flagged, and no unresolved outcome-classification failures were reported beyond those listed above.

## 11. Discussion

This meta-analysis of 14 randomised trials and 4,012 participants found that erythropoiesis-stimulating agents did **not** significantly reduce the incidence of acute kidney injury in patients at risk, with a pooled risk ratio of 0.909 (95% CI 0.811 to 1.019, p = 0.101), classified as favouring neither arm. The accompanying heterogeneity statistics (Q = 17.416, df = 13, p = 0.181; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0) indicate that the superficially contradictory literature — three trials reporting significant harm, two reporting significant benefit — is entirely explicable by sampling error around a single, near-null underlying effect.

Three features of the result merit interpretation.

**The point estimate is not nothing, but it is not enough.** An RR of 0.909 corresponds to a 9% proportional reduction in the number of patients developing AKI. If real, and if applied to a cardiac surgical population with a baseline AKI incidence of 25–30%, this would translate to roughly 2–3 fewer AKI diagnoses per 100 patients treated — not trivial, but modest. The problem is that the confidence interval (0.811 to 1.019) is equally compatible with a 19% reduction and with a 2% increase, and the p-value of 0.101 provides no secure basis for inference. With 4,012 participants the analysis has reasonable power to detect a 25–30% relative risk reduction but is clearly underpowered for a true effect of the magnitude actually estimated. The honest reading is that a small benefit has not been excluded, and neither has no benefit.

**The large trials tell a consistent story.** The three largest contributing trials (study_12, n = 1,302; study_10, n = 923; study_6, n = 603 — the last outside the native pool) all returned estimates within 0.06 standardized units of the null. In a literature where the extreme results come exclusively from trials of 60 to 151 participants, this convergence is the most informative single feature of the dataset, and the cumulative plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows the evidence settling into that null-adjacent region rather than trending towards benefit as data accumulate. The partial exception, study_15 (n = 600, standardized +0.384, 95% CI 0.222 to 0.545), is the single largest source of the pooled estimate's displacement below RR = 1.0, and the robustness of the overall finding to this one trial's definitional and design choices deserves scrutiny in any future update.

**The biological hypothesis is not refuted, but the clinical translation has not materialised.** The gap between the robust preclinical nephroprotection literature and the null clinical result is familiar from other candidate AKI therapies. Plausible explanations include: dosing below the threshold required to engage the EpoR/β-common tissue-protective receptor without incurring haematopoietic and thrombotic effects; administration after the injurious insult has already committed tubular cells to apoptosis; outcome ascertainment windows too short or creatinine sampling too sparse to classify AKI reliably; and the inherent insensitivity of creatinine-based AKI definitions to the tubular stress that ESAs are hypothesised to attenuate. It is also possible that any genuine tissue-protective benefit is offset in some populations by ESA-associated thrombotic and hypertensive effects, a concern that has been raised particularly in the kidney transplantation and critical care settings and that this meta-analysis — which addressed only AKI incidence — is not designed to quantify.

The clinical implication is straightforward: these data do not support the routine administration of ESAs for AKI prophylaxis in any of the at-risk settings studied. Given a moderate publication-bias risk rating and residual uncertainty about safety, ESA use for this indication should remain confined to randomised trials.

## 12. Limitations

Several limitations constrain the strength of the conclusions.

1. **One large trial excluded from native pooling.** study_6 (n = 603) lacked recoverable raw event counts and was omitted from the primary RR analysis, reducing the analysed sample from 4,615 to 4,012 participants (86.9%) and distorting the funnel used for bias assessment.

2. **Three eligible trials reported no extractable AKI outcome.** Their exclusion raises the possibility of selective outcome reporting that cannot be detected by funnel-based methods.

3. **Statistical power.** With k = 14 and 4,012 participants, the analysis cannot reliably detect or exclude a relative risk reduction of the order of 10%, which is the magnitude the pooled estimate actually suggests. The non-significant result is as much a statement about the size of the evidence base as about the efficacy of the intervention.

4. **Definitional heterogeneity in the outcome.** AKI was accepted under a hierarchy spanning KDIGO, AKIN, RIFLE and trial-specific creatinine thresholds. Differential sensitivity across these definitions introduces non-differential (and potentially differential) outcome misclassification that would generally bias the pooled estimate towards the null.

5. **Clinical heterogeneity masked by τ² = 0.** Cardiac surgery, critical illness/trauma, contrast exposure and kidney transplantation differ fundamentally in the pathophysiology, timing and reversibility of kidney injury. The zero estimated between-study variance licenses pooling statistically but does not establish that a common effect exists biologically; with k = 14 and imprecise component trials, Q and I² have limited power to detect real heterogeneity.

6. **No moderator analysis possible.** Trial-level covariates (setting, agent, dose, timing, AKI criteria, risk of bias) were not available in coded form, and with τ² = 0 there was in any case no between-study variance for moderators to explain. Important subgroup questions therefore remain unaddressed.

7. **No formal risk-of-bias assessment or GRADE rating** is presented in this synthesis, as domain-level bias data were not available in the structured inputs. The predominance of small single-centre trials in the dataset raises a general concern about allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessment and selective reporting that could not be quantified here.

8. **Borderline funnel asymmetry.** Egger's p = 0.054 with trim-and-fill k₀ = 0 yields a moderate, unresolved bias risk; neither method is powerful at this value of k.

9. **One record of unverified document type** was retained on the basis of manual curation alone (Section 10, flag 1).

10. **Safety outcomes were outside scope.** Thrombovascular events, transfusion requirement, mortality and dialysis initiation were not analysed, so no benefit–harm balance can be inferred from this review.

---

## 13. Conclusion

In patients at risk of acute kidney injury after cardiac surgery, critical illness or trauma, contrast exposure, or kidney transplantation, erythropoiesis-stimulating agents did not significantly reduce the proportion of participants who developed AKI. Pooling 14 randomised trials and 4,012 participants on the native binary scale gave a **risk ratio of 0.909 (95% CI 0.811 to 1.019, p = 0.101)**, a result that **favours neither arm**, with **no detectable between-study heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q = 17.416 on 13 df, p = 0.181)** and a **moderate** residual risk of publication bias (Egger's p = 0.054; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0).

The apparent conflict between individual trials reporting significant benefit and significant harm is fully attributable to sampling error in small studies; the large trials converge on the null. A modest benefit of the order of a 10–20% relative risk reduction remains statistically compatible with these data but is unproven, and the evidence base is too small to settle the question. Current evidence does not justify routine ESA administration for AKI prophylaxis. Resolution would require adequately powered, multicentre, placebo-controlled trials using KDIGO criteria with protocolised creatinine sampling, pre-specified dose and timing relative to the insult, concurrent reporting of thrombotic and mortality outcomes, and ideally pooling at the individual-participant level to permit the subgroup analyses that the present aggregate data cannot support.
