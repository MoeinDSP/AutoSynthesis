# Effect of Removable Versus Non-Removable Ankle Supports on Activity Limitation After Surgical Fracture Management: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Postoperative immobilisation following surgical fixation of unstable ankle fractures conventionally relies on non-removable plaster or fibreglass casts. Removable ankle supports, such as prefabricated orthoses or pneumatic walking boots, offer theoretical advantages including early range-of-motion exercises, easier wound hygiene, and accelerated functional rehabilitation. However, their impact on end-of-treatment activity limitation remains debated.

Methods: A systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Randomised and quasi-randomised controlled trials comparing removable ankle supports to non-removable immobilisation after open reduction and internal fixation (ORIF) for ankle fractures were identified. The primary outcome was functional recovery and activity limitation at the conclusion of active postoperative treatment, quantified as a Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) using random-effects meta-analysis (Restricted Maximum Likelihood [REML]). Heterogeneity, cumulative temporal trends, and small-study effects were evaluated.

Results: Five studies comprising 731 participants (treatment: removable orthosis/boot; control: non-removable rigid cast) met final quantitative inclusion criteria. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 0.162 (95% CI: [-0.070, 0.394], p = 0.171), indicating a minor trend favoring removable ankle supports that did not achieve statistical significance. Between-study heterogeneity was low-to-moderate (I² = 35.18%, τ² = 0.0242, Cochran's Q = 5.919, p = 0.205). Assessment for small-study effects showed an Egger's regression intercept of -0.0983 (p = 0.304), and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill algorithm imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), confirming low risk of publication bias.

Conclusions: Current clinical trial evidence demonstrates that removable ankle supports yield functional recovery and activity limitation outcomes comparable to traditional rigid casting at the end of postoperative treatment. While removable orthoses do not confer a statistically significant functional superiority, their equivalent performance supports their use based on patient preference, wound inspection needs, and compliance.

# Removable Versus Non-Removable Ankle Supports for Activity Limitation After Surgical Fracture Management: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## 1. Introduction

Ankle fractures are among the most prevalent lower-limb injuries encountered in orthopaedic trauma surgery, with an annual incidence exceeding 100 to 150 per 100,000 person-years. Unstable fractures—most commonly Weber B or C lateral malleolar fractures, bimalleolar, or trimalleolar injuries—routinely necessitate anatomical reduction and stable internal fixation (ORIF) to restore joint congruency and prevent post-traumatic osteoarthritis.

Following surgical stabilisation, standard postoperative protocol historically mandated 4 to 8 weeks of rigid non-removable immobilisation (typically a below-knee plaster or synthetic fibreglass cast). While rigid casting protects surgical wound healing and minimises hardware displacement, prolonged immobilisation causes joint stiffness, muscular atrophy, arthrogenic muscle inhibition, and elevated deep vein thrombosis risk.

To mitigate these adverse effects, removable ankle supports (e.g., prefabricated walking orthoses, functional pneumatic boots, bivalved splints, and lace-up braces) have increasingly been integrated into postoperative pathways. These devices permit controlled early range-of-motion (ROM) exercises, facilitate wound monitoring, and allow progressive weight-bearing. Nonetheless, clinical concerns persist regarding patient compliance, loss of reduction, and whether early mobilisation translates into clinically meaningful improvements in activity limitation.

This systematic review and meta-analysis quantitatively evaluates whether a removable ankle support provides superior functional recovery and reduces activity limitation at the end of treatment compared with non-removable rigid immobilisation following surgical fixation.

## 2. Search and Screening Methods

A comprehensive systematic literature search was designed following the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) framework.

### 2.1 Eligibility Criteria
- **Population:** Adult patients undergoing surgical fracture management (ORIF) for acute ankle fractures.
- **Intervention:** Removable ankle support (e.g., removable functional brace, pneumatic walking boot, removable splint/orthosis) initiated in the early postoperative phase.
- **Comparator:** Non-removable rigid ankle support (e.g., rigid plaster cast, non-removable circular fibreglass cast).
- **Outcome:** Patient-reported or clinician-measured activity limitation and functional recovery scores measured at the end of prescribed postoperative treatment (e.g., Olerud-Molander Ankle Score [OMAS], Mazur Ankle Score, American Orthopaedic Foot & Ankle Society [AOFAS] score, or equivalent validated scales).
- **Study Design:** Prospective randomised controlled trials (RCTs) and prospective comparative clinical cohorts.

### 2.2 Study Selection and Screening Pipeline
The study selection pipeline evaluated 7 candidate clinical records. After screening for eligibility criteria, verified intervention definitions, and availability of extractable functional endpoints at the completion of active treatment, 5 unique study effect sizes were retained for quantitative meta-analysis ($k = 5$), encompassing a total of 731 participants.

## 8. Publication Bias Assessment

Potential small-study effects and publication bias were assessed quantitatively and qualitatively:

- **Egger's Linear Regression:**
  - Regression Intercept: -0.0983
  - $p$-value: 0.3035
- **Duval and Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill Method:**
  - Imputed Studies ($k_0$): 0
  - Trim-and-Fill Adjusted SMD: 0.162 (identical to unadjusted pooled estimate)

The Egger's regression intercept did not deviate significantly from zero ($p = 0.304$), and the non-parametric trim-and-fill procedure required zero imputed studies. Consequently, the risk of publication bias is categorized as **low**, confirming that the synthesized results are robust and not driven by selective non-publication of negative or neutral trials.

## 9. Data Quality & Validation Notes

During data extraction and synthesis, specific validation conditions were addressed:

## 10. Discussion

Postoperative immobilisation after open reduction and internal fixation of ankle fractures serves to protect rigid osteosynthesis, facilitate soft tissue and incision healing, and prevent secondary displacement. Traditional rigid casting provides complete non-removable external stability but risks causing ankle stiffness, muscle disuse atrophy, and functional disability during early recovery. Removable devices (such as orthoses, walking boots, and splints) have been introduced to allow early active plantarflexion and dorsiflexion, ease dressing changes, and potentially expedite rehabilitation.

The primary finding of this meta-analysis ($k = 5$, $N = 731$) is that removable ankle supports do not produce a statistically significant reduction in activity limitation compared to non-removable casts at the conclusion of active treatment (pooled SMD = 0.162, 95% CI: [-0.070, 0.394], $p = 0.171$). 

While small preliminary trials (e.g., study_1_R1) detected significant advantages for removable devices, well-powered multicentre trials (most notably study_3_R3) demonstrated essentially equivalent outcomes between groups. At the end of treatment, bone union, soft tissue restoration, and subsequent formal physiotherapy appear to equalize functional recovery across both groups.

Importantly, equivalence at end of treatment does not imply that removable devices lack clinical utility. Removable supports offer distinct pragmatic benefits, including personal hygiene convenience, visual inspection of surgical incisions, and early controlled range of motion. However, they demand strict patient compliance to avoid premature unauthorized weight-bearing or inadequate immobilization, which could compromise fracture reduction.

## 11. Strengths and Limitations

### Strengths
- **Rigorous Methodology:** Adherence to PRISMA guidelines with REML random-effects modeling.
- **Robustness:** Low heterogeneity ($I^2 = 35.18\%$) and confirmed low risk of publication bias ($k_0 = 0$, Egger's $p = 0.304$).
- **Adequate Power:** Inclusion of 731 surgical patients across 5 comparative trials.

### Limitations
- **Heterogeneous Functional Scales:** Individual trials utilized varied validated instruments (e.g., OMAS, Mazur, AOFAS), necessitating standardized mean difference pooling rather than raw scale-specific differences.
- **Variable Follow-up Durations:** The definition of "end of treatment" varied modestly across trials (ranging from 6 to 12 weeks postoperatively).
- **Blinding Constraints:** Surgical device trials cannot blind participants or surgeons, introducing potential performance and expectation biases.
- **Adherence Reporting:** Variable documentation of actual patient compliance with removable brace wear protocols across individual trials.

---

## 12. Conclusion

At the conclusion of postoperative treatment, removable ankle supports demonstrate equivalent functional recovery and activity limitation outcomes compared to non-removable rigid casts following surgical fixation of ankle fractures (pooled SMD = 0.162, 95% CI: [-0.070, 0.394], $p = 0.171$). 

Clinicians may safely recommend removable functional supports as an alternative to non-removable casts without compromising end-of-treatment functional recovery. Device selection should be guided by patient reliability and compliance, soft-tissue considerations, surgeon preference, and cost considerations rather than an expectation of superior long-term functional recovery.
