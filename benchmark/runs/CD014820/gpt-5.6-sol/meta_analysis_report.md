# Erythropoiesis-Stimulating Agents for Prevention of Acute Kidney Injury: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs) have been evaluated for prevention of acute kidney injury (AKI) in several high-risk clinical settings, but their overall effect remains uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of an ESA, compared with placebo or usual care without an ESA, on the incidence of AKI during trial follow-up.

**Methods:** Eligible participants were people at risk of AKI, including those undergoing cardiac surgery or kidney transplantation, experiencing critical illness or trauma, or receiving iodinated contrast. The intervention included any ESA; comparators were placebo or usual care without an ESA. The outcome was the number of participants developing at least one AKI event, using each trial’s definition and preferring KDIGO, then AKIN, then RIFLE when multiple definitions were available. Thirteen effect sizes from 18 studies were included in the quantitative pipeline, representing 3,861 participants. Risk ratios were pooled using a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model. Statistical heterogeneity and small-study effects were assessed.

**Results:** Across 13 comparisons, the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.9371875851531399 (95% CI 0.807–1.088; p = 0.39557084882166416). The supplied analysis classified the result as favoring neither arm, and the confidence interval included the null value. Heterogeneity was low to moderate (I² = 27.2%; τ² = 0.01614; Q = 20.905, p = 0.0518). Egger’s intercept was 0.3371 (p = 0.1034), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0); the supplied publication-bias assessment was low risk.

**Conclusions:** The available pooled evidence did not demonstrate a statistically significant reduction in AKI incidence with ESAs compared with placebo or usual care. The estimate was close to the null and remained compatible with both modest benefit and modest harm. Interpretation is limited by incomplete reporting of the search, trial characteristics, AKI definitions, arm-level event counts, risk of bias, and reasons why five of the 18 studies did not contribute to the pooled analysis.

## Introduction

Acute kidney injury (AKI) is a frequent complication among people exposed to cardiac surgery, kidney transplantation, critical illness, major trauma, and iodinated contrast. Even relatively mild AKI is associated with prolonged hospitalization, subsequent chronic kidney disease, cardiovascular complications, and mortality. Strategies capable of preventing renal injury in these settings therefore have substantial clinical importance.

Erythropoiesis-stimulating agents (ESAs), including epoetin alfa, beta, or zeta; darbepoetin; methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta; and related agents, have effects beyond stimulation of erythropoiesis. Experimental work has suggested possible anti-apoptotic, anti-inflammatory, and tissue-protective actions. These observations have led to clinical trials of ESAs as prophylactic interventions in people at risk of AKI. Potential renoprotection must nevertheless be considered alongside uncertainty about efficacy and the established thrombotic and hypertensive risks of ESAs in some clinical contexts.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among people at risk of AKI, what is the effect of an ESA, compared with placebo or usual care without an ESA, on the incidence of AKI during trial follow-up?

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

The prespecified population comprised people at risk of AKI, including those undergoing cardiac surgery or kidney transplantation, experiencing critical illness or trauma, or receiving iodinated contrast. Eligible interventions were any ESA, including epoetin alfa, beta, or zeta; darbepoetin; methoxy polyethylene glycol-epoetin beta; or another ESA. Eligible comparators were placebo or usual care without an ESA.

The outcome was binary AKI incidence during trial follow-up, defined as the number of randomized participants who experienced at least one AKI event. Each trial’s own AKI definition was to be retained. When more than one definition was reported, the intended hierarchy was KDIGO, followed by AKIN, RIFLE, and then the trial’s primary prespecified definition, such as a serum-creatinine rise or estimated-glomerular-filtration-rate decline. Dialysis requirement, continuous serum creatinine, and continuous estimated GFR were not acceptable substitutes for AKI incidence.

## Effect Sizes

The prespecified and pooled effect measure was the **Risk Ratio (RR)**, calculated on its native ratio scale. An RR of 1 indicates equal AKI risk in the ESA and control groups. Under the requested clinical orientation, an RR below 1 is numerically consistent with fewer AKI events in the ESA arm, whereas an RR above 1 is numerically consistent with fewer events in the control arm.

The context also supplied 13 study-level standardized diagnostic values under a field that is not the confirmed native RR scale. These ranged from −0.807940 to 0.383517 and were accompanied by confidence intervals. Because those values are not labeled as Risk Ratios and include negative numbers, they cannot be interpreted as trial-level RRs for a binary event. They were therefore not relabeled as RRs or used to make trial-specific clinical claims. Arm-level AKI event counts and trial-level native RRs were not provided.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled 13 comparisons involving 3,861 participants. The pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** was **0.9371875851531399**, with a **95% confidence interval of 0.807 to 1.088** and **p = 0.39557084882166416**.

The supplied analysis classified this result as favoring **neither arm**—essentially a tie—and the confidence interval crossed the null value of 1. Thus, the meta-analysis did not show a statistically significant difference in AKI incidence between ESA and control groups. The confidence interval remains compatible with a modest relative reduction as well as a modest relative increase in AKI risk.

Because baseline AKI risk and arm-level event counts were not supplied, the pooled RR cannot be translated into an absolute risk difference or number needed to treat or harm. No subgroup-specific pooled analyses were provided for cardiac surgery, transplantation, critical illness, trauma, contrast exposure, ESA type, dose, timing, or AKI definition.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was low to moderate:

- **I² = 27.2%**
- **τ² = 0.01614**
- **Cochran’s Q = 20.905**
- **Q-test p = 0.0518**

The Q test was close to, but did not cross, the conventional 0.05 threshold. The I² estimate indicates that approximately 27.2% of the observed variability was attributed by the model to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error. The nonzero τ² indicates some between-study dispersion, supporting the use of a random-effects model. Nevertheless, heterogeneity estimates based on 13 comparisons are imprecise, and the lack of trial-level clinical information prevents assessment of whether variation reflects population, intervention, co-intervention, follow-up, or AKI-definition differences.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression yielded an intercept of **0.3371** with **p = 0.1034**. This was not statistically significant and provided no clear evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. The trim-and-fill procedure estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies. The supplied trim-and-fill diagnostic value was **−0.0542**, but it was not labeled on the confirmed native RR scale and therefore should not be interpreted as an adjusted pooled RR. The pipeline classified the overall risk of publication bias as **low**.

These analyses should be interpreted cautiously. With only 13 comparisons, tests of funnel-plot asymmetry have limited power, and asymmetry—when present—can arise from genuine heterogeneity or methodological differences rather than publication bias alone. Conversely, a nonsignificant Egger test does not establish the absence of selective publication.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

In this random-effects meta-analysis of 13 comparisons and 3,861 participants, ESA administration was not associated with a statistically significant difference in AKI incidence relative to placebo or usual care. The pooled Risk Ratio was 0.9371875851531399 (95% CI 0.807–1.088; p = 0.39557084882166416), and the supplied result classification favored neither arm. Between-study heterogeneity was low to moderate (I² = 27.2%), while formal analyses did not provide clear evidence of small-study effects.

The result does not support a definitive renoprotective effect of ESAs. Equally, it does not establish equivalence or exclude clinically meaningful effects in either direction. The confidence interval includes relative effects ranging from a potentially modest reduction to a potentially modest increase in AKI. The absence of absolute event rates also prevents evaluation of how these relative limits would translate into patient-important absolute effects in populations with different baseline risks.

### Clinical interpretation

Any decision to use an ESA for AKI prevention would require evidence that renal benefit outweighs treatment burden and potential adverse effects. This synthesis provides no statistically significant evidence of lower AKI incidence. Moreover, the supplied results contain no pooled safety outcomes, including thrombosis, hypertension, stroke, graft thrombosis, cardiovascular events, or mortality. The present analysis therefore cannot support prophylactic ESA use solely to prevent AKI, nor can it define a favorable benefit–harm balance.

Clinical diversity may be important even though statistical heterogeneity was not high. Ischemia-reperfusion injury after cardiac surgery or transplantation, inflammatory injury in critical illness or trauma, and contrast-associated kidney injury differ in pathophysiology, timing, and baseline risk. ESA regimens may likewise differ in molecule, dose, administration timing, and exposure duration. A single pooled estimate can summarize the available comparisons but cannot establish that effects are uniform across these settings.

### Strength and certainty of the evidence

The synthesis used the requested binary outcome and native Risk Ratio scale, and the random-effects REML model allowed for between-study variation. The overall sample size was 3,861, including two relatively large comparisons of 923 and 1,302 participants. However, no formal certainty-of-evidence assessment was supplied. A GRADE rating cannot be assigned reliably without study-level risk-of-bias information, trial characteristics, outcome definitions, attrition, indirectness assessment, and native event counts.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations arising from the supplied evidence package:

1. **Incomplete search documentation:** Databases, registries, search dates, search strategies, language restrictions, grey-literature methods, and citation-searching procedures were unavailable.
2. **Incomplete screening information:** A PRISMA flow diagram and stage-specific exclusion reasons could not be reconstructed.
3. **Unexplained missing quantitative contributions:** Five of 18 studies did not contribute to the 13-effect-size meta-analysis, and the reasons were not given.
4. **Absent native study-level data:** Numbers randomized and numbers experiencing AKI in each arm were not supplied. Trial-level RRs could not be independently verified.
5. **Unavailable AKI definitions:** The report cannot document whether individual studies used KDIGO, AKIN, RIFLE, or another prespecified definition, despite the clinical importance of this variation.
6. **Limited study characterization:** Clinical setting, ESA regimen, comparator details, follow-up, baseline renal function, anemia status, and demographic characteristics were not reported.
7. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** Internal validity and the possible influence of trial methods could not be evaluated.
8. **No safety synthesis:** Potential adverse consequences of ESA treatment were outside the supplied analysis.
9. **No moderator estimates:** Differences across clinical settings, interventions, or outcome definitions remain unresolved.
10. **Limited power for publication-bias tests:** Egger’s test and trim-and-fill are not definitive with 13 comparisons.
11. **No certainty assessment:** The certainty of evidence could not be graded formally.
12. **Potential document-classification uncertainty:** One retained input could not be confidently classified by document type.

## Conclusion

Among people at risk of AKI, pooled evidence from 13 comparisons involving 3,861 participants did not demonstrate a statistically significant effect of ESAs on AKI incidence compared with placebo or usual care. The random-effects pooled Risk Ratio was 0.9371875851531399 (95% CI 0.807–1.088; p = 0.39557084882166416), with low-to-moderate heterogeneity (I² = 27.2%). The supplied analysis classified the result as favoring neither arm. There was no clear statistical evidence of small-study effects, although the relevant tests had limited power.

On the basis of these results, ESAs cannot be considered proven prophylaxis for AKI. Future evidence syntheses should retrieve native randomized arm-level event counts, document trial-specific AKI definitions and follow-up periods, assess risk of bias, separate clinically distinct settings where appropriate, evaluate adverse events, and report absolute effects alongside relative effects.
