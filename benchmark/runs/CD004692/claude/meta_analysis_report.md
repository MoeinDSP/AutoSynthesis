# Omega-3 Polyunsaturated Fatty Acid Supplementation and Quality of Life in Adults with Depression: A PRISMA-Compliant Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs; eicosapentaenoic acid [EPA], docosahexaenoic acid [DHA], or their combination) are among the most widely used nutraceutical adjuncts in depression. Most evidence syntheses have focused on symptom severity, while quality of life (QoL) — a patient-centred endpoint that integrates functioning, well-being and treatment burden — has rarely been pooled.

**Objective.** To estimate the effect of omega-3 PUFA supplementation versus placebo on quality of life at end of treatment in adults with major depressive disorder, elevated depressive symptoms, or depression comorbid with a medical or neurological condition.

**Methods.** Randomised placebo-controlled trials of EPA, DHA or EPA+DHA capsules/oil in adults with depression reporting a validated generic or disease-specific QoL/health-related QoL/well-being instrument at end of treatment were eligible. One QoL measure was extracted per trial arm (preferring total/overall scores), as end-of-treatment mean, SD and n per arm; standard errors, 95% confidence intervals and interquartile ranges were converted to SDs where required, and all scales were oriented so that a higher score denotes better QoL. Effect sizes were computed as Hedges' g and pooled with a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Nine reports met full-text eligibility criteria; four randomised comparisons (n = 305 participants) provided extractable end-of-treatment QoL data and entered the quantitative synthesis. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 0.487 (95% CI −0.062 to 1.035; p = 0.082; k = 4), favouring omega-3 PUFA supplementation but not reaching conventional statistical significance. In the user-requested mirror-image reporting convention (SMD < 0 = favours omega-3) the same result is expressed as SMD = −0.487 (95% CI −1.035 to 0.062). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial to considerable (Q = 13.15, df = 3, p = 0.0043; I² = 80.28%; τ² = 0.243184), with individual g values ranging from −0.007 to 1.318. Egger's regression indicated significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = −0.9152, p = 0.017; bias risk rated high), although trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0, adjusted g = 0.4865).

**Conclusions.** Across a small evidence base of four randomised comparisons, omega-3 PUFA supplementation was associated with a moderate-sized but statistically non-significant advantage in end-of-treatment quality of life over placebo, with considerable unexplained heterogeneity and statistically significant small-study asymmetry. The pooled estimate is fragile and potentially inflated; it should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as evidence of benefit. Adequately powered trials that report validated QoL instruments as pre-specified endpoints are needed.

## 1. Introduction

Major depressive disorder is a leading contributor to years lived with disability worldwide, and its burden extends well beyond symptom counts to impaired role functioning, social participation, vitality and subjective well-being. Pharmacological and psychological treatments produce, on average, moderate reductions in symptom severity, yet many patients continue to report substantially degraded quality of life (QoL) even when they meet criteria for symptomatic response. This divergence between symptom-based and patient-centred endpoints has motivated increasing interest in outcomes such as health-related quality of life (HRQoL), well-being and functioning as primary targets of depression treatment.

Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) — principally eicosapentaenoic acid (EPA) and docosahexaenoic acid (DHA) — have been investigated as monotherapy and as adjuncts in depression for more than two decades. Proposed mechanisms include modulation of neuroinflammatory signalling, membrane fluidity and monoaminergic neurotransmission, alteration of hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal axis reactivity, and effects on neurotrophic factors. Omega-3 PUFAs are also attractive because of their favourable tolerability profile, their availability without prescription, and their plausible benefits in populations where depression is comorbid with cardiometabolic, oncological, renal or neurological disease — precisely the populations in whom HRQoL is most compromised and most clinically meaningful.

Existing meta-analyses of omega-3 PUFAs in depression have focused almost exclusively on depressive symptom severity, where pooled estimates are small-to-moderate, highly heterogeneous, and sensitive to EPA dose fraction and to publication bias. Quality of life has been reported inconsistently as a secondary endpoint and, to our knowledge, has not been synthesised systematically in this population. Given that symptom change and QoL change correlate only imperfectly, a dedicated synthesis is warranted.

**Objective.** This systematic review and meta-analysis estimates the effect of omega-3 PUFA supplementation (EPA, DHA, or EPA+DHA, given as capsules/oil) compared with placebo on quality of life at end of treatment in adults with major depressive disorder, elevated depressive symptoms, or depression comorbid with a medical or neurological condition, pooled as a random-effects standardised mean difference.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

The review was conducted and reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement. The review question was framed using the PICO structure set out below.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults (≥18 years) with major depressive disorder diagnosed by standardised criteria, with elevated depressive symptoms on a validated screening or severity instrument, or with depression comorbid with a medical or neurological condition (e.g. cardiovascular disease, chronic kidney disease, cancer, multiple sclerosis, Parkinson's disease).

**Intervention.** Omega-3 PUFA supplementation delivered as capsules, soft-gels or oil, comprising EPA alone, DHA alone, or an EPA+DHA combination, at any dose and for any duration, as monotherapy or as an adjunct to usual care.

**Comparator.** Placebo — an inert oil (e.g. paraffin, olive, corn, soybean or medium-chain triglyceride oil) or a matched placebo capsule.

**Outcome.** Quality of life at end of treatment, measured with any validated generic or disease-specific QoL/HRQoL/well-being instrument (e.g. SF-36 or SF-12 total or component summary scores, WHOQOL-BREF, EQ-5D index or visual analogue scale, Q-LES-Q, Quality of Life Index, FACT, MSQoL-54, PDQ-39, KDQOL).

**Design.** Randomised controlled trials with a placebo comparator.

**Exclusions.** Non-randomised designs; trials in populations without depression or elevated depressive symptoms; dietary-advice or whole-food fish interventions without supplement capsules; studies reporting only change-from-baseline scores without usable dispersion data, only responder dichotomies, or no validated QoL instrument; reviews, editorials, protocols and conference abstracts without extractable data; duplicate reports of the same randomised comparison.

### 2.3 Information sources and search strategy

Electronic databases (MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials and Web of Science) were searched from inception, supplemented by trial registry records (ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP) and backward/forward citation chasing of included reports and of prior systematic reviews of omega-3 PUFAs in depression. The search combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms across three concept blocks: (i) *omega-3 / n-3 / polyunsaturated fatty acid / fish oil / EPA / eicosapentaenoic / DHA / docosahexaenoic*; (ii) *depression / depressive disorder / major depressive / depressive symptoms / dysthymia*; and (iii) *quality of life / health-related quality of life / HRQoL / well-being / SF-36 / SF-12 / WHOQOL / EQ-5D / Q-LES-Q / functioning*, intersected with a randomised controlled trial filter. No language restriction was applied at the search stage.

### 2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Records were de-duplicated and screened at title/abstract level, followed by full-text assessment against the eligibility criteria. For each eligible trial, one QoL measure was extracted per trial arm, applying a pre-specified hierarchy: (1) the instrument total or overall score; (2) where no total exists, the global/general QoL item; (3) where neither exists, the mean of the available summary components. Extracted quantities were the end-of-treatment mean, standard deviation and analysed n per arm. Standard errors, 95% confidence intervals and interquartile ranges were converted to SDs using standard formulae. All scales were oriented so that a **higher score denotes better quality of life**, with the sign reversed for instruments on which higher scores indicate worse QoL (e.g. PDQ-39).

### 2.5 Effect size computation and synthesis

For each comparison, the standardised mean difference was computed from end-of-treatment means, SDs and sample sizes and corrected for small-sample bias (Hedges' g), with the variance derived from the standard large-sample expression. A **positive g denotes better quality of life in the omega-3 arm** (omega-3 favoured). Because the research question specified a mirror-image reporting convention in which SMD < 0 favours omega-3, pooled estimates are additionally reported with the sign flipped; both presentations are given explicitly to avoid ambiguity.

Effect sizes were pooled using a random-effects model with between-study variance estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The effect measure used for pooling was the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD), specified by explicit user request; native-scale pooling on this metric was used (i.e. the reported statistic is a genuine SMD-scale result, not a converted fallback), and no conflict between the requested and the computed effect measure was detected.

### 2.6 Heterogeneity, moderators and publication bias

Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, the I² statistic and τ². Pre-specified moderators of interest were EPA dose and EPA:DHA ratio, treatment duration, depression population type (primary MDD versus depression comorbid with medical/neurological disease), QoL instrument class (generic versus disease-specific) and baseline depression severity; these were to be examined by subgroup analysis or meta-regression only if a sufficient number of studies (conventionally k ≥ 10 per covariate) were available. Small-study effects were assessed by visual inspection of the funnel plot, Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry, and the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

## 3. Search and Screening

The search and screening process followed the PRISMA 2020 flow. After de-duplication, records were screened at title/abstract level and potentially eligible reports were retrieved for full-text assessment. **Nine reports** satisfied the full-text eligibility criteria for the review (population, intervention, comparator and design) and constitute the included study set for the systematic review.

Of these nine reports, **four randomised comparisons** provided end-of-treatment quality-of-life data in a form that could be converted into a standardised mean difference (end-of-treatment mean, SD or convertible dispersion statistic, and analysed n for both the omega-3 and the placebo arm) and therefore entered the quantitative synthesis. The remaining five reports were retained in the narrative review but could not be pooled, for reasons that included: reporting QoL only as change-from-baseline without usable dispersion statistics; reporting only subscale profiles with no total, global item, or derivable component mean consistent with the pre-specified extraction hierarchy; reporting QoL only at a timepoint other than end of treatment; or reporting results graphically or as responder proportions without numeric means and SDs. No eligible trial was excluded on language grounds.

The ratio of included reports to poolable comparisons (9 → 4) is itself an important finding: fewer than half of the trials that measured quality of life in this literature reported it in a form that permits quantitative synthesis.

Across the four comparisons, the total analysed sample was **305 participants**. Individual trial sizes ranged from 35 to 118 participants, with a median of approximately 76; no comparison exceeded 120 participants, so all four would conventionally be classified as small trials for a continuous patient-reported outcome. In all four, the comparator was a placebo capsule or inert oil, and the intervention was an EPA-containing, DHA-containing or combined EPA+DHA preparation administered as capsules/oil, consistent with the review's intervention definition. ### 4.1 Risk of bias considerations

Because all four comparisons were placebo-controlled randomised trials, allocation concealment and blinding of participants and personnel were in principle protected; however, omega-3 preparations can be partially unblinded by fishy aftertaste or eructation, which is a recognised threat to blinding integrity in this literature and is particularly consequential for self-reported endpoints such as QoL. Additional domain-level concerns that apply generically to this evidence base include attrition in longer trials, the frequent designation of QoL as a secondary or exploratory endpoint (raising the possibility of selective outcome reporting, consistent with the 9 → 4 reporting attrition documented above), and the heterogeneity of analysis populations (completer versus intention-to-treat). These considerations should be borne in mind when interpreting the pooled estimate; the small number of comparisons precluded formal risk-of-bias subgroup analysis.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Effect sizes were computed as Hedges' g from end-of-treatment means, SDs and sample sizes, with a positive value indicating better quality of life in the omega-3 arm.

The four comparisons produced a notably dispersed set of estimates spanning essentially the full range from null to large:

- **study_5_R5** (n = 118): g = −0.007 (95% CI −0.368 to 0.353) — an effectively zero difference, with a confidence interval tightly centred on no effect.
- **study_1_R1** (n = 106): g = 0.259 (95% CI −0.124 to 0.642) — a small advantage for omega-3 that is compatible with no effect.
- **study_4_R4** (n = 35): g = 0.563 (95% CI −0.113 to 1.239) — a moderate point estimate with a wide interval crossing the null.
- **study_2_R2** (n = 46): g = 1.318 (95% CI 0.680 to 1.956) — a large and statistically significant advantage for omega-3, and the only individually significant comparison.

Only one of the four comparisons (study_2_R2) excluded zero from its confidence interval. The ordering of effect sizes shows a clear inverse relationship with sample size: the two largest comparisons (n = 118 and n = 106) produced the two smallest effects (g = −0.007 and g = 0.259), whereas the two smallest comparisons (n = 35 and n = 46) produced the two largest effects (g = 0.563 and g = 1.318). This pattern is the classic signature of small-study effects and is quantified formally in Section 8.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

Pooling the four comparisons under a random-effects model with REML estimation of between-study variance yielded a **pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) of 0.487 (95% CI −0.062 to 1.035; p = 0.082; k = 4; n = 305)**. The effect measure is the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD), as explicitly requested, and native-scale pooling on this metric was used, so the value is a confirmed SMD-scale result rather than a standardized fallback conversion.

Per the direction field of the analysis, the pooled result **favours omega-3 PUFA supplementation (EPA, DHA, or EPA+DHA capsules/oil)**. However, the 95% confidence interval includes zero and the p value of 0.082 does not reach the conventional 0.05 threshold; the result is therefore best described as a moderate-sized point estimate favouring omega-3 that is **statistically compatible with no effect**. The interval spans values from a small advantage for placebo (−0.062) to a large advantage for omega-3 (1.035), so the data are consistent with anything from a trivial disadvantage to a substantial benefit.

**Reporting convention.** Under the analytic convention used throughout this report, a positive g favours omega-3. The research question additionally requested the mirror-image convention in which SMD < 0 favours omega-3; expressed that way, the identical result is **SMD = −0.487 (95% CI −1.035 to 0.062)**, again favouring omega-3 and again not statistically significant. The two presentations are numerically equivalent and differ only in sign.

**Magnitude.** By conventional benchmarks, g ≈ 0.49 is a moderate effect. If taken at face value on a generic HRQoL instrument, an SMD of this size would correspond to roughly 4–6 points on a 0–100 SF-36-type summary scale (assuming a between-person SD of 8–12 points), which approaches commonly cited minimally important difference thresholds. This translation should be treated as illustrative only, given that the confidence interval includes zero, that heterogeneity is considerable, and that the estimate is pooled across different instruments.

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) illustrates how unstable the summary estimate is as comparisons accrue — with only four studies, the pooled value and its interval shift materially with the addition of each trial, and the summary is not approaching an asymptote.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **substantial to considerable**:

- Cochran's **Q = 13.15** on 3 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.0043** — the null hypothesis of homogeneity is rejected.
- **I² = 80.28%** — approximately four-fifths of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to genuine between-study differences rather than sampling error; this falls in the "considerable heterogeneity" band of the Cochrane classification.
- **τ² = 0.243184** — corresponding to a between-study standard deviation of effects of τ ≈ 0.49 on the g scale, which is as large as the pooled effect itself.

The practical implication of τ ≈ 0.49 is that the approximate 95% prediction interval for the true effect in a new, exchangeable trial is extremely wide and comfortably spans zero in both directions — on the order of roughly −1.9 to +2.9 given only three degrees of freedom for the τ estimate. In other words, the data do not support a confident prediction that a future trial would show benefit; they are consistent with future trials showing substantial benefit, no effect, or harm to quality of life. The width of this interval is driven both by genuine dispersion (τ²) and by the extreme imprecision with which τ² itself is estimated from only four studies — with k = 4, both I² and τ² carry very large uncertainty, and the point estimates reported above should not be over-interpreted.

Inspection of the forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) shows that the heterogeneity is dominated by the divergence between study_2_R2 (g = 1.318, CI 0.680 to 1.956) and the two larger comparisons study_5_R5 (g = −0.007) and study_1_R1 (g = 0.259), whose confidence intervals do not overlap with that of study_2_R2 at all. The heterogeneity diagnostics are summarised graphically at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

Given the non-overlap and the leverage of a single large-effect small trial, the pooled random-effects estimate is best regarded as an average of qualitatively dissimilar results rather than as an estimate of a common underlying effect. Formal influence diagnostics (leave-one-out analysis) were not interpretable with k = 4, but it is evident from the data that removal of study_2_R2 would substantially reduce both the pooled estimate and the heterogeneity.

## 8. Moderators

Pre-specified moderators — EPA dose and EPA:DHA ratio, treatment duration, primary MDD versus depression comorbid with a medical or neurological condition, generic versus disease-specific QoL instrument, and baseline depression severity — could **not** be examined by subgroup analysis or meta-regression. With only **k = 4** comparisons and three degrees of freedom available for the heterogeneity estimate, any moderator model would be fitted on fewer studies than covariate parameters are meaningfully supported by; the widely applied minimum of approximately ten studies per covariate for meta-regression is not remotely met. Subgroup analyses would likewise have reduced to comparisons of one or two studies per subgroup and would have been uninterpretable.

The only moderator-like pattern that can be described, and then only descriptively, is the relationship between **precision and effect magnitude**. As noted in Section 5, the two comparisons with the largest samples returned near-null effects (n = 118, g = −0.007; n = 106, g = 0.259), while the two smallest returned the largest effects (n = 46, g = 1.318; n = 35, g = 0.563). This gradient is visualised in the bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, in which bubble area reflects study weight. Interpreted cautiously, it suggests that study size/precision — rather than any substantive clinical or pharmacological moderator — may be the dominant correlate of effect size in this dataset. This is a bias-relevant rather than a biologically informative pattern and is formally tested in the next section.

Accordingly, **no moderator is identified as explaining the considerable heterogeneity observed**, and the sources of between-study variation (which plausibly include differences in EPA content, trial duration, comorbidity profile, instrument sensitivity and baseline QoL impairment) remain unresolved by these data.

## 9. Publication Bias

Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. The overall risk of publication bias was rated **high**.

- **Egger's regression:** intercept = **−0.9152**, **p = 0.017**. This indicates statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry — the association between effect size and precision is stronger than would be expected by chance, consistent with small-study effects that may reflect publication bias, selective outcome reporting (particularly plausible here given that QoL was frequently a secondary endpoint), or methodological differences between small and large trials.
- **Trim-and-fill:** **k₀ = 0** imputed studies; adjusted pooled g = **0.4865**, i.e. identical to the observed estimate.

These two results are discrepant and must be interpreted together rather than selectively. The significant Egger test signals asymmetry, while the rank-based trim-and-fill algorithm imputed no missing studies, so the bias-adjusted estimate is unchanged. As noted in the analysis interpretation, this is a common pattern that arises when asymmetry is driven by heterogeneity, by a small number of influential small trials, or by asymmetry in a direction the rank-based algorithm does not correct; with k = 4 the trim-and-fill procedure also has almost no power to detect or correct suppression. The null trim-and-fill result should therefore **not** be read as reassurance.

The overall conclusion is that the pooled effect should be interpreted cautiously as **potentially inflated**. Corroborating analyses — a contour-enhanced funnel plot, PET-PEESE, or formal selection models — would be advisable before any firmer inference, although all such methods are themselves unreliable at k = 4. It is also worth noting that the near-null estimates come from the two largest trials, which is precisely the configuration under which a plausible "true" effect would be closer to the small/negligible end of the reported confidence interval than to the point estimate of 0.487.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 Summary of findings

In adults with major depressive disorder, elevated depressive symptoms, or depression comorbid with a medical or neurological condition, omega-3 PUFA supplementation was associated with a pooled Standardized Mean Difference of 0.487 (95% CI −0.062 to 1.035; p = 0.082) in end-of-treatment quality of life relative to placebo, across four randomised comparisons and 305 participants. The direction of the pooled result favours omega-3 supplementation, but the estimate is not statistically significant, is accompanied by considerable heterogeneity (I² = 80.28%; τ² = 0.243184; Q = 13.15, p = 0.0043), and is subject to statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger's p = 0.017). In the user's mirror-image convention the same finding is SMD = −0.487 (95% CI −1.035 to 0.062).

The honest summary is therefore one of **insufficient evidence**: the data neither establish that omega-3 PUFAs improve quality of life in depressed adults nor exclude a clinically meaningful benefit. The point estimate is of a size that would matter to patients if real, which is precisely why the imprecision and the bias signals are consequential rather than academic.

### 10.2 Relation to the wider literature

The pattern observed here — a moderate point estimate, high heterogeneity and significant small-study asymmetry, driven substantially by one small trial with a large effect while the largest trials are null — closely mirrors what has repeatedly been reported for omega-3 PUFAs on **depressive symptom severity**. In that literature, pooled benefits attenuate markedly when analyses are restricted to large, low-risk-of-bias trials or when bias-adjustment methods are applied. The present analysis suggests the same caution applies to quality of life, with the additional complication that QoL has typically been a secondary endpoint and is accordingly more exposed to selective reporting.

A further noteworthy observation is the **reporting attrition** documented in Section 3: nine reports met eligibility criteria for the review, yet only four supplied end-of-treatment QoL data in a poolable format. Incomplete and non-standardised reporting of patient-reported outcomes is thus a first-order obstacle to synthesis in this field, independent of the number of trials conducted.

### 10.3 Clinical implications

For clinicians, these results do not support recommending omega-3 PUFA supplementation specifically for the purpose of improving quality of life in depression. Omega-3 preparations are generally well tolerated and inexpensive, and decisions about their use will reasonably continue to rest on considerations of symptom response, cardiometabolic indications, patient preference and tolerability. What the present synthesis adds is that the QoL evidence base is too small, too heterogeneous and too asymmetric to justify a QoL-specific claim in either direction. Patients who ask about omega-3 supplementation for well-being should be told that the evidence is currently inconclusive.

### 10.4 Research implications

Three priorities follow. First, **adequately powered trials**: all four poolable comparisons had fewer than 120 participants, and detecting a g of 0.3 with 80% power requires roughly 350 participants per arm — an order of magnitude beyond the current evidence base. Second, **pre-specified, fully reported QoL endpoints** using a small number of validated instruments (e.g. SF-36/SF-12 summary scores, WHOQOL-BREF, EQ-5D, Q-LES-Q), with end-of-treatment means, SDs and analysed n reported numerically for every arm and timepoint, in line with CONSORT-PRO guidance. Third, **mechanistic and dose characterisation**: EPA content, EPA:DHA ratio, achieved plasma or erythrocyte fatty acid levels, and adherence should be reported so that future syntheses can test the dose–response hypotheses that could not be examined here.

## 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were raised for this synthesis. Nonetheless, two data-quality features of the analytic dataset warrant plain statement:

1. **Report-to-comparison attrition.** Nine reports met full-text eligibility criteria for the review, but only **four** supplied end-of-treatment quality-of-life data extractable as mean, SD and n per arm; the remaining five contribute to the narrative review only. The pooled estimate therefore rests on fewer than half of the eligible trials, and the possibility that unpooled trials differ systematically from pooled ones (selective outcome reporting) cannot be excluded.
2. **Effect measure provenance.** The pooled statistic is a **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, specified by explicit user request, with native-scale pooling used (`native_pooling_used = true`). The reported value is thus a confirmed SMD-scale result and not a standardized fallback conversion, and no conflict between requested and computed effect measures was detected. Only one outcome/timepoint group was analysed, so no separate-measure pooling was required.

A deterministic Effect Measure Summary table accompanies this report; the values reported in the text above are consistent with it.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small number of comparisons (k = 4).** This is the overriding limitation. It renders τ² and I² highly imprecise, makes the prediction interval essentially uninformative, precludes meta-regression and subgroup analysis, and leaves publication-bias diagnostics severely underpowered. Conventional guidance would question whether pooling is advisable at all with four studies and I² above 80%; the pooled value is reported here as requested but should be read as a descriptive average rather than a dependable parameter estimate.
2. **Small total sample (n = 305).** No single comparison exceeded 118 participants, so the synthesis is a collection of small trials, each individually underpowered for a continuous patient-reported endpoint.
3. **Considerable unexplained heterogeneity.** With I² = 80.28% and non-overlapping confidence intervals between the largest-effect and the largest-sample trials, the comparisons may not be estimating a common effect, and no moderator could be identified to explain the dispersion.
4. **Significant funnel asymmetry and high bias risk.** Egger's p = 0.017 indicates small-study effects; the null trim-and-fill result (k₀ = 0) provides no genuine reassurance at this k. The pooled estimate may be inflated.
5. **Instrument heterogeneity.** Pooling across different generic and disease-specific QoL instruments, re-oriented to a common direction and standardised, assumes approximate comparability of the underlying construct and of between-person variability. Different instruments have different ceiling effects, recall periods and responsiveness, which contributes to heterogeneity in an unquantifiable way.
6. **Clinical heterogeneity of the population.** Eligibility deliberately spanned primary MDD, elevated depressive symptoms, and depression comorbid with medical or neurological disease. Baseline QoL impairment, scope for improvement, and the plausibility of omega-3 benefit (e.g. via cardiometabolic pathways) differ across these groups, but k = 4 precluded any stratified analysis.
7. **Blinding integrity.** The characteristic taste/eructation of fish-oil preparations can partially unblind participants, a particular concern for self-reported QoL outcomes.
8. **Outcome timing.** Analyses were restricted to end of treatment, which varied across trials; durability of any effect beyond the active treatment period was not assessed, and no follow-up synthesis was possible.
9. **Unavailable analyses.** Formal GRADE certainty rating, leave-one-out influence analysis, contour-enhanced funnel plotting and PET-PEESE were not performed as part of this synthesis; on the basis of the available information, the certainty of evidence would in any case be expected to be low or very low because of imprecision, inconsistency and suspected publication bias.

---

## 13. Conclusion

Across four randomised placebo-controlled comparisons comprising 305 adults with depression, omega-3 PUFA supplementation produced a pooled Standardized Mean Difference in end-of-treatment quality of life of **0.487 (95% CI −0.062 to 1.035; p = 0.082)** — equivalently **−0.487 (95% CI −1.035 to 0.062)** in the mirror-image convention in which negative values favour omega-3. The result **favours omega-3 PUFA supplementation** in direction but is not statistically significant, is accompanied by considerable and unexplained between-study heterogeneity (I² = 80.28%; τ² = 0.243184; Q = 13.15, df = 3, p = 0.0043), and shows statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger's intercept = −0.9152, p = 0.017; bias risk high), with trim-and-fill imputing no studies (k₀ = 0) and therefore leaving the estimate unadjusted but not vindicated.

Given that the two largest trials returned essentially null effects while the two smallest drove the positive summary, the pooled estimate is fragile and plausibly inflated. Pre-specified moderator analyses were not possible at k = 4. The appropriate conclusion is that **the current evidence is insufficient to determine whether omega-3 PUFA supplementation improves quality of life in adults with depression**. Large, adequately powered randomised trials that pre-specify and completely report validated quality-of-life instruments are required before any clinical recommendation on this endpoint can be made.
