# Omega-3 Polyunsaturated Fatty Acid Supplementation and End-of-Treatment Quality of Life in Adults With Depression: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFAs) have been studied as adjunctive or standalone supplements for adults with major depressive disorder, depressive symptoms, and depression associated with medical or neurological conditions. This review evaluated their effect on end-of-treatment quality of life relative to placebo.

**Methods:** Eligible studies were randomised, placebo-controlled trials in adults with depression that compared EPA, DHA, or combined EPA+DHA supplementation with placebo and reported end-of-treatment quality of life. One quality-of-life instrument per trial was to be selected, preferentially its total or overall score, and oriented so that higher raw scores represented better quality of life. Standardized Mean Differences (SMDs) were pooled with a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Under the prespecified sign convention, negative values favor omega-3 and positive values favor placebo.

**Results:** The supplied pipeline contained nine studies, but only two study-level quality-of-life effect estimates, representing 152 participants, were available for quantitative synthesis. The estimates were 0.258969 (95% CI −0.124 to 0.642; n = 106) and 1.821142 (95% CI 1.133 to 2.509; n = 46). The pooled SMD was 1.0128290868066774 (95% CI −0.517 to 2.543; p = 0.19446571302516819). The supplied meta-analysis result classifies this estimate as favoring omega-3 supplementation. Heterogeneity was considerable: Q(1) = 15.132, p = 0.0001; I² = 93.39%; and τ² = 1.139554. The confidence interval included no effect.

**Conclusions:** Based on two trials, the pooled result was imprecise, statistically non-significant, and highly heterogeneous. Although the supplied `favors` classification identifies omega-3 supplementation as favored, that classification conflicts with the prespecified sign convention because the pooled value is positive. The direction coding therefore requires verification before a directional clinical conclusion can be accepted. The evidence is insufficient to establish that omega-3 supplementation improves end-of-treatment quality of life in adults with depression.

## Search and Screening

The review question was defined using the following eligibility framework:

- **Population:** Adults with major depressive disorder, depressive symptoms, or depression occurring in a medical or neurological condition.
- **Intervention:** Omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid supplementation as EPA, DHA, or combined EPA+DHA capsules or supplements.
- **Comparator:** Placebo.
- **Design:** Randomised placebo-controlled trials.
- **Outcome:** End-of-treatment quality of life, using one quality-of-life instrument per trial and preferentially the total or overall score.

Depression severity, treatment response, attrition, and adverse events were outside the outcome scope of this review. Quality-of-life outcomes were to be oriented so that higher raw scores indicated better quality of life; scores or signs were to be reversed where necessary. Arm-level means, standard deviations, and analysed sample sizes were the intended inputs, with standard errors converted to standard deviations when required.

The supplied pipeline summary listed **nine studies**. However, only **two quality-of-life effect estimates** were available for quantitative synthesis. The supplied information did not include database names, search dates, full search strings, deduplication counts, title/abstract screening totals, full-text exclusion reasons, or a PRISMA flow diagram. Consequently, the search cannot be reproduced and a complete PRISMA study-flow accounting cannot be reconstructed from the available results. The status of the other seven studies—such as whether they lacked an eligible quality-of-life outcome, lacked extractable statistics, or were otherwise excluded from this specific synthesis—was not reported and should not be inferred.

## Included Studies

Two trials, identified only as `study_1_R1` and `study_2_R2`, contributed to the meta-analysis. Their combined analysed sample was **152 participants**, comprising 106 participants in `study_1_R1` and 46 in `study_2_R2`.

No trial-level bibliographic citations or details about country, recruitment setting, depression diagnosis, comorbid medical or neurological conditions, omega-3 formulation, EPA:DHA ratio, dosage, treatment duration, placebo composition, quality-of-life instrument, or risk of bias were supplied. It was therefore not possible to characterize clinical diversity, confirm the appropriateness of selecting a particular quality-of-life scale, or evaluate trial-level methodological limitations.

The two effect estimates differ markedly in magnitude, suggesting potentially important differences between the included populations, interventions, outcome instruments, treatment durations, or study methods. These possibilities could not be investigated with the available metadata.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model using **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** pooled the two trial estimates. The resulting **pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** was **1.0128290868066774**, with a **95% CI from −0.517 to 2.543** and **p = 0.19446571302516819**. The analysis included **k = 2 trials** and **152 participants**.

The supplied meta-analysis record designates the result as favoring **omega-3 polyunsaturated fatty acid supplementation**. The pooled result was not statistically significant because its confidence interval included zero and its p value exceeded 0.05. Its confidence interval was also wide, encompassing no effect and effects of substantial magnitude.

There is an internal discrepancy in the supplied results. The prespecified convention states that negative values favor omega-3 and positive values favor placebo, yet the positive pooled SMD of 1.0128290868066774 is marked in the supplied `favors` field as favoring omega-3. ## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was considerable:

- **Cochran’s Q(1) = 15.132**
- **Q-test p = 0.0001**
- **I² = 93.39%**
- **τ² = 1.139554**

The Q test rejects the hypothesis that the studies share a common underlying effect, while I² indicates that a very high proportion of observed variability was attributed to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error. The estimated between-study variance was also substantial on the SMD scale.

These statistics must nevertheless be interpreted cautiously. With only two studies, heterogeneity estimates are unstable, and neither the magnitude nor the causes of heterogeneity can be estimated reliably. The large separation between the two study estimates—0.258969 versus 1.821142—is directly consistent with the high Q and I² values. A prediction interval was not supplied and was not reconstructed.

## Publication Bias

Publication bias and small-study effects could not be meaningfully assessed with **k = 2**. Funnel-plot asymmetry tests generally have very low power with small numbers of studies and may be misleading when substantial heterogeneity is present. No Egger test, rank-correlation test, selection model, or trim-and-fill estimate was supplied. The difference in effect magnitude between the smaller trial (n = 46) and the larger trial (n = 106) may be visually notable, but two observations are insufficient to distinguish small-study effects from clinical heterogeneity, methodological differences, or chance.

The absence of a formal publication-bias result must not be interpreted as evidence that publication bias is absent. Selective non-reporting of quality-of-life outcomes is also plausible because only two effect estimates were available from a pipeline containing nine studies, although the reasons the other seven studies did not contribute remain unknown.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis examined whether EPA, DHA, or combined EPA+DHA supplementation improves end-of-treatment quality of life among adults with depression relative to placebo. Only two trials with 152 participants contributed usable estimates. Under an REML random-effects model, the pooled SMD was 1.0128290868066774 (95% CI −0.517 to 2.543; p = 0.19446571302516819). The confidence interval crossed zero, and the result therefore did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference between omega-3 supplementation and placebo.

The principal feature of the evidence is its inconsistency. The two study estimates were widely separated, producing I² = 93.39% and τ² = 1.139554. Such heterogeneity limits the clinical meaning of the pooled average: the studies may not be estimating a sufficiently similar intervention effect, and the mean of two disparate effects may not represent either study setting well. The absence of information about omega-3 composition, dosage, patient diagnosis, comorbidity, treatment duration, and quality-of-life instruments prevents explanation of this inconsistency.

The analysis also contains a consequential direction-coding conflict. The supplied meta-analysis metadata classify the pooled result as favoring omega-3, as required for reporting the generated result. In contrast, the prespecified convention states that positive values favor placebo. This may reflect an arm-order error, an unrecorded scale reversal, or an inconsistency in the generated `favors` field. Because quality-of-life scales may differ in whether high scores represent better or worse status, verification against the original arm-level means and instrument scoring rules is essential. Until verified, neither the numerical sign nor the supplied favoring label should be used alone to claim benefit or harm.

The findings are therefore best understood as inconclusive. The pooled estimate is imprecise, the statistical test is non-significant, heterogeneity is extreme, and the direction requires validation. Larger, well-reported trials should specify the quality-of-life instrument and scoring direction, report arm-level means and standard deviations, prespecify an overall quality-of-life endpoint, and provide sufficient information to distinguish EPA- and DHA-related effects.

## Data Quality and Interpretation Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` object was supplied, several limitations in the provided dataset require explicit documentation:

1. The pipeline listed nine studies, but only two effect estimates were available; reasons for non-contribution by the other seven studies were not provided.
2. Trial citations, participant characteristics, intervention details, outcome instruments, arm-level summary statistics, and risk-of-bias judgments were unavailable.
3. The positive pooled value conflicts with the prespecified direction convention while the supplied `favors` field classifies the result as favoring omega-3. Original extraction and coding must be audited.
4. Search and screening information was insufficient to reproduce the review or complete a numerical PRISMA flow diagram.
5. The very small evidence base precluded dependable moderator, sensitivity, influence, and publication-bias analyses.

## Limitations

This report is limited by both the evidence base and the supplied reporting dataset. First, only two trials were meta-analysed, yielding low precision and unstable estimates of between-study heterogeneity. Second, substantial clinical and methodological heterogeneity is likely but could not be investigated. Third, the lack of trial-level bibliographic and methodological details prevents risk-of-bias assessment and limits judgments about applicability. Fourth, it was not possible to verify whether one appropriate quality-of-life instrument was selected per trial, whether all scales were oriented correctly, or whether standard errors had been converted to standard deviations where necessary. Fifth, no sensitivity analyses could test alternative estimators, fixed-effect assumptions, influential-study removal, or outcome-selection decisions. Sixth, publication bias and selective outcome reporting remain unassessed. Finally, the conflict between the sign convention and the supplied favoring classification materially limits directional interpretation.

## Conclusion

Two randomised placebo-controlled trials involving 152 participants contributed to this analysis of omega-3 supplementation for end-of-treatment quality of life in adults with depression. The REML pooled SMD was 1.0128290868066774 (95% CI −0.517 to 2.543; p = 0.19446571302516819), with considerable heterogeneity (I² = 93.39%; τ² = 1.139554). The supplied result classifies the pooled estimate as favoring omega-3 supplementation, but this conflicts with the prespecified sign convention and requires source-data verification. Overall, the available evidence does not establish a statistically reliable improvement in quality of life with omega-3 supplementation compared with placebo.
