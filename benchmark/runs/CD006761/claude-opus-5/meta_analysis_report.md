# LigaSure Vessel-Sealing Haemorrhoidectomy versus Conventional Excisional Haemorrhoidectomy for Operating Time in Adults with Symptomatic Haemorrhoids: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Conventional excisional haemorrhoidectomy (Milligan–Morgan or Ferguson, performed with scissors and/or monopolar diathermy) requires sequential pedicle ligation or cautery for haemostasis. The LigaSure bipolar vessel-sealing device permits simultaneous sealing and division of the haemorrhoidal pedicle and has been proposed to shorten operative duration. We quantified the difference in operating time between the two techniques.

**Methods.** A systematic search of controlled studies comparing LigaSure haemorrhoidectomy with conventional excisional haemorrhoidectomy in adults with symptomatic haemorrhoids was conducted; the pre-specified outcome was operating time in minutes. Ten records entered the data-extraction stage; eight yielded computable per-arm effect sizes, and four reported the raw arm-level inputs (mean, SD, n per arm) required for pooling on the native minute scale. The primary analysis was a random-effects meta-analysis of raw mean differences (MD) estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** Across the four studies contributing native-scale data (n = 313 patients), the pooled **Mean Difference (MD)** was **−8.900 minutes** (95% CI **−18.211 to 0.411**; p = 0.0610), a direction that **favours LigaSure (vessel-sealing device) haemorrhoidectomy** but does not reach conventional statistical significance because the confidence interval includes zero. Heterogeneity was extreme: Q = 560.095 (p < 0.0001), **I² = 99.42%**, τ² = 89.108508 (τ ≈ 9.44 minutes). The eight individual standardized effect sizes were uniformly negative (Hedges' g from −0.818 to −9.307), i.e. every study individually favoured LigaSure, but their magnitudes differed by more than an order of magnitude. Egger's regression indicated significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = 1.9409, p = 0.0003) while trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized estimate −4.1403); overall publication-bias risk was rated **high**.

**Conclusions.** LigaSure haemorrhoidectomy is consistently associated with a shorter operation than conventional excisional haemorrhoidectomy in every individual study, and the pooled estimate of roughly nine minutes saved favours LigaSure; however, the pooled mean difference was not statistically significant, rested on only four poolable studies, and was accompanied by near-total between-study heterogeneity (I² = 99.42%) and strong evidence of small-study effects. The magnitude of any time saving should be regarded as highly uncertain, and the certainty of this evidence is low.

# 1. Introduction

Symptomatic grade II–IV haemorrhoidal disease that fails conservative and office-based management is commonly treated by excisional haemorrhoidectomy. The two reference operations — the open Milligan–Morgan and the closed Ferguson haemorrhoidectomy — are classically performed with scissors and/or monopolar diathermy, with haemostasis secured by transfixion ligature of the vascular pedicle. Both steps (dissection and pedicle control) are sequential and contribute to total operative duration, intraoperative blood loss and thermal spread to the anoderm.

The LigaSure system is a bipolar electrothermal vessel-sealing device that applies pressure and a feedback-controlled current to denature collagen and elastin within vessel walls, producing a translucent seal that can be divided in the same manoeuvre. In haemorrhoid surgery this collapses dissection and haemostasis into a single step and removes the need for suture ligation of the pedicle. Shorter operating time is the most frequently claimed advantage and carries direct implications for theatre throughput, anaesthetic exposure and cost — the latter being pivotal, because the single-use LigaSure handpiece carries a substantial consumable cost that can only be offset if theatre time is genuinely and materially reduced.

Although several randomised and quasi-randomised comparisons have been published, they differ in surgical technique in the comparator arm (cold scissors versus diathermy; open versus closed), in the number of piles excised, in operator experience, and critically in **how operating time was defined and timed** (skin incision to completion of excision; anaesthetic start to dressing; or total theatre occupancy). The present systematic review and meta-analysis was undertaken to estimate, on the native minute scale, the mean difference in operating time between LigaSure and conventional excisional haemorrhoidectomy, and to characterise the consistency of that estimate.

**Review question (PICO).**
- **P:** Adults undergoing surgery for symptomatic haemorrhoids.
- **I:** LigaSure (vessel-sealing device) haemorrhoidectomy.
- **C:** Conventional excisional haemorrhoidectomy (scissors, diathermy/electrocautery, Milligan–Morgan or Ferguson technique).
- **O:** Operating time in minutes (duration of the haemorrhoidectomy procedure), reported as mean (SD) per arm or as convertible statistics (median/IQR/range, SE, 95% CI, t or F).
- **Effect direction:** A positive effect (i.e. a *negative* raw mean difference in minutes) indicates that LigaSure results in a **shorter** operating time and therefore favours LigaSure.

# 2. Methods

## 2.1 Protocol and reporting

This review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. The analysis was executed within a structured evidence-synthesis pipeline that performed record retrieval, eligibility screening, outcome classification, arm-level data extraction, effect-size computation and random-effects pooling. No prospective protocol registration number is available for this analysis, which should be regarded as a limitation (Section 11).

## 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Included:** controlled comparative studies (randomised controlled trials and prospective/retrospective comparative cohorts) of adults undergoing excisional haemorrhoidectomy for symptomatic haemorrhoidal disease, allocating patients to LigaSure vessel-sealing haemorrhoidectomy versus a conventional excisional technique, and reporting operating time in minutes with per-arm statistics sufficient to derive an effect size.

**Excluded:** narrative reviews, prior systematic reviews and meta-analyses, editorials and conference abstracts without extractable arm-level data; single-arm case series; comparisons of LigaSure against non-excisional procedures only (e.g. stapled haemorrhoidopexy, Doppler-guided artery ligation) without a conventional excisional arm; duplicate publications and secondary reports of a trial already represented in the dataset (retained once, to avoid double-counting).

## 2.3 Outcome definition

The single pre-specified outcome was **operating time in minutes**, defined as the duration of the haemorrhoidectomy procedure (e.g. from incision or start of excision to completion of the operation). Where studies reported median with interquartile range or range, standard distributional conversions were applied to approximate the mean and standard deviation; where standard errors or 95% confidence intervals were reported, these were converted to standard deviations using the arm sample size.

## 2.4 Effect measures and synthesis

The pre-specified and user-requested effect measure was the **raw Mean Difference (MD)** in minutes (`effect_measure_source: explicit_user_request`). Native MD pooling was successfully applied (`native_pooling_used: true`), meaning the primary pooled estimate is reported on the original minute scale and is **not** a standardized fallback. Native MD pooling requires complete arm-level raw inputs (mean, SD and n in both arms); studies that supplied only a standardized or derived effect size could not enter this analysis (Section 9).

Pooling used a random-effects model with between-study variance estimated by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**. Heterogeneity was summarised by Cochran's Q with its p-value, the I² statistic and τ². For descriptive and small-study-effect purposes, standardized mean differences (Hedges' g, bias-corrected) were additionally computed for every study with computable statistics.

## 2.5 Assessment of small-study effects

Funnel-plot asymmetry was tested by Egger's regression of the standardized effect on its precision, and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure was applied to estimate the number of potentially missing studies (k₀) and a bias-adjusted estimate.

# 5. Effect Sizes

## 5.1 Direction and consistency

All eight computable effect sizes were negative, and in every case the 95% confidence interval excluded zero. On the standardized scale, every individual study therefore favoured LigaSure for operating time, with not a single point estimate or confidence interval crossing into favouring conventional haemorrhoidectomy. The **direction** of effect is thus unanimous across the evidence base.

## 5.2 Magnitude and dispersion

The magnitude of the standardized effects is, however, extraordinarily dispersed. Hedges' g ranged from **−0.818** (study_9_R9) to **−9.307** (study_8_R8) — an eleven-fold difference. Four studies reported standardized effects in excess of g = −4 (study_2_R2, study_3_R3, study_8_R8, study_10_R10), values that are scarcely credible as true population effects and almost certainly reflect implausibly small reported within-arm standard deviations (for example, operating times recorded to the nearest five minutes with minimal variance, or standard errors mislabelled as standard deviations). By contrast, study_6_R6 (g = −0.879) and study_9_R9 (g = −0.818) report moderate, physiologically plausible effects. The distribution of effect sizes is displayed in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`.

This bimodal pattern — a cluster of moderate effects and a cluster of extreme effects — is the proximate source of the heterogeneity reported in Section 7 and should condition the interpretation of every pooled quantity below.

## 6.1 Primary synthesis (native minute scale)

The pooled estimate indicates that LigaSure haemorrhoidectomy was, on average, **8.900 minutes shorter** than conventional excisional haemorrhoidectomy. Per the `favors` field, the result **favours LigaSure (vessel-sealing device) haemorrhoidectomy**. Because the primary outcome is a duration, the favourable direction corresponds to the negative raw sign: a lower value is the better value here, and the sign convention of the pooled estimate (treatment minus control) must not be read naively.

Two caveats must be stated with equal prominence. First, the 95% confidence interval **includes zero** and the p-value of **0.0610** does not meet the conventional α = 0.05 threshold; the pooled time saving is therefore *not* statistically significant despite the unanimity of the individual studies' directions. The interval is compatible with anything from an 18.2-minute saving to a trivial 0.4-minute *increase* in operating time. Second, the estimate derives from only four studies, so the random-effects confidence interval — constructed with an estimated between-study variance on 3 degrees of freedom — is itself unstable.

An approximate 95% **prediction interval**, computed from τ² = 89.108508 and the standard error of the pooled effect (SE ≈ 4.75 minutes) on 2 degrees of freedom, spans roughly **−54 to +37 minutes**. In other words, the available data do not permit confident prediction of even the *direction* of the effect in a new, exchangeable setting. This is the single most informative summary of the state of this evidence base.

## 6.2 Descriptive standardized synthesis

For completeness, a standardized synthesis of all eight computable effect sizes yields a pooled Hedges' g of approximately **−4.14** (the value returned as the trim-and-fill reference estimate, k₀ = 0). This figure is reported here only as a descriptive adjunct for the small-study-effect analysis; it is **not** the review's effect measure, it mixes studies whose reported standard deviations are of doubtful validity, and a standardized effect of this magnitude has no sensible clinical interpretation. The confirmed native-scale result in Section 6.1 is the result of record. The deterministic Effect Measure Summary table appended to this report should be read as authoritative for measure labelling.

# 7. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity was **extreme and statistically unambiguous**:

- **Cochran's Q = 560.095** on 3 degrees of freedom, **p < 0.0001** — the null hypothesis of homogeneity is rejected with overwhelming force (the expected value of Q under homogeneity is 3).
- **I² = 99.42%** — essentially all of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. Values above 75% are conventionally described as "considerable"; 99.42% is at the extreme of the scale.
- **τ² = 89.108508 minutes²**, i.e. **τ ≈ 9.44 minutes**. The estimated standard deviation of true effects across studies (9.44 minutes) slightly *exceeds* the magnitude of the pooled effect itself (8.90 minutes).

A heterogeneity summary visualisation is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

When τ exceeds the pooled effect, the pooled mean is a poor description of the evidence: it is the average of a set of genuinely disparate quantities rather than an estimate of a single common parameter. Plausible sources of this dispersion include:

1. **Inconsistent definition of "operating time"** — procedure-only timing versus total theatre or anaesthetic time. A study timing only the excision will register a much larger *relative* advantage for a device that compresses the dissection–haemostasis sequence.
2. **Heterogeneous comparators** — cold-scissors Milligan–Morgan with transfixion ligatures is markedly slower than diathermy haemorrhoidectomy; the size of the LigaSure advantage is comparator-dependent by construction.
3. **Number of piles excised and disease grade**, which scale the absolute time difference multiplicatively.
4. **Surgeon learning curve and seniority**, including operator familiarity with the device.
5. **Reporting artefacts** — implausibly small reported standard deviations in several studies (Section 5.2) inflate both precision and standardized effect magnitude, mechanically generating heterogeneity.
6. **Design heterogeneity** — randomised versus non-randomised comparative designs within the same pool.

Given I² = 99.42%, the fixed-effect model was neither computed nor considered interpretable, and the random-effects pooled mean must be read as a summary of a distribution, not as a point estimate of a common effect.

# 8. Moderators

No formal moderator analysis (meta-regression or subgroup analysis) was performed, and none could be defended statistically. With **k = 4** studies in the native-scale model, there are insufficient degrees of freedom to fit even a single-covariate meta-regression with any reliability; conventional guidance requires a minimum of roughly ten studies per covariate. Candidate moderators that would be of greatest substantive interest — comparator technique (scissors versus diathermy; Milligan–Morgan versus Ferguson), timing definition, number of piles excised, surgeon experience, and study design — were not available as structured covariates in the extracted dataset and could not be coded consistently across records.

A descriptive bubble chart relating effect magnitude to study size is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`. Inspection of Table 1 suggests no monotonic relationship between sample size and effect magnitude: the largest study (study_2_R2, n = 112) reports g = −4.462 while a study of similar size (study_8_R8, n = 110) reports g = −9.307, and the two smallest studies (n = 34) report among the *most* moderate effects (g = −2.451 and −1.992). The dispersion therefore appears to be driven by reporting and definitional factors rather than by precision alone — a reading consistent with the small-study-effect findings in Section 9. All moderator statements in this section are exploratory and hypothesis-generating only.

# 9. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | **1.9409** |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.0003** |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **0** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (standardized scale) | **−4.1403** |
| Overall publication-bias risk rating | **High** |

Egger's regression test indicates **statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.0003)**, i.e. strong evidence of small-study effects. This is the basis for the overall publication-bias risk rating of **high**.

The Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure, however, imputed **no** missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the bias-adjusted pooled estimate is numerically identical to the unadjusted one on the scale on which it was computed (standardized; −4.1403). This null adjustment **must not be read as reassurance**. It means only that the detected asymmetry does not take the specific form of a gap in one tail of the funnel that this rank-based method is able to detect. The asymmetry may instead reflect the extreme heterogeneity documented in Section 7, the presence of outlying studies with implausibly precise reported variances, or genuine systematic differences between small and large trials — for example in methodological quality, blinding of outcome assessment, or completeness of operative-time recording.

**Practical implication.** The pooled effect is still likely to be inflated relative to the true average time saving and should be reported with caution. More robust sensitivity analyses — PET-PEESE, selection models, or limit meta-analysis — would be appropriate before any confident point estimate is quoted, and the certainty of this body of evidence should be downgraded for publication bias. Note also that with k = 4 in the native model, Egger-type regression is substantially underpowered and its own result is fragile; the convergence of a significant Egger test with I² = 99.42% is best interpreted as a general signal of an unreliable, internally inconsistent evidence base rather than as a precise quantification of selective reporting.

# 10. Discussion

## 10.1 Summary of findings

Across four poolable studies (n = 313), LigaSure haemorrhoidectomy was associated with a mean operating time **8.900 minutes shorter** than conventional excisional haemorrhoidectomy (95% CI −18.211 to 0.411; p = 0.0610). The result **favours LigaSure (vessel-sealing device) haemorrhoidectomy** in direction, and this direction is corroborated by the unanimous negativity of all eight computable standardized effect sizes (g from −0.818 to −9.307, every confidence interval excluding zero). Nevertheless, the pooled mean difference did not reach statistical significance, between-study heterogeneity was near-total (I² = 99.42%, τ ≈ 9.44 minutes), and there was significant evidence of small-study effects (Egger's p = 0.0003).

## 10.2 Reconciling unanimous direction with a non-significant pooled estimate

The apparent paradox — eight of eight studies individually favouring LigaSure, yet a pooled interval that crosses zero — is explained entirely by heterogeneity and by the restriction of the primary model to four studies. In a random-effects model, the confidence interval reflects not only sampling error but also the estimated spread of true effects; when τ² is large (89.11) and k is small (4), the interval widens dramatically. The non-significance should therefore be read as *"the average magnitude of the advantage cannot be pinned down"*, not as *"there is no advantage"*. A sign test on the eight independent effect directions (8/8 favouring LigaSure, p = 0.008 two-sided under the null of equal probability) provides informal support for a real directional effect, albeit without quantifying it.

## 10.3 Clinical and economic interpretation

A saving in the region of 5–10 minutes per haemorrhoidectomy is mechanistically plausible: eliminating transfixion ligature of each pedicle removes several discrete suturing steps. Whether such a saving is *clinically* meaningful depends on context. For a procedure typically lasting 15–35 minutes, nine minutes represents a substantial proportional reduction and could allow additional cases per list; but the lower bound of the confidence interval (0.411 minutes in the direction of harm) is compatible with no practical gain at all. Because the LigaSure handpiece carries a material per-case consumable cost, cost-effectiveness arguments resting on theatre-time savings alone should be regarded as unproven on the strength of this synthesis, particularly as the studies with the most moderate — and most plausible — effect sizes (study_6_R6, g = −0.879; study_9_R9, g = −0.818) would imply savings at the lower end of the range. Decision-makers should additionally weigh outcomes not addressed here (postoperative pain, bleeding, wound healing, return to work, long-term recurrence), which may dominate the overall value case.

## 10.4 Relation to existing literature

The direction of our finding is concordant with the prevailing view in the colorectal surgical literature that vessel-sealing devices shorten haemorrhoidectomy. Our contribution is to show how fragile the *magnitude* of that claim is once effect sizes are examined study-by-study: the literature contains a subset of reports whose variance estimates imply standardized effects of g ≈ −4 to −9, which are not biologically interpretable and which dominate naive standardized pooling. Reviews that pool standardized effects without scrutinising reported dispersion will produce confident-looking but artefactual summaries.

# 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

The following data-handling issues arose during synthesis and are reported plainly because they materially condition the results:

- **Four of eight studies with computable effect sizes were excluded from the primary native-scale pooling for lacking raw inputs.** The pipeline recorded the following conflict: *"4 row(s) excluded from native MD pooling for lacking raw inputs: study_3, study_4, study_7, study_8."* These four studies (study_3_R3, study_4_R4, study_7_R7, study_8_R8; 218 patients in total) did not supply the mean, standard deviation and n per arm required to compute a raw mean difference in minutes, and therefore contribute to the descriptive and publication-bias analyses only. The primary synthesis consequently rests on **4 of the 10 retrieved records**.
- **Two of ten retrieved records yielded no computable effect size at all** for operating time (10 studies entered extraction; 8 effect sizes were produced) and contribute nothing to any quantitative result.
- **Two reporting scales coexist in this report.** The confirmed primary result is on the native minute scale (Mean Difference, `native_pooling_used: true`, k = 4). The publication-bias statistics and the value −4.1403 are computed on the standardized (Hedges' g) scale over all eight effect sizes. These are not interchangeable and should never be quoted as a single number; the appended Effect Measure Summary table is authoritative for labelling.
- **Several studies report implausibly small within-arm standard deviations**, producing standardized effects of g = −4 to −9. These were not excluded (no pre-specified outlier rule existed) but should be treated as suspect, and they are the most likely explanation for both the I² of 99.42% and the significant Egger test.
- **Eligibility filtering** removed narrative reviews, prior meta-analyses and duplicate/secondary reports of the same trial; where more than one publication described the same patient cohort, a single report was retained to prevent double-counting.
- **No prospective protocol registration** and **no formal risk-of-bias instrument** (e.g. RoB 2 or ROBINS-I) output was available in the analytic payload; study-level risk of bias could therefore not be tabulated or incorporated into sensitivity analyses.
- **No GRADE assessment** was executed by the pipeline; the certainty judgement offered in Section 12 is the authors' narrative appraisal, based on imprecision (CI crossing zero), inconsistency (I² = 99.42%) and publication bias (Egger's p = 0.0003).

# 12. Limitations

1. **Very small number of poolable studies (k = 4).** With 3 degrees of freedom for Q and an estimated τ², both the confidence interval and the heterogeneity statistics are imprecise, and no moderator analysis was possible.
2. **Selective availability of raw data.** Restricting the primary model to the four studies with complete arm-level data introduces a form of data-availability selection that is unrelated to study quality and could bias the pooled estimate in either direction; the four excluded studies include both the largest standardized effect (study_8_R8, g = −9.307) and two of the most moderate (study_4_R4, study_7_R7).
3. **Near-total heterogeneity (I² = 99.42%, τ ≈ 9.44 min).** The pooled mean does not describe a common effect, and the approximate 95% prediction interval (≈ −54 to +37 minutes) does not exclude the possibility of longer operating times in some settings.
4. **High risk of publication/small-study bias** (Egger's p = 0.0003), with a null trim-and-fill adjustment that provides no reassurance.
5. **Outcome-definition heterogeneity.** "Operating time" was not harmonised across studies (procedure-only versus theatre-occupancy timing), and this could not be coded or adjusted for.
6. **Unassessed risk of bias and absence of blinding.** Operating time cannot be measured blind to allocation in these trials, and performance bias (including a learning-curve or enthusiasm effect favouring the novel device) cannot be excluded.
7. **Mixed study designs** (randomised and non-randomised comparative studies) were pooled without stratification.
8. **Single-outcome scope.** This review addresses operating time only; no inference about pain, bleeding, healing, continence, recurrence or cost-effectiveness is supported.
9. **Incomplete bibliographic metadata.** Included studies are identified by internal pipeline labels rather than full citations, which limits independent verification and reproducibility of the extraction step.

# 13. Conclusion

In adults undergoing surgery for symptomatic haemorrhoids, LigaSure vessel-sealing haemorrhoidectomy was associated with a shorter operation than conventional excisional haemorrhoidectomy in **every** individual study examined (8/8 effect sizes negative, all confidence intervals excluding zero), and the primary random-effects synthesis on the native minute scale gave a pooled **Mean Difference of −8.900 minutes (95% CI −18.211 to 0.411; p = 0.0610; k = 4; N = 313)**, which **favours LigaSure (vessel-sealing device) haemorrhoidectomy**. However, this pooled estimate was **not statistically significant**, was derived from only four poolable studies, exhibited **near-total between-study heterogeneity (I² = 99.42%; τ² = 89.108508; Q = 560.095, p < 0.0001)**, and was accompanied by **significant evidence of small-study effects (Egger's intercept 1.9409, p = 0.0003; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0)**.

The qualitative conclusion — that vessel sealing shortens haemorrhoidectomy — is directionally well supported. The quantitative conclusion — that it shortens it by approximately nine minutes — is **not** reliable, and the overall certainty of this evidence is **low**, downgraded for imprecision, inconsistency and publication bias. Adequately powered, prospectively registered randomised trials using a **pre-specified, harmonised definition of operating time**, complete per-arm reporting of means and standard deviations, and parallel capture of consumable costs are required before any economic case for routine adoption on theatre-efficiency grounds can be considered established.
