# Immediate Post-Intervention Effects of Cognitive Behavioural Therapy–Based Interventions on Fear of Falling in Community-Dwelling Older People: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Fear of falling (low falls-related self-efficacy) is highly prevalent in community-dwelling older people and predicts activity restriction, functional decline and future falls independently of falls history. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and allied behaviour-change approaches — delivered alone or alongside exercise, Tai Chi or motivational interviewing (e.g. "A Matter of Balance") — directly target the maladaptive appraisals that sustain fear of falling, but trial results have been inconsistent.

**Objective.** To quantify the immediate post-intervention effect of CBT-based interventions versus usual care, no intervention, waiting list, or attention/education/discussion control on fear of falling / falls-related self-efficacy in people aged 60+ living in the community.

**Methods.** Randomised and controlled trials were identified through a structured screening pipeline. Fear of falling was extracted at the first assessment after intervention end (within ~1 month where available) using a pre-specified instrument hierarchy (FES-I/short FES-I → mFES/FES/FES-S/ABC → single-item fear question); confidence-scored instruments were reverse-signed so that lower values always denote less fear, and post-intervention means, SDs and arm-level n were preferred to change scores. Hedges' g was computed per study and pooled with a random-effects (REML) model on the standardized mean difference scale. Heterogeneity was summarised with Q, I² and τ²; small-study effects with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Twelve records entered the quantitative stage and 10 contributed analysable effect sizes (N = 2,083 participants). The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was −0.052 (95% CI −0.263 to 0.159; p = 0.630; k = 10), a result that the analysis designates as favouring the CBT-based intervention arm but that is statistically indistinguishable from no effect. Heterogeneity was substantial (Q = 39.317, df = 9, p < 0.001; I² = 80.17%; τ² = 0.084894), with individual effects spanning g = −0.388 to g = +0.715 and six of ten confidence intervals excluding zero in *opposing* directions. Egger's test indicated funnel asymmetry (intercept = −0.5317, p = 0.0411) while trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted SMD = −0.0518); overall small-study-effect risk was rated high.

**Conclusions.** Pooled across heterogeneous trials, CBT-based interventions did not produce a demonstrable immediate benefit on fear of falling relative to control. The wide prediction interval and directionally conflicting significant trials indicate that the question "does CBT reduce fear of falling?" is not answerable as a single average effect; identifying which components, doses and populations drive benefit should take precedence over further undifferentiated pooling.

## 1. Introduction

Fear of falling — operationalised in most contemporary trials as low falls-related self-efficacy — affects between a fifth and a half of community-dwelling older adults, including many who have never fallen. It is not a benign epiphenomenon of frailty: fear of falling independently predicts activity curtailment, deconditioning, social withdrawal, depressive symptoms, loss of independence and incident falls. Because the construct is, by definition, an appraisal of one's own capability rather than a direct measure of capability, interventions that target cognition and behaviour — rather than physical capacity alone — have strong theoretical appeal.

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and closely allied behaviour-change approaches address fear of falling by restructuring catastrophic beliefs about falling, correcting over-estimates of risk, setting graded activity goals, and reducing avoidance. In practice these components are delivered in several configurations: manualised group CBT programmes (most prominently "A Matter of Balance" and its cultural adaptations), CBT combined with exercise or Tai Chi, and brief behaviour-change or motivational-interviewing packages appended to falls-prevention advice. Comparators in this literature range from true no-treatment and waiting-list control to carefully matched attention, education or discussion control.

Existing syntheses have reached divergent conclusions, in part because they have mixed timepoints, mixed outcome constructs (fear of falling, balance confidence, activity avoidance) and mixed scale polarity. The present review addresses one narrowly specified question: **what is the immediate post-intervention effect of CBT-based interventions on fear of falling / falls-related self-efficacy in community-dwelling people aged 60+, relative to usual care, no intervention, waiting list, or attention/education/discussion control?** By restricting to the first post-intervention assessment and harmonising scale direction before computing effect sizes, we aim to estimate the short-term signal as cleanly as the primary literature allows.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

The review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. Evidence identification, de-duplication, eligibility classification and outcome extraction were executed through a structured, auditable review pipeline; all quantitative synthesis decisions (effect measure, timepoint, instrument hierarchy, scale polarity) were pre-specified before pooling. No separate protocol was registered in a public prospective registry, which is recorded as a limitation (Section 11).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Community-dwelling older people with a mean/target age of 60 years or above. Studies conducted wholly in residential aged care, hospital inpatient or rehabilitation inpatient settings were not eligible.

**Intervention.** CBT-based interventions, defined as (a) CBT delivered alone, or (b) CBT / explicit behaviour-change or motivational components combined with exercise, Tai Chi, or motivational interviewing. Multi-component falls-prevention programmes with an identifiable cognitive-behavioural core (e.g. "A Matter of Balance") were eligible.

**Comparator.** Usual care, no intervention, waiting list, or an attention/education/discussion control. Active-versus-active comparisons without such a control arm were not eligible for this analysis.

**Outcome.** Fear of falling / falls-related self-efficacy at the **first assessment after the intervention ended** — within approximately one month of intervention end where available, and the earliest available follow-up otherwise.

**Design.** Randomised and controlled trials reporting arm-level post-intervention mean, SD and n (or data from which these could be derived).

### 2.3 Outcome extraction and harmonisation

Where a study reported more than one eligible instrument, a fixed preference order was applied:

1. **FES-I**, including the short FES-I;
2. **Modified FES (mFES), original FES, Swedish FES-S, or the Activities-specific Balance Confidence (ABC) scale**;
3. a **single-item fear-of-falling question**.

Confidence-type instruments (FES-S, mFES, ABC), on which *higher* scores denote *greater* confidence, were **reverse-signed** so that, across all studies, a lower value always denotes less fear of falling before the effect size was computed. **Post-intervention scores were used in preference to change scores** throughout, to avoid mixing analysis models across studies.

### 2.4 Effect size computation

For each study, the standardized mean difference between the CBT-based intervention arm and the control arm was computed from post-intervention means, SDs and arm-level n, and corrected for small-sample bias (Hedges' g), with the corresponding standard error and 95% confidence interval.

### 2.5 Synthesis

Effect sizes were pooled using a **random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**. The effect measure for the pooled analysis was the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, specified explicitly a priori; pooling was carried out natively on that scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the pooled statistic is a confirmed SMD rather than a standardized fallback for an unconfirmed measure.

Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and the between-study variance τ², and complemented by an approximate 95% prediction interval. Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. Graphical outputs comprised a forest plot, a cumulative meta-analysis, an effect-size distribution plot, a bubble chart of effect size against study size, and a heterogeneity summary panel.

## 3. Search and Screening

Records retrieved by the search were de-duplicated and screened against the eligibility criteria defined in Section 2.2. Screening proceeded in two stages — title/abstract classification followed by full-text eligibility and outcome-availability assessment — with records excluded at full text where the intervention lacked an identifiable cognitive-behavioural or behaviour-change core, where no eligible control arm was present, where the population was not community-dwelling or aged 60+, or where no extractable fear-of-falling outcome was reported at the first post-intervention assessment.

**Twelve records reached the quantitative stage of the pipeline (`n_studies = 12`), of which 10 yielded an analysable, extractable effect size for the primary outcome (`n_effect_sizes = 10`).** The two records that did not contribute an effect size could not be pooled because the required arm-level post-intervention mean, SD and n for an eligible fear-of-falling instrument were not recoverable from the report; they are therefore described narratively only and are excluded from every quantitative result below. All reported pooled statistics, heterogeneity statistics and bias diagnostics are based on **k = 10 studies and N = 2,083 participants**.

A PRISMA flow diagram was not generated as a separate figure by the pipeline; the counts above (12 records at the quantitative stage → 10 contributing effect sizes) constitute the reportable flow at that stage.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Per-study effect sizes are plotted in the forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) and their spread is shown in the effect-size distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`).

Three features of the effect-size set dominate the synthesis:

1. **Wide dispersion.** Estimates range from **g = −0.388** (study_7) to **g = +0.715** (study_8), a span of 1.10 standard-deviation units — far wider than sampling error alone would generate given these sample sizes.

2. **Directional conflict among the statistically significant studies.** Six of ten confidence intervals exclude zero, but they do not agree on direction: four are on the negative side (study_1, g = −0.386; study_6, g = −0.263; study_7, g = −0.388; study_10, g = −0.342) and two on the positive side (study_3, g = +0.447; study_8, g = +0.715). This is the defining characteristic of the dataset — it is not a case of a consistent effect diluted by imprecise null trials, but of trials that significantly disagree with one another.

3. **Effect size tracks study size.** The two largest positive estimates come from two of the three smallest studies (study_8, n = 42, g = +0.715; study_3, n = 117, g = +0.447), whereas the three largest trials all sit on the negative side (study_6, n = 540, g = −0.263; study_1, n = 378, g = −0.386; study_10, n = 312, g = −0.342). The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) displays this size–effect relationship, which is the empirical basis of the funnel-asymmetry signal reported in Section 8.

Four studies (study_2, study_4, study_5, study_9) were individually inconclusive, with confidence intervals spanning zero and compatible with small benefit or small harm in either direction.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was defined for this review — the primary outcome, fear of falling / falls-related self-efficacy at the first post-intervention assessment. No additional outcome/timepoint groups were returned, so no subdivided pooling was required.

The **pooled Standardized Mean Difference was −0.052 (95% CI −0.263 to 0.159, p = 0.630)**. Because the SMD was the effect measure specified a priori and pooling was performed natively on that scale, this is a confirmed native-scale result and not a standardized fallback for an unconfirmed measure.

**Direction.** The analysis designates the **CBT-based intervention arm** as the favoured arm for this result. That designation should, however, be read strictly alongside the statistics: with p = 0.630 and a confidence interval that straddles zero and extends to roughly ±0.26 in both directions, the data are fully compatible with no difference between CBT-based interventions and control on fear of falling immediately after the intervention. The pooled point estimate is, in magnitude, trivially small (|SMD| ≈ 0.05 — well below conventional thresholds for even a "small" effect), and the sign of the raw pooled number follows the treatment-minus-control convention on the harmonised (lower = less fear) extraction scale rather than any substantive judgement of benefit. **The defensible summary is that no immediate post-intervention benefit of CBT-based interventions on fear of falling was demonstrated in this pooled analysis.**

The approximate 95% prediction interval, ≈ [−0.77, 0.66], is the most informative single quantity in Table 2: it indicates that a new trial of a CBT-based intervention drawn from this literature could plausibly return anything from a moderate effect in one direction to a moderate effect in the other. The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows the running estimate stabilising near the null without converging on a consistent direction as evidence accrues.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity was **substantial and statistically unambiguous**:

- **Cochran's Q = 39.317 on 9 degrees of freedom, p < 0.001** — the hypothesis that all ten studies estimate a common underlying effect is firmly rejected. Q is more than four times its expected value under homogeneity (E[Q] = df = 9).
- **I² = 80.17%** — approximately four-fifths of the observed variability in effect estimates reflects genuine between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. This falls in the "considerable heterogeneity" band of the Cochrane classification.
- **τ² = 0.084894**, i.e. a between-study standard deviation of **τ ≈ 0.291 SMD units**. Relative to a pooled estimate of −0.052, the between-study spread is roughly six times the size of the average effect — the dispersion dwarfs the mean.

The heterogeneity summary panel is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

The practical consequence is important and should not be glossed. When τ is this large relative to the mean, the pooled average is a poor description of any individual study, and the near-null pooled SMD arises substantially through the **cancellation of genuinely opposing effects** (four significantly negative, two significantly positive) rather than through consistent demonstration of equivalence. Reporting "no effect" without this caveat would misrepresent the evidence: the correct reading is that CBT-based interventions produced clearly different results in different trials, and the average of those results is approximately zero.

Plausible sources of this heterogeneity, inferable from the eligibility criteria rather than from measured covariates, include: (i) intervention composition (CBT alone versus CBT plus exercise/Tai Chi versus brief motivational interviewing), which spans very different doses of cognitive restructuring; (ii) comparator stringency, since an attention/education/discussion control can itself reduce fear of falling and will therefore attenuate the contrast relative to a no-treatment or waiting-list control; (iii) instrument heterogeneity across the three tiers of the extraction hierarchy, from the 16-item FES-I to a single-item fear question, which differ in reliability and in the breadth of the construct captured; (iv) baseline fear severity and the presence or absence of a fear-based inclusion criterion, which governs floor/ceiling behaviour and room for improvement; and (v) exact timing of the "immediate" assessment within the permitted window.

## 8. Moderators

**No formal moderator or subgroup analysis was performed, and none can be reported.** The pipeline did not return meta-regression coefficients, subgroup contrasts, or study-level covariate values (intervention type, control type, instrument tier, session dose, mean age, baseline fear severity, or risk-of-bias rating) for the 10 contributing studies. With k = 10 and substantial residual heterogeneity, any such analysis would in any case have been severely underpowered — a widely used rule of thumb requires roughly ten studies per covariate — and would have carried a high risk of spurious findings.

What can be reported is one **descriptive, hypothesis-generating observation**, visible in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`): effect size covaries with study size. The three largest trials (n = 540, 378 and 312) all produced estimates on the negative side of zero (g = −0.263, −0.386 and −0.342 respectively, all with intervals excluding zero), whereas the two largest positive estimates came from the smallest studies (n = 42, g = +0.715; n = 117, g = +0.447). This pattern is consistent with the funnel asymmetry formally tested in Section 9 and could reflect any of: small-study bias, greater intervention fidelity or more intensive delivery in small single-centre trials, selected or more symptomatic samples in small trials, or simple chance given the very wide confidence intervals attached to the smallest studies. It is presented as a pattern to be tested in future work, **not** as an established moderator effect.

The following moderators should be pre-specified in any updated review once a sufficient number of trials accumulates: CBT alone versus CBT + exercise/Tai Chi versus motivational interviewing; control type (no-treatment/waiting list versus attention/education/discussion); instrument tier (FES-I versus other validated scale versus single item); total contact hours; and whether elevated fear of falling was an inclusion criterion.

## 9. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

**Table 3. Small-study-effect diagnostics**

| Diagnostic | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | −0.5317 |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.0411** |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **0** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted SMD | **−0.0518** (identical to unadjusted) |
| Overall small-study-effect risk rating | **High** |

Egger's regression test was **marginally significant (intercept = −0.5317, p = 0.0411)**, indicating detectable funnel-plot asymmetry consistent with small-study effects. The Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure, however, **imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0)**, leaving the adjusted pooled estimate numerically identical to the original (−0.0518). This means the asymmetry detected by Egger's test was not of the form — or in the direction — that trim-and-fill is designed to correct.

Such discordance between the two diagnostics is common, and typically arises when asymmetry is driven by a few influential studies, by substantial between-study heterogeneity, or by true small-study effects (differences in design, delivery intensity or population between small and large trials) rather than by suppression of null findings. Two further cautions apply here: Egger's p = 0.0411 sits very close to the 0.05 threshold, and the test is both underpowered and prone to false positives when the number of studies is small (k = 10) and heterogeneity is high (I² = 80.17%). In this dataset, the asymmetry is also readily explained by the size–effect pattern described in Section 8, in which the largest trials cluster on one side of zero.

**The overall small-study-effect risk was rated high.** That rating reflects not only the borderline asymmetry signal but also the modest number of included studies, the absence of a registered protocol, and the absence of grey-literature searching, each of which limits the ability to rule out selective availability of results. Accordingly, the pooled estimate should be regarded as potentially distorted, and sensitivity analyses — selection models, PET-PEESE, and leave-one-out/influence diagnostics — are warranted before any firm conclusion is drawn. Given that the pooled estimate is already null and trivially small, bias adjustment is unlikely to reveal a hidden benefit; its more plausible consequence is to further widen the uncertainty around an already uninformative average.

## 10. Discussion

Pooling ten controlled trials in 2,083 community-dwelling older adults, **CBT-based interventions showed no demonstrable immediate post-intervention effect on fear of falling relative to usual care, no intervention, waiting list, or attention/education/discussion control** (pooled SMD −0.052, 95% CI −0.263 to 0.159, p = 0.630). The confidence interval excludes effects larger than about a quarter of a standard deviation in either direction, so a large immediate benefit of the kind sometimes claimed for programmes such as "A Matter of Balance" is not supported by these data at this timepoint. The analysis's direction field nominates the CBT-based intervention arm as favoured, but with a p-value of 0.630 that nomination carries no evidential weight; it reflects the sign of a point estimate whose magnitude is negligible.

The more consequential finding is the **heterogeneity** (I² = 80.17%, τ² = 0.084894, Q = 39.317, p < 0.001, prediction interval ≈ −0.77 to 0.66). Six of ten trials returned statistically significant results, but in opposing directions. A near-zero average built from significant effects pointing both ways is scientifically quite different from a near-zero average built from uniformly null trials: the former signals that *something* in the intervention, comparator, measurement or population is systematically changing the sign of the result, and that the field's framing of a single "CBT effect on fear of falling" may be mis-specified. The appropriate research response is not a larger undifferentiated pooled analysis but component-level and individual-participant-data work capable of identifying *which* cognitive-behavioural ingredients, at what dose, help *which* older people.

Several mechanisms plausibly contribute. First, **comparator stringency** matters: an attention/education/discussion control plausibly delivers part of the active ingredient — reassurance, normalisation of fall risk, social contact — and would therefore shrink or reverse the observed contrast relative to a waiting-list control. Second, **measurement heterogeneity** is built into the extraction hierarchy: a single-item fear question and the 16-item FES-I do not measure the same construct with the same reliability, and the required reverse-signing of confidence scales (FES-S, mFES, ABC), though methodologically necessary, assumes that confidence and fear are opposite poles of one dimension — an assumption that is only approximately true. Third, **floor and ceiling effects** are likely where trials recruited without a fear-of-falling inclusion criterion: participants with little baseline fear have little room to improve, attenuating effects toward zero. Fourth, **timing**: the immediate post-intervention window may precede the point at which graded activity exposure translates into revised self-efficacy appraisals, so a null immediate effect does not preclude later benefit — a question this review, which was deliberately restricted to the first post-intervention assessment, cannot address.

Clinically, the results do not justify abandoning cognitive-behavioural approaches to fear of falling, which remain theoretically well grounded and are frequently valued by participants; they do, however, argue against promising older people a reliable short-term reduction in fear of falling from a single CBT-based programme, and against using immediate post-intervention fear-of-falling scores as the sole index of programme success. Commissioners should note that the average effect across this literature is indistinguishable from zero and that individual programme results vary widely.

## 11. Data Quality & Validation Notes

No formal `validation_flags` were returned by the analysis pipeline for this review. Three data-quality matters nonetheless require plain statement:

1. **Two records contributed no effect size.** Twelve records reached the quantitative stage but only 10 produced an analysable effect size. The two non-contributing records are absent from every pooled statistic, heterogeneity measure and bias diagnostic reported here. Their exclusion was driven by unavailability of arm-level post-intervention mean, SD and n for an eligible instrument, not by their findings; nevertheless, the possibility that their omission shifts the pooled estimate cannot be excluded, and no quantitative sensitivity analysis of their absence was possible.

2. **A sign-convention discrepancy in the specification.** The extraction protocol harmonised all instruments so that a *lower* value denotes *less* fear (confidence scales reverse-signed), which implies that a *negative* intervention-minus-control difference corresponds to less fear in the intervention arm; the accompanying effect-direction note, by contrast, states that a *positive* Hedges' g denotes less fear. These two statements are mutually inconsistent. As instructed, the favoured arm has been reported throughout from the analysis's own direction field (**CBT-based intervention**) rather than inferred from the sign of the pooled estimate. Because the pooled result is null (p = 0.630), this ambiguity does not alter the substantive conclusion; it would be material if the pooled effect were significant, and it must be resolved before any directional claim is made from these data.

3. **Study-level metadata were not available at the pipeline output stage.** Contributing studies are identified only by record label (study_1/R1 … study_10/R10). Consequently no study-characteristics table (country, programme, dose, instrument, comparator type), no formal risk-of-bias assessment (e.g. RoB 2 domain judgements), and no GRADE certainty rating could be produced. Readers should treat the certainty of this body of evidence as unrated and, given the heterogeneity and high small-study-effect risk, most likely low.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Small number of studies.** With k = 10, the pooled estimate, τ², Egger's test and any moderator analysis are all imprecisely estimated. τ² in particular is poorly determined at this k, and the prediction interval should be read as indicative rather than exact.
2. **Considerable unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 80.17% with no covariate data available means the dominant feature of this dataset — why trials disagree — remains entirely unexplained.
3. **High risk of small-study effects.** Egger's test was significant (p = 0.0411) while trim-and-fill imputed nothing (k₀ = 0); the overall risk rating was high. Recommended follow-up analyses (selection models, PET-PEESE, leave-one-out) were not performed.
4. **No risk-of-bias or GRADE assessment.** Blinding of participants is impossible in these interventions, and fear of falling is a self-reported outcome, so performance and detection bias are expected to be material across the set — but this could not be quantified or incorporated into sensitivity analyses.
5. **Outcome construct and instrument heterogeneity.** Pooling across FES-I, mFES/FES/FES-S/ABC and single-item measures, with reverse-signing of confidence scales, introduces construct-level noise that the SMD standardisation cannot fully absorb.
6. **Comparator pooling.** Usual care, no intervention, waiting list and attention/education/discussion controls were combined into a single comparator category despite differing substantially in expected activity, which is itself a probable source of heterogeneity.
7. **Timepoint restriction.** Only the first post-intervention assessment was analysed. Medium- and long-term effects, maintenance, and falls or activity outcomes are outside this review's scope and may differ.
8. **Two eligible records unpooled** (see Section 11), and **no grey-literature search or prospective protocol registration**, both of which limit the ability to exclude selective availability of results.
9. **Incomplete study-level reporting** in the available outputs precludes replication of the extraction at study level by readers.

---

## 13. Conclusion

In community-dwelling older people aged 60 and over, CBT-based interventions — delivered alone or combined with exercise, Tai Chi or motivational interviewing — did not produce a demonstrable immediate post-intervention benefit on fear of falling relative to usual care, no intervention, waiting list, or an attention/education/discussion control. The random-effects pooled **Standardized Mean Difference was −0.052 (95% CI −0.263 to 0.159; p = 0.630; k = 10; N = 2,083)**, a trivially small and statistically non-significant estimate that the analysis designates as favouring the CBT-based intervention arm but that is wholly compatible with no difference.

This average conceals considerable and unexplained between-study disagreement (I² = 80.17%; τ² = 0.084894; Q = 39.317, df = 9, p < 0.001; prediction interval ≈ −0.77 to 0.66), with six trials reporting statistically significant effects in *opposing* directions. Funnel-plot asymmetry was detected (Egger's p = 0.0411) although trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0, adjusted SMD = −0.0518), and the overall small-study-effect risk was rated high.

The evidence therefore does not support a general claim that CBT-based programmes reliably reduce fear of falling in the short term, nor does it establish that they are ineffective. It supports a narrower and more useful conclusion: the question as currently posed — a single average effect of "CBT" on "fear of falling" — is not answerable from this literature. Priority should shift to adequately powered trials with harmonised FES-I measurement, clearly specified and separately analysed comparators, enrolment of participants with demonstrably elevated baseline fear, and component-level or individual-participant-data analyses capable of identifying who benefits and from which ingredients.
