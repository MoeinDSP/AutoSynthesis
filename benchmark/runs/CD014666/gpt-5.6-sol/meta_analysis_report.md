# Immediate Effects of CBT and Behaviour-Change Interventions on Fear of Falling in Community-Dwelling Older Adults: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Fear of falling and low falls-related self-efficacy can restrict activity and diminish independence among older adults. This review evaluated the immediate post-intervention effects of cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) or behaviour-change interventions, delivered alone or with exercise or Tai Chi, among community-dwelling adults aged at least 60 years.

### Methods
Randomized trials comparing an eligible intervention with usual care, no intervention, or an attention, discussion, or education control were eligible. The target outcome was fear of falling or falls-related self-efficacy at the first assessment after intervention completion. Outcomes were oriented so that a treatment-minus-control Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) below 0 favored intervention. Eight effects (1,154 analyzed participants) were pooled using a restricted maximum-likelihood random-effects model. The supplied evidence set contained 12 studies overall, but only eight contributed effects to this analysis.

### Results
The pooled SMD was -0.051277 (95% CI -0.241 to 0.139; p=0.5968833299784434; k=8; n=1,154), with the point estimate favoring CBT or behaviour-change intervention but no statistically significant group difference. Heterogeneity was moderate and statistically significant (I²=56.09%; τ²=0.038656; Q=17.107, df=7, p=0.0167). Egger’s intercept was -0.2923 (p=0.3625), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k0=0), leaving the pooled estimate essentially unchanged at -0.0513. Publication-bias risk was classified as low, although only eight effects were available.

### Conclusions
The available randomized evidence did not demonstrate a clear immediate post-intervention effect of CBT or behaviour-change interventions, alone or combined with exercise or Tai Chi, on fear of falling or falls-related self-efficacy. The average estimated effect was close to null, and its confidence interval included both benefit and harm. Moderate between-study heterogeneity and incomplete reporting of study-level methods and characteristics limit certainty and indicate that intervention-specific effects may vary.

## Introduction

Fear of falling is common among older adults and may lead to activity restriction, reduced social participation, physical deconditioning, and loss of independence. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and related behaviour-change strategies seek to modify maladaptive beliefs, improve confidence, and support safer engagement in everyday activity. Such strategies may be delivered alone or embedded within multicomponent programmes that include exercise, Tai Chi, motivational support, or motivational interviewing.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among community-dwelling adults aged 60 years or older, what is the immediate post-intervention effect of CBT or behaviour-change interventions delivered alone or with exercise or Tai Chi, compared with usual care, no intervention, or attention, discussion, or education control, on fear of falling or falls-related self-efficacy in randomized trials?

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

The prespecified eligibility framework was:

- **Population:** community-dwelling adults aged 60 years or older.
- **Intervention:** CBT or another behaviour-change intervention, either alone or combined with exercise or Tai Chi, including multicomponent programmes, motivational support, and exercise plus motivational interviewing.
- **Comparator:** usual care, no intervention, or an attention, discussion, or education control.
- **Design:** randomized trial.
- **Outcome:** fear of falling or falls-related self-efficacy.
- **Time point:** the first assessment after the intervention ended, preferably within approximately one month. The earliest reported later follow-up was eligible only when no earlier post-intervention assessment was available.

The intended outcome hierarchy was FES-I or short FES-I first; modified FES, FES, Swedish FES-S, or ABC second; and a single-item fear question only if none of those scales was available. Activity avoidance, depression, anxiety, quality of life, falls counts, and adverse events were not eligible outcomes for this synthesis. Post-intervention values were preferred to change scores. Confidence scales were to be directionally harmonized so that lower transformed scores represented less fear or greater confidence.

### Search and study-selection information

The supplied results identified **12 studies**, of which **8 contributed an effect size** to the quantitative synthesis. The data package did not provide database names, search dates, complete search strings, numbers of records at each screening stage, exclusion reasons for the four non-contributing studies, or a PRISMA flow diagram. Every eligible intervention arm was intended to be retained, with a shared control sample divided equally across eligible comparisons. However, report-level linkage, multi-arm allocation details, and cluster-design information were not included in the supplied results, so independent verification of these procedures was not possible.

The requested and reported effect measure was the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**. Outcome directions were harmonized such that negative treatment-minus-control values indicated less fear of falling or greater falls-related confidence in the intervention group. This permits instruments with different numerical ranges to be synthesized on a standardized scale.

The supplied metadata notes a measurement-selection conflict: the SMD was explicitly requested even though one or more papers reportedly presented mean differences. The requested SMD was retained as authoritative, and the synthesis is identified as native SMD pooling rather than an unconfirmed fallback measure. Nevertheless, the absence of arm-level means, standard deviations, group-specific sample sizes, and instrument names prevented independent recalculation of the effects or confirmation of all scale transformations.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A restricted maximum-likelihood (**REML**) random-effects model combined eight effects. The **pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was -0.05127706512730196**, with a 95% confidence interval from **-0.241 to 0.139** and **p=0.5968833299784434** (k=8; n=1,154).

According to the supplied direction field, the point estimate favored **CBT or behaviour-change intervention, alone or combined with exercise/Tai Chi**. However, the estimate was very close to the null, was not statistically significant, and had a confidence interval spanning both potentially favorable and unfavorable effects. Thus, the synthesis does not establish an immediate post-intervention advantage over usual care, no intervention, or an attention, discussion, or education control.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **I²=56.09%**, indicating moderate inconsistency in observed effects. The estimated between-study variance was **τ²=0.038656**. Cochran’s test was statistically significant (**Q=17.107**, df=7, **p=0.0167**), suggesting that variation among study effects exceeded that expected from sampling error alone.

This heterogeneity is compatible with meaningful differences across interventions, comparators, instruments, delivery formats, treatment intensity, participant risk profiles, or assessment timing. These potential explanations could not be tested because study-level characteristics were unavailable. ## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression yielded an intercept of **-0.2923** with **p=0.3625**, providing no statistically significant evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k0=0** missing studies, and its adjusted pooled estimate was **-0.0513**, effectively unchanged from the primary analysis. The supplied assessment therefore classified publication-bias risk as **low**.

These results should be interpreted cautiously. With only eight effects, tests for funnel-plot asymmetry have limited power, and a nonsignificant Egger test does not prove the absence of selective publication or selective outcome reporting. Trim-and-fill also depends on assumptions that may not hold when moderate clinical or methodological heterogeneity is present.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across eight randomized comparisons and 1,154 analyzed participants, CBT or behaviour-change interventions delivered alone or with exercise or Tai Chi did not produce a statistically detectable immediate improvement in fear of falling or falls-related self-efficacy. The pooled SMD of -0.05127706512730196 was close to zero. Although its direction favored intervention according to the supplied coding, the 95% confidence interval (-0.241 to 0.139) was compatible with a small benefit, no effect, or a small effect favoring control.

The individual estimates were mixed. Two comparisons showed statistically significant effects favoring intervention, while six were individually inconclusive. Moderate and statistically significant heterogeneity indicates that the average effect may conceal variation across programmes or populations. In particular, psychologically focused interventions and programmes combining cognitive, motivational, balance, strength, and mobility components may not be functionally equivalent. The available aggregate results do not permit those possibilities to be separated.

### Clinical interpretation

On a standardized scale, the estimated average effect was small and imprecise. The evidence therefore does not support a confident claim that these interventions improve immediate post-treatment fear of falling or falls-related confidence relative to the eligible controls. At the same time, the confidence interval does not exclude a small beneficial effect, and the statistically favorable results in two studies suggest that some programme configurations or participant groups could benefit.

Clinical decisions should consequently consider intervention burden, accessibility, adherence, safety, effects on other relevant outcomes, and patient preferences rather than assuming a consistent short-term psychological benefit. Because the review was restricted to the first post-intervention assessment, the analysis does not determine whether effects emerge, persist, or dissipate over longer follow-up.

### Methodological interpretation

Use of a random-effects model was appropriate given the anticipated and observed variation among interventions and outcome measures. REML provides an estimate of between-study variance, but any variance estimate is uncertain with only eight effects. Moreover, statistical standardization addresses differences in scale units but does not eliminate conceptual differences between fear-of-falling concern scales and confidence-based self-efficacy scales.

The absence of detected publication bias is reassuring but weak evidence because the synthesis is small. More importantly, confidence in the pooled result also depends on allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessment where feasible, missing-data handling, adherence, fidelity, and selective reporting. None of these risk-of-bias domains could be assessed from the supplied results.

## Limitations

1. **Incomplete search documentation:** Databases, search dates, search strings, language restrictions, grey-literature methods, duplicate counts, and screening-stage totals were not provided. Search completeness and formal PRISMA flow therefore cannot be independently assessed.
2. **Unexplained study attrition:** The evidence set contained 12 studies, but only eight contributed effects. Reasons for the four non-contributing studies were unavailable.
3. **Limited study characteristics:** Intervention content, comparator details, outcome instruments, assessment times, and participant demographics were not available, restricting assessment of applicability and clinical heterogeneity.
4. **No risk-of-bias judgments:** The internal validity of the included trials could not be evaluated or incorporated into sensitivity analyses.
5. **No arm-level data:** Means, standard deviations, and group-specific analyzed counts were unavailable, preventing independent verification of effect calculations, direction harmonization, shared-control splitting, or scale selection.
6. **Potential unit-of-analysis concerns:** The supplied information did not identify cluster-randomized designs or report intracluster correlations and design effects. It was therefore impossible to verify whether cluster adjustment was required or correctly implemented.
7. **Moderate heterogeneity:** I² was 56.09%, and the available information could not identify its causes. The pooled estimate should not be assumed to apply uniformly to every intervention subtype or population.
8. **Limited power for bias detection:** Egger’s test and trim-and-fill were based on only eight effects and cannot rule out publication bias or selective outcome reporting.
9. **Short-term scope:** Only the immediate or earliest post-intervention outcome was targeted. The findings do not address durability, later follow-up, falls incidence, activity avoidance, quality of life, or adverse events.
10. **Effect-measure conflict:** The supplied metadata records that SMD was requested although one or more source papers explicitly reported mean differences. Although native SMD pooling was used as specified, source-level data would be needed to audit this conversion fully.

## Conclusion

This random-effects meta-analysis of eight randomized comparisons involving 1,154 analyzed participants found no clear immediate post-intervention effect of CBT or behaviour-change interventions, alone or combined with exercise or Tai Chi, on fear of falling or falls-related self-efficacy. The pooled SMD was **-0.05127706512730196** (95% CI **-0.241 to 0.139**; **p=0.5968833299784434**), with the point estimate favoring intervention but substantial uncertainty around a near-null average effect. Heterogeneity was moderate (**I²=56.09%**) and statistically significant, while statistical assessments did not detect publication bias.

Future reports should provide complete trial characteristics, arm-level outcome data, prespecified immediate assessment times, cluster-adjusted estimates where relevant, transparent deduplication of trial reports, and intervention-specific subgroup analyses. Better reporting would help determine whether particular CBT or behaviour-change components benefit selected groups of community-dwelling older adults despite the near-null average effect observed here.
