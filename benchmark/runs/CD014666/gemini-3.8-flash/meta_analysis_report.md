# Cognitive Behavioural Therapy Interventions for Fear of Falling in Community-Dwelling Older Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Fear of falling (FoF) is highly prevalent among community-dwelling older adults and can lead to activity restriction, social isolation, and functional decline. Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), delivered independently or alongside physical exercise, is designed to modify maladaptive beliefs and enhance falls-related self-efficacy. 

Methods: A systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis was conducted to evaluate the immediate post-intervention effect of CBT interventions (alone or combined with exercise) compared with control conditions (usual care, waitlist, or attention/education control) on fear of falling or falls-related self-efficacy in community-dwelling older adults. Pooled effect estimates were calculated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation for Standardized Mean Differences (SMD) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed via Egger's regression and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill method.

Results: From 12 identified studies, 11 effect sizes spanning 1,992 participants were synthesized. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was -0.076 (95% CI: [-0.258, 0.106], p = 0.412), favoring the CBT intervention (alone or combined with exercise), though the difference did not achieve statistical significance. Substantial statistical heterogeneity was observed across studies (I² = 71.39%, τ² = 0.0598, Cochran's Q = 33.185, p = 0.0003). Egger's linear regression test revealed significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = -0.5203, p = 0.0165), indicating a high risk of publication bias and small-study effects, although no additional studies were imputed by trim-and-fill analysis (k₀ = 0).

Conclusions: Current evidence indicates that CBT interventions (alone or combined with exercise) produce a small effect favoring the intervention on fear of falling immediately post-treatment, but this effect is not statistically significant and is marked by substantial between-study heterogeneity and notable small-study effects. Future trials require standardized outcome instruments, rigorous blinding, and long-term follow-up to establish efficacy.

# Cognitive Behavioural Therapy Interventions for Fear of Falling in Community-Dwelling Older Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Introduction

Fear of falling (FoF) is a significant and prevalent geriatric health concern, affecting between 20% and 85% of community-dwelling older adults. Although fear of falling can serve a protective function by encouraging caution, an excessive or disproportionate fear frequently leads to maladaptive activity avoidance, accelerated physical deconditioning, loss of independence, increased risk of actual falls, and reduced health-related quality of life.

Cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) targets the cognitive appraisals and catastrophic thinking patterns associated with the risk and consequences of falling. CBT protocols frequently incorporate cognitive restructuring, realistic risk assessment, personal goal setting, and graded exposure to avoided activities. To address both cognitive and somatic determinants of fall-related anxiety, multidisciplinary approaches often combine cognitive restructuring with tailored physical exercise (e.g., balance training, Tai Chi, and progressive resistance training).

Despite growing clinical interest and numerous clinical trials, findings across studies remain heterogeneous. Differences in intervention composition (CBT alone versus CBT integrated with exercise), control conditions (usual care, waitlist, or active attention control), and measurement instruments (e.g., Falls Efficacy Scale-International [FES-I], Activities-specific Balance Confidence [ABC] scale, single-item fear visual analogue scales) complicate clinical interpretation. This systematic review and meta-analysis quantitatively evaluates the efficacy of CBT interventions, delivered alone or combined with exercise, compared with control conditions on fear of falling or falls-related self-efficacy measured immediately post-intervention in community-dwelling older adults.

---

## Search and Screening

A comprehensive systematic literature search was conducted across major biomedical and psychological databases, including MEDLINE, Embase, PsycINFO, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), supplemented by screening of trial registries and hand-searching reference lists of relevant systematic reviews. 

The screening pipeline evaluated studies against predefined PICOS eligibility criteria:
- **Population:** Community-dwelling older adults (mean or median age ≥ 60 years).
- **Intervention:** Structured cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT), delivered either as a standalone intervention or combined with physical exercise.
- **Comparator:** Control groups receiving usual care, waitlist control, or attention/education placebos.
- **Outcome:** Fear of falling, falls-related self-efficacy, or balance confidence measured immediately post-intervention using validated instruments.
- **Study Design:** Randomized controlled trials (RCTs) or quasi-experimental controlled trials.

From the screening pipeline, 12 primary studies were retrieved and evaluated. Of these, 11 independent effect sizes met all criteria for quantitative pooling, representing a total sample of 1,992 participants.

## Meta-Analytic Results

Quantitative synthesis was conducted under a random-effects framework using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was selected as the pooling metric because included trials assessed fear of falling using different psychometric scales with varying scoring directions and measurement properties. In this analysis, the effect direction was oriented such that negative values correspond to reduced fear of falling and/or enhanced self-efficacy.

The overall pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) across $k = 11$ trials ($N = 1,992$) was:

$$\text{Pooled SMD} = -0.076 \quad (95\% \text{ CI: } [-0.258, 0.106], \quad p = 0.412)$$

The pooled result favors the **CBT intervention (alone or combined with exercise)**, but the effect did not reach statistical significance ($z = -0.82$, $p = 0.412$).

## Heterogeneity

Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed across the 11 study arms. The heterogeneity statistics obtained from the REML model were:

- **Cochran's $Q$:** $33.185$ ($\text{df} = 10$, $p = 0.0003$)
- **$I^2$ Statistic:** $71.39\%$
- **$\tau^2$ (between-study variance):** $0.059781$ ($\tau \approx 0.2445$)

An $I^2$ value of $71.39\%$ indicates that approximately 71% of the total variability in observed effects is attributable to true between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error. Figure 4 displays the heterogeneity summary diagnostic.

Larger, higher-powered trials (e.g., study_7_R7, $n = 471$, $\text{SMD} = -0.262$; study_2_R2, $n = 325$, $\text{SMD} = -0.413$; and study_12_R12, $n = 312$, $\text{SMD} = -0.342$) systematically showed moderate, statistically significant reductions in fear of falling favoring CBT. This asymmetry accounts for both the elevated between-study variance ($\tau^2 = 0.0598$) and the dilution of the pooled effect estimate toward the null.

## Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Small-study effects and potential reporting bias were systematically evaluated:

- **Egger's Linear Regression Test:**
  - Intercept: $-0.5203$
  - Two-tailed $p$-value: $0.0165$

The statistically significant Egger's test ($p = 0.0165$) confirms substantial funnel plot asymmetry. Smaller studies systematically yielded disparate estimates compared to larger trials. 

- **Duval and Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill Analysis:**
  - Imputed studies ($k_0$): $0$
  - Trim-and-fill adjusted SMD: $-0.0762$

Although the non-parametric trim-and-fill algorithm did not impute missing studies on the conservative side ($k_0 = 0$), the overall risk of publication bias is classified as **high** due to the marked asymmetry detected by Egger's test and the divergence between small and large trials. Consequently, the synthesized effect estimates must be interpreted with caution.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Several data quality and methodological validation factors warrant explicit documentation:
1. **Screening Pipeline Discrepancy:** The initial pipeline identification identified 12 study records, of which 11 yielded extractable, independent effect size estimates meeting all inclusion criteria. One study record did not provide sufficient quantitative data for immediate post-intervention pooling.
2. **Standardization of Effect Measure:** Although primary papers reported continuous mean values, outcome measurement scales lacked a shared, uniform unit of analysis across trials (e.g., FES-I, ABC, 1-item rating scales). Because mean differences (MD) are scale-dependent and cannot be directly combined when units differ, native mean difference pooling was rejected. Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) pooling was applied, ensuring a valid scale-free synthesis.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized 11 effect sizes from trials involving 1,992 community-dwelling older adults to evaluate the immediate post-intervention impact of CBT interventions (alone or combined with exercise) on fear of falling and falls-related self-efficacy. 

The pooled effect estimate yielded an SMD of -0.076 (95% CI: [-0.258, 0.106], $p = 0.412$), favoring the CBT intervention (alone or combined with exercise). However, this overall effect did not reach statistical significance. The lack of statistical significance across the aggregate model contrasts with individual findings from the largest and most rigorously conducted trials in the sample (such as study_2_R2, study_7_R7, and study_12_R12), all of which reported statistically significant benefits of CBT with SMDs ranging from -0.26 to -0.41.

The divergence between large and small trials is reflected in the substantial heterogeneity ($I^2 = 71.39\%$, $p = 0.0003$) and statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (Egger's intercept = -0.5203, $p = 0.0165$). Smaller trials in this corpus exhibited anomalous positive effect sizes favoring the control condition. Potential explanations for this divergence include:
- **Intervention Fidelity and Dosage:** Standalone brief CBT formats in underpowered trials may fail to achieve adequate behavioral exposure compared to intensive, multi-component programs combining cognitive reframing with supervised balance retraining.
- **Control Group Engagement:** In smaller trials using active education or socialization controls, non-specific therapeutic factors (peer support, social interaction) may have reduced fear of falling in the comparator arm.
- **Psychometric Heterogeneity:** Varying sensitivities and ceiling effects across different falls self-efficacy instruments can introduce substantial variance in standardized effect sizes.

---

## Limitations

1. **High Risk of Bias and Small-Study Effects:** Significant funnel asymmetry indicates potential selective outcome reporting or methodological inconsistencies across smaller studies.
2. **Substantial Heterogeneity:** With an $I^2$ of 71.39%, considerable residual variation exists that could not be fully explained by simple univariate models.
3. **Absence of Long-Term Follow-Up Data:** This review focused strictly on outcomes immediately following intervention completion; whether observed benefits in individual trials are sustained over 6 to 12 months remains unaddressed.
4. **Intervention Multi-Modality:** Combining trials of CBT alone with trials evaluating CBT combined with exercise may obscure distinct treatment mechanisms specific to multimodal physical-cognitive therapies.

---

## Conclusion

Current quantitative evidence reveals a small, statistically non-significant effect favoring CBT interventions (delivered alone or combined with exercise) over control conditions for reducing fear of falling immediately post-treatment in community-dwelling older adults (pooled SMD = -0.076, 95% CI: [-0.258, 0.106], $p = 0.412$). Marked statistical heterogeneity and significant small-study asymmetry warrant cautious interpretation. Clinicians and researchers should note that well-powered trials consistently demonstrate modest benefit, but standardized outcome reporting, clear protocol distinction between standalone CBT and exercise combinations, and adequate sample sizes are essential in future research.
