# Active Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation Versus Sham for Post-Traumatic Stress Disorder in Adults: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), including conventional rTMS, deep TMS, and theta-burst stimulation, has been evaluated as a treatment for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD), but its immediate post-treatment efficacy relative to sham remains uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of active rTMS versus sham stimulation on change from baseline in validated total PTSD symptom severity at the assessment immediately after treatment.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons enrolled adults with PTSD and compared active rTMS with sham stimulation. The prespecified outcome was change in total PTSD symptom severity on the trial’s primary validated PTSD scale. Because scales could differ, effects were synthesized as the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, with negative values indicating greater symptom reduction with active rTMS. A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were explored using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** The supplied pipeline contained 6 studies, of which 4 contributed effect sizes, comprising 117 participants. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was −0.5751039133895136 (95% CI [−1.086, −0.064]; p=0.02734300639835048), favoring active rTMS. Heterogeneity was moderate (I²=39.75%; τ²=0.107724), although Cochran’s Q was not statistically significant (Q=4.854, df=3, p=0.1828). Egger’s intercept was 1.1416 (p=0.0402), and trim-and-fill estimated 2 potentially missing studies, producing an adjusted estimate of −0.2697. Publication-bias risk was therefore classified as high.

**Conclusions:** Active rTMS was associated with a statistically significant greater immediate reduction in PTSD symptom severity than sham stimulation. However, the evidence base was small, two of the six pipeline studies did not contribute to the quantitative synthesis, heterogeneity was present, and small-study/publication bias may have inflated the estimated benefit. The result should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

## Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a disabling psychiatric condition characterized by intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and hyperarousal. Although trauma-focused psychotherapy and pharmacotherapy are established treatments, incomplete response and treatment intolerance create a need for additional interventions. Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) is a non-invasive neuromodulation technique that can be delivered using conventional coils, deep-TMS coils, or patterned protocols such as theta-burst stimulation.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among adults with PTSD, what is the effect of active rTMS—including conventional rTMS, deep TMS, and theta-burst stimulation—versus sham stimulation on change in validated PTSD symptom severity from baseline to the assessment closest to and immediately after completion of treatment?

The prespecified direction of effect was that a negative value indicates a greater reduction in PTSD symptom severity with active rTMS and therefore favors active treatment, whereas a positive value indicates less improvement or greater worsening with active rTMS and favors sham.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was quantified as follows:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 4.854 with 3 degrees of freedom
- **Q-test p value:** 0.1828
- **I²:** 39.75%
- **τ²:** 0.107724

The I² value suggests moderate inconsistency in observed effects, while the Q test did not reject homogeneity at conventional significance thresholds. With only 4 studies, however, the Q test has limited power, and both I² and τ² are imprecisely estimated. The non-significant Q test should therefore not be interpreted as evidence that the true effects are identical.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **1.1416** with **p=0.0402**. Trim-and-fill estimated **2** potentially missing studies and yielded an adjusted estimate of **−0.2697**, compared with the primary pooled estimate of −0.5751039133895136. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **high**.

These results indicate funnel-plot asymmetry and suggest that the magnitude of benefit may be overstated in the available literature. Nevertheless, publication-bias tests are unreliable with very few studies. With k=4, Egger’s test can have unstable type I error and low power, while trim-and-fill depends on assumptions that may not hold when asymmetry arises from genuine heterogeneity, chance, selective outcome reporting, or methodological differences. Accordingly, the bias analyses are concerning but not definitive. The trim-and-fill estimate should be treated as a sensitivity analysis rather than as a corrected true effect; no confidence interval or significance test for the adjusted estimate was supplied.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This meta-analysis found that active rTMS was associated with a greater immediate reduction in validated PTSD symptom severity than sham. The REML random-effects pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was −0.5751039133895136 (95% CI [−1.086, −0.064]; p=0.02734300639835048), based on 4 comparisons and 117 participants. The supplied direction classification favors active rTMS.

The individual estimates were directionally consistent, with all four favoring active treatment at the point-estimate level. However, three of the four individual confidence intervals included zero. Moderate heterogeneity was present (I²=39.75%; τ²=0.107724), and the most favorable individual estimate came from a study with only 20 participants. These features limit certainty regarding the magnitude and reproducibility of the pooled benefit.

### Clinical interpretation

The pooled estimate is standardized and therefore expresses the effect relative to within-study variability rather than in points on a particular PTSD symptom scale. This facilitates synthesis across different validated instruments but reduces direct clinical interpretability. Without scale-specific mean changes, responder rates, minimal clinically important differences, or functional outcomes, the analysis cannot determine how often the average standardized effect translates into a clinically important benefit for an individual patient.

The finding applies specifically to symptom change measured immediately after the treatment course. It does not establish persistence of benefit, prevention of relapse, improvement at later follow-up, or comparative efficacy among conventional rTMS, deep TMS, and theta-burst stimulation. Safety, tolerability, acceptability, quality of life, depression, and other non-PTSD outcomes were not part of the supplied quantitative result.

### Robustness and certainty

The statistical significance of the primary result is sensitive to the small evidence base and possible reporting bias. Egger’s test was statistically significant, and trim-and-fill reduced the estimate in magnitude to −0.2697 after imputing 2 potentially missing studies. Although these procedures are especially unstable with only 4 studies, both point toward possible inflation of the observed benefit.

The synthesis also included only 4 effect sizes despite a pipeline summary of 6 studies. Because reasons for non-contribution were not supplied, it is unclear whether this discrepancy reflects unavailable change-score data, ineligible outcomes or time points, duplicate reports, or another cause. If availability of analyzable data was related to the observed results, selection bias could affect the pooled estimate.

### Implications for research

Future trials should use rigorous allocation concealment, credible sham stimulation, masking assessments, prespecified PTSD outcomes, and intention-to-treat analyses. Reports should provide arm-level baseline and immediate post-treatment means and standard deviations, directly estimated change scores and their standard deviations, analysed and randomized sample sizes, attrition, and transparent handling of missing data. Protocols should also report sufficient stimulation detail to permit reproducibility and moderator analyses.

Larger multicenter trials are needed to distinguish effects by rTMS modality, cortical target, stimulation parameters, trauma population, comorbid depression, and concomitant treatment. Long-term follow-up and clinically interpretable outcomes should accompany symptom scales. Prospective registration and complete reporting of null as well as favorable findings would reduce selective-reporting and publication-bias concerns.

These issues do not alter the numerical results as supplied, but they constrain reproducibility and confidence in the evidence synthesis.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations:

- Only 4 comparisons with 117 total participants contributed to the pooled result.
- The discrepancy between 6 pipeline studies and 4 analyzed effects was unexplained.
- Search methods and PRISMA screening counts were not supplied, preventing assessment of search completeness.
- Full study characteristics and bibliographic citations were unavailable.
- Study-level risk of bias and certainty-of-evidence judgments were unavailable.
- Arm-level data were absent, preventing independent recalculation of change scores, variance estimates, and standardized effects.
- Moderate heterogeneity was present, but the small number of studies prevented reliable exploration of its causes.
- Egger’s test and trim-and-fill suggested possible publication bias, yet both methods are unstable with k=4.
- The analysis addressed only immediate post-treatment symptom change and cannot establish durability.
- The pooled estimate combined eligible rTMS forms and cannot determine whether conventional rTMS, deep TMS, and theta-burst stimulation have equivalent effects.
- The standardized effect does not directly identify an absolute symptom-score change or a clinically important response rate.
- No prediction interval was provided, limiting inference about the likely effect in a new setting.

## Conclusion

In 4 sham-controlled comparisons involving 117 participants, active rTMS produced a statistically significant greater immediate reduction in PTSD symptom severity than sham. The REML random-effects pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was **−0.5751039133895136** (95% CI **[−1.086, −0.064]**; **p=0.02734300639835048**), favoring active rTMS. Heterogeneity was moderate (**I²=39.75%; τ²=0.107724**), and publication-bias analyses raised substantial concern (**Egger’s intercept=1.1416, p=0.0402; trim-and-fill k₀=2; adjusted estimate=−0.2697**). Active rTMS is therefore promising for short-term PTSD symptom reduction, but the estimated magnitude of benefit is uncertain and requires confirmation in larger, rigorously reported trials with credible sham control and longer follow-up.
