# Active Repetitive Transcranial Magnetic Stimulation versus Sham Stimulation for PTSD Symptom Severity at End of Treatment: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomised Sham-Controlled Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS), including deep TMS (dTMS) and theta-burst stimulation (TBS), has been proposed as a non-invasive neuromodulatory treatment for post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD). Sham-controlled randomised evidence remains limited and inconsistent.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of active rTMS (including dTMS and TBS) compared with sham stimulation on change in PTSD symptom severity from baseline to the assessment immediately after the stimulation course in adults with PTSD.

**Methods.** A systematic search and structured screening pipeline identified randomised sham-controlled trials of active rTMS in adults with PTSD reporting a validated PTSD severity scale (CAPS/CAPS-5, PCL/PCL-5, Davidson Trauma Scale, PSS, IES-R). Six records were retained at full-text stage, of which three provided sufficient end-of-treatment data (mean change, SD of change and n per arm) for quantitative synthesis. Where only baseline and post-treatment means and SDs were reported, mean change was computed as post minus baseline and the SD of change was derived assuming a pre–post correlation of 0.5; standard errors were converted to SDs as SE × √n. All scales were oriented so that higher scores indicate worse PTSD; only the nearest post-course assessment was used, and in multi-arm trials active arms were combined against the single sham arm. Effect sizes were expressed as the standardized mean difference (Hedges' g corrected) and pooled with a random-effects model (REML). Heterogeneity was assessed with Q, I² and τ²; small-study effects with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Three trials contributing 88 randomised participants were pooled. Individual study effects were heterogeneous in direction and magnitude: g = −0.559 (95% CI −1.452 to 0.335; n = 20), g = +0.792 (95% CI −0.167 to 1.752; n = 18) and g = −0.062 (95% CI −0.617 to 0.492; n = 50). The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 0.023 (95% CI −0.636 to 0.683; p = 0.9443), a result that, per the direction field of the analysis, favours sham rTMS, although the estimate is statistically indistinguishable from no difference. Heterogeneity was moderate (I² = 52.48%, τ² = 0.1792, Q = 4.169, df = 2, p = 0.1244). Egger's test showed no funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept = −0.6351, p = 0.7622) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted SMD = 0.0235), yielding a "low" small-study-bias rating. No moderator analyses were feasible with k = 3.

**Conclusions.** In this small body of sham-controlled randomised evidence, active rTMS did not produce a statistically detectable advantage over sham stimulation on PTSD symptom severity measured immediately after the treatment course. The pooled estimate is essentially null with a confidence interval wide enough to be compatible with both a moderate benefit and a moderate harm, and moderate between-study heterogeneity remains unexplained. The findings should be treated as inconclusive rather than as evidence of absence of effect, and adequately powered trials with harmonised outcome reporting are required.

## 1. Introduction

Post-traumatic stress disorder (PTSD) is a chronic and frequently treatment-refractory condition characterised by intrusive re-experiencing, avoidance, negative alterations in cognition and mood, and hyperarousal. Although trauma-focused psychotherapies and serotonergic antidepressants have established efficacy, a substantial minority of patients retain clinically significant symptoms after first-line treatment, motivating interest in neuromodulatory approaches.

Repetitive transcranial magnetic stimulation (rTMS) delivers focal, time-varying magnetic pulses through the scalp to induce electrical currents in cortical tissue. In PTSD, stimulation has most commonly targeted the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex, with variants including high-frequency and low-frequency conventional rTMS, deep TMS (dTMS) using H-coils to reach deeper medial prefrontal structures, and intermittent or continuous theta-burst stimulation (TBS), which compresses the treatment course into much shorter sessions. The mechanistic rationale rests on normalising prefrontal–limbic connectivity and restoring top-down regulation of amygdala-driven fear circuitry.

Despite a growing clinical literature, the randomised sham-controlled evidence base in PTSD remains small, methodologically heterogeneous, and reported on multiple different severity instruments. Previous syntheses have reached divergent conclusions, in part because they have mixed end-of-treatment with longer-term follow-up assessments, mixed open-label with sham-controlled designs, or pooled across heterogeneous outcome definitions. The present meta-analysis addresses a deliberately narrow and pre-specified question: what is the effect of **active rTMS (including dTMS and TBS)** compared with **sham rTMS (sham coil or sham stimulation)** on **change in PTSD symptom severity from baseline to the assessment immediately after the course of stimulation** in adults with PTSD?

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Information sources and eligibility

Records were identified through a systematic literature search and processed through a structured screening and data-extraction pipeline. Eligibility criteria were applied as follows:

- **Population:** adults (≥18 years) with a diagnosis of PTSD.
- **Intervention:** active rTMS of any frequency or coil geometry, explicitly including deep TMS and theta-burst stimulation protocols.
- **Comparator:** sham rTMS delivered via a sham coil, coil-angulation sham, or electronically attenuated sham stimulation.
- **Outcome:** change in PTSD symptom severity from baseline to the assessment immediately following the stimulation course, measured on the trial's primary validated PTSD instrument (CAPS/CAPS-5, PCL/PCL-5, Davidson Trauma Scale, PSS, IES-R, or equivalent).
- **Design:** randomised, sham-controlled parallel-group or multi-arm trials reporting data permitting computation of a mean change, an SD of change and an arm-level sample size.

Studies were excluded if they were non-randomised, lacked a sham arm, reported no extractable severity outcome at the end of the stimulation course, or were secondary/duplicate reports of an already-included trial.

### 2.2 Screening outcome

The screening and extraction pipeline retained **six study records** at the full-text stage as potentially eligible primary reports addressing the review question. Of these, **three trials** supplied the complete set of quantitative inputs (mean change from baseline, SD of change, and n per arm, in the trial's intention-to-treat or declared primary analysis population) required for pooling at the end-of-treatment timepoint, and these three therefore constitute the analytic sample (k = 3; **n_total = 88** participants). The remaining three retained records did not yield a usable end-of-treatment effect size — for example because outcomes were reported only graphically, only as within-group significance tests, or only at a later follow-up assessment — and are described narratively rather than pooled. This gap between the number of studies retained (6) and the number contributing effect sizes (3) is a material limitation and is revisited in Sections 9 and 10.

### 2.3 Outcome harmonisation rules

To make instruments comparable, the following pre-specified conventions were applied uniformly:

1. All scales were **oriented so that a higher score denotes worse PTSD**; instruments scored in the opposite direction were reversed prior to analysis.
2. Where only baseline and post-treatment means and SDs were reported, the **mean change was computed as post minus baseline**, and the **SD of change was derived assuming a pre–post correlation of r = 0.5**.
3. Reported standard errors were converted to standard deviations as **SE × √n**.
4. Only the **end-of-treatment (nearest post-course) assessment** was used; all later follow-up timepoints were ignored.
5. In **multi-arm trials, active arms were combined** into a single active group and compared against the single sham arm, avoiding unit-of-analysis error from double-counting the shared control.

Because the review question specifies a single outcome construct at a single timepoint, the synthesis comprises **one meta-analytic group**; no separate outcome/timepoint strata were required, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data for pooling.

## 3. Included Studies

Three randomised sham-controlled trials contributed to the quantitative synthesis. Arm-level sample sizes were small throughout, ranging from 18 to 50 randomised participants per trial, with a combined analytic sample of 88 participants — a total that is smaller than many single adequately powered psychiatric trials.

Key descriptive features of the included evidence:

- **Interventions** spanned the protocol families specified in the review question (conventional high- or low-frequency rTMS, deep TMS and theta-burst stimulation), and course length, pulse number, stimulation intensity relative to motor threshold and cortical target were not uniform across trials.
- **Sham procedures** comprised sham coils or electronically attenuated/angulated stimulation; the adequacy of blinding (including whether scalp sensation was matched) is a recognised vulnerability of the field.
- **Outcome instruments** differed across trials within the permitted set of validated PTSD severity scales, which is precisely why a standardized rather than a native-scale effect measure was used.
- **Timing** of the pooled assessment was, by design, the first post-course evaluation in every trial, so the estimate reflects immediate rather than durable effect.

No study contributed more than one effect size to the pooled analysis, so dependency among effect sizes was not an issue.

## 4. Effect Sizes

Effect sizes were computed as standardized mean differences with the Hedges' g small-sample correction, oriented so that a **positive value indicates that active rTMS produced a greater reduction in PTSD symptom severity from baseline to immediately after treatment than sham stimulation** (i.e. the sign convention is treatment minus control on the harmonised change metric, with positive favouring rTMS on the raw scale convention).

The three study-level estimates were strikingly inconsistent:

- **study_3_R3:** g = **−0.559** (95% CI −1.452 to 0.335), n = 20. A moderate point estimate in the direction of sham, with a confidence interval spanning from a large sham advantage to a small rTMS advantage.
- **study_4_R4:** g = **+0.792** (95% CI −0.167 to 1.752), n = 18. A large point estimate in the direction of active rTMS, again with an interval crossing the null.
- **study_5_R5:** g = **−0.062** (95% CI −0.617 to 0.492), n = 50. An essentially null estimate; this is the largest and most precise of the three trials and consequently carries the greatest weight.

Notably, **none of the three individual trials produced a statistically significant between-group difference** at the end of treatment: every 95% confidence interval includes zero. The spread of point estimates (from −0.559 to +0.792, a range of roughly 1.35 standard-deviation units) is wide relative to the number of studies and foreshadows the moderate heterogeneity reported below. ## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects meta-analysis was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of the between-study variance component.

**Primary result (k = 3, n = 88):**

- **Pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) = 0.023** (95% CI **−0.636 to 0.683**), **p = 0.9443**.
- Per the direction field of the analysis, this result **favours sham rTMS (sham coil or sham stimulation)**.
- Effect measure: **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, specified by explicit user request, computed and pooled on the SMD scale. **Native pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`), i.e. the reported statistic is a confirmed SMD-scale result rather than a converted or fallback approximation, and no conflict between requested and derived effect measures was detected.

Interpretively, the pooled estimate is as close to zero as a standardized effect can practically be: 0.023 standard-deviation units corresponds to an utterly negligible difference by any conventional benchmark (Cohen's thresholds of 0.2/0.5/0.8 for small/medium/large). The p-value of 0.9443 provides no evidence whatsoever against the null hypothesis of equivalence at the end of treatment.

However, the **width of the confidence interval is the dominant feature of this result**. The interval spans −0.636 to 0.683, meaning the data are statistically compatible with a moderate-to-large advantage for sham, with no difference, and with a moderate-to-large advantage for active rTMS. With only three small trials and non-trivial between-study variance, the analysis is **underpowered to detect even a moderate true effect**. The correct reading is therefore *inconclusive*, not *null*: this synthesis fails to demonstrate a benefit of active rTMS over sham at end of treatment, but it does not establish the absence of one.

The forest plot summarising individual and pooled estimates is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. A cumulative meta-analysis, illustrating how the pooled estimate evolves as studies are added, is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; with k = 3 this plot is primarily descriptive and shows the instability expected when each incremental study can materially shift the pooled value.

Because the review question defines a single outcome construct at a single timepoint, there is **one meta-analytic group only**; no subsidiary groups required separate k, pooled effect, CI or heterogeneity reporting, and no group was marked as having insufficient data.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q, the I² statistic and the REML estimate of τ².

- **Q = 4.169**, df = 2, **p = 0.1244**
- **I² = 52.48%**
- **τ² = 0.1792** (τ ≈ 0.42 standardized units)

By the Q-test alone, heterogeneity is not statistically significant (p = 0.1244). This should not be over-read: Cochran's Q has notoriously low power when k is small, and a non-significant Q with only three studies is uninformative. The I² value of **52.48%** falls in the conventional "moderate" band (roughly 30–60%), indicating that approximately half of the observed variability in effect estimates reflects genuine between-study differences rather than sampling error alone. The τ² estimate of 0.1792 implies a between-study standard deviation of about 0.42 SMD units — of the same order as the clinically meaningful effect sizes one would hope to detect, which is substantively important.

Put concretely: the underlying true effects across trials plausibly range from a modest sham advantage to a modest rTMS advantage. Any prediction interval constructed around this pooled estimate would be substantially wider than the 95% confidence interval and would be dominated by the τ² term; with k = 3 such an interval is estimated so imprecisely that we report τ² directly rather than a spuriously precise prediction interval.

Plausible sources of this heterogeneity, none of which can be tested here, include: differences in **stimulation protocol family** (conventional rTMS versus dTMS versus TBS), **cortical target and laterality**, **stimulation frequency and total pulse dose**, **course duration**, **sham credibility and blinding integrity**, **population characteristics** (combat-related versus civilian trauma, comorbid depression, medication status, chronicity), and **choice of outcome instrument** (clinician-administered CAPS-type interviews versus self-report PCL-type checklists, which differ in responsiveness). The heterogeneity diagnostics are visualised at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 7. Moderators

**No moderator or subgroup analyses were performed, and none could be defensibly performed.** With k = 3 effect sizes, there are insufficient degrees of freedom to fit a meta-regression with even a single covariate; conventional guidance recommends a minimum of roughly 10 studies per covariate, and any such model here would be hopelessly overfitted and uninterpretable.

Covariates that would have been of greatest interest, had the evidence base permitted, include:

- **Stimulation modality** (conventional rTMS vs deep TMS vs theta-burst stimulation);
- **Stimulation frequency** (high-frequency excitatory vs low-frequency inhibitory protocols);
- **Target hemisphere and site**;
- **Total number of sessions / cumulative pulse dose**;
- **Outcome instrument type** (clinician-rated vs self-report);
- **Baseline PTSD severity** and **trauma type**;
- **Risk-of-bias domain ratings**, particularly blinding integrity.

A bubble chart exploring the relationship between study size/precision and effect magnitude is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`; it should be read as purely descriptive. Visual inspection is consistent with the pattern noted earlier — the two smallest trials produced the two most extreme estimates in opposite directions, while the largest trial sits near the null — a configuration typical of small-study noise rather than of a systematic dose–response or moderator relationship. The moderate I² therefore remains **entirely unexplained** by any measured characteristic.

## 8. Publication Bias

Small-study and publication-bias diagnostics were applied with the explicit caveat that they have very limited power at k = 3.

- **Egger's regression test:** intercept = **−0.6351**, **p = 0.7622**. There is no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry; effect size is not systematically related to study precision.
- **Trim-and-fill:** **k₀ = 0** studies imputed. The adjusted pooled estimate is **0.0235**, numerically identical to the unadjusted estimate of 0.0235.
- **Overall risk rating: low.**

Taken together, these diagnostics provide no indication that the pooled estimate is distorted by selective publication or by a systematic small-study effect: the p-value for Egger's test (0.7622) is far above the conventional 0.05 threshold, and the trim-and-fill procedure found no asymmetry requiring correction, leaving the pooled estimate unchanged.

This "low risk" rating must nonetheless be interpreted with substantial caution. With only three studies and moderate heterogeneity (I² = 52.48%), both Egger's regression and trim-and-fill are severely underpowered — a non-significant asymmetry test under these conditions is close to uninformative, and the absence of imputed studies reflects the sparsity of the data as much as any genuine symmetry. A low-risk rating here should therefore be read as "no detectable asymmetry" rather than as positive assurance that the literature is unbiased. It is also worth noting that the field-level bias of greatest concern in rTMS research — selective outcome and timepoint reporting, and the exclusion from this synthesis of three retained records that did not report extractable end-of-treatment data — is not captured by funnel-plot methods at all.

## 9. Discussion

This meta-analysis of three randomised sham-controlled trials (n = 88) found **no statistically detectable difference between active rTMS and sham stimulation** in change in PTSD symptom severity from baseline to the assessment immediately following the stimulation course. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was 0.023 (95% CI −0.636 to 0.683; p = 0.9443), a result that the analysis direction field attributes to **favouring sham rTMS**, although the magnitude is negligible and the interval encompasses the null by a wide margin. No individual trial demonstrated a significant between-group effect.

Several points deserve emphasis.

**First, the result is a non-finding, not a refutation.** A 95% confidence interval extending from −0.636 to +0.683 does not exclude clinically meaningful benefit. With 88 participants in total, this synthesis has power to detect only large effects; the commonly hypothesised moderate benefit of rTMS in PTSD (g ≈ 0.3–0.5) could not be reliably distinguished from zero with this sample. Statements that rTMS "does not work" for PTSD cannot be supported by these data.

**Second, the apparent discordance with more optimistic prior reviews is explicable by design.** This analysis deliberately restricted itself to (i) sham-controlled randomised comparisons only, (ii) the end-of-treatment timepoint only, with all later follow-ups discarded, and (iii) the trial's primary validated severity scale in the intention-to-treat population, with multi-arm active conditions collapsed against the single sham arm. Reviews that incorporate open-label data, pool across follow-up assessments, or select the most favourable outcome per trial will tend to report larger effects. The narrower framing here trades breadth for internal validity.

**Third, heterogeneity is substantively, if not statistically, important.** An I² of 52.48% and τ² of 0.1792 indicate that the three trials are plausibly estimating different true effects, with a between-study SD (≈0.42) comparable in size to the effects of clinical interest. The most parsimonious explanation is protocol diversity — conventional rTMS, deep TMS and theta-burst stimulation differ markedly in coil geometry, depth of field, pulse pattern and session structure, and were nonetheless combined as specified by the review question. It is entirely possible that one of these modalities is efficacious and another is not, and that pooling them produces an average that describes none of them. Resolving this requires more trials, not further analysis of these three.

**Fourth, blinding integrity is a persistent concern in this literature.** Active rTMS produces distinctive scalp sensation and, at higher intensities, facial muscle twitching; sham coils vary considerably in how convincingly they reproduce these cues. Imperfect blinding tends to *inflate* apparent benefit in subjective outcomes, so the essentially null pooled result here is, if anything, conservative with respect to that particular bias direction — which makes it harder to attribute the non-finding to methodological pessimism.

**Fifth, the immediate post-course timepoint may not be the most informative one.** Some neuromodulation effects consolidate or decay over weeks. By pre-specification this analysis ignored all later assessments, so it is silent on durability; a treatment with delayed benefit or with benefit confined to maintenance would not be detected here.

Clinically, these results do not support a confident recommendation of rTMS as an evidence-based monotherapy for PTSD symptom reduction on the basis of immediate post-course sham-controlled data. They also do not justify abandoning a promising modality. The honest summary is that the sham-controlled evidence base at this timepoint is too thin to answer the question.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No formal `validation_flags` were returned by the analysis pipeline for this synthesis. The following data-handling facts nevertheless warrant plain statement, as they bear directly on how the result should be read:

- **Six study records were retained at full-text stage, but only three contributed an effect size** to the pooled analysis (k = 3 of 6). The three non-contributing records lacked the extractable end-of-treatment quantities (mean change, SD of change, n per arm) required by the pre-specified extraction rules. Their exclusion is a data-availability exclusion, not a judgement on their quality, and it means that **half of the identified eligible evidence does not inform the pooled estimate**.
- **SDs of change were partly derived, not observed.** Where trials reported only baseline and post-treatment means and SDs, the SD of change was imputed assuming a pre–post correlation of r = 0.5. This assumption is conventional but unverified; a higher true correlation would make the derived SDs too large (and the study weights too small), and vice versa.
- **Standard errors were converted to SDs as SE × √n**, which assumes the reported SE corresponds to the arm-level mean with the stated denominator.
- **Multi-arm trials had their active arms combined** against the single sham arm to prevent double-counting of shared control participants; this is the correct unit-of-analysis handling but it averages across distinct stimulation protocols within a trial.
- **Scales were reoriented** so that higher scores denote worse PTSD, and only the nearest post-course assessment was used; later follow-up data present in the source reports were discarded by design.
- **Effect measure:** the Standardized Mean Difference was specified by explicit user request and was pooled natively on the SMD scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the reported pooled value is a confirmed SMD-scale result, not a standardized fallback. No conflict between the requested and the computed effect measure was detected. Individual study estimates are reported as Hedges' g (the bias-corrected SMD); the pooled statistic is reported as the Standardized Mean Difference per its measure label.
- **No outcomes were unclassifiable**, and no separate effect-measure strata required independent pooling; the synthesis comprises a single outcome/timepoint group.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base (k = 3, n = 88).** This is the overriding limitation. All inferences — the pooled estimate, the heterogeneity statistics and the bias diagnostics — rest on three studies and are correspondingly fragile. Removal or addition of any single trial would materially change the pooled value.
2. **Severe imprecision.** The 95% CI (−0.636 to 0.683) spans effects of opposite sign and moderate magnitude. The analysis cannot exclude clinically important benefit or harm.
3. **Half of retained eligible records could not be pooled** for want of extractable end-of-treatment data, raising the possibility of selective availability of quantitative outcomes.
4. **Imputed SDs of change** under an assumed pre–post correlation of 0.5 introduce unquantified uncertainty into study weights.
5. **Clinical and technical heterogeneity was pooled by design.** Conventional rTMS, deep TMS and theta-burst stimulation were combined, as were different cortical targets, frequencies, doses and course lengths, and different PTSD instruments. I² = 52.48% is consistent with these protocols not sharing a common true effect.
6. **No moderator analysis was possible**, so none of the heterogeneity could be explained or attributed.
7. **Bias diagnostics are underpowered.** Egger's test (p = 0.7622) and trim-and-fill (k₀ = 0) are uninformative at k = 3; the "low risk" rating should not be over-interpreted.
8. **Formal risk-of-bias assessment across domains (randomisation, allocation concealment, blinding integrity, missing outcome data, selective reporting) and GRADE certainty rating were not incorporated into the quantitative model.** On the available information, certainty in the pooled estimate would plausibly be rated *very low* because of imprecision and inconsistency.
9. **Single timepoint.** Only the immediate post-course assessment was analysed; durability, relapse and maintenance effects are outside the scope of this synthesis.
10. **No analysis of harms, tolerability, dropout or acceptability** was undertaken, so the benefit–risk balance of rTMS in PTSD is not addressed.

---

## 12. Conclusion

Across three randomised sham-controlled trials comprising 88 adults with PTSD, active rTMS (including deep TMS and theta-burst stimulation) did not produce a statistically detectable advantage over sham stimulation in PTSD symptom severity change from baseline to the assessment immediately after the treatment course. The pooled **Standardized Mean Difference was 0.023 (95% CI −0.636 to 0.683; p = 0.9443)** — a negligible magnitude that, per the analysis direction field, **favours sham rTMS** — with moderate, unexplained between-study heterogeneity (**I² = 52.48%, τ² = 0.1792, Q = 4.169, df = 2, p = 0.1244**) and no detectable small-study or publication bias (**Egger's p = 0.7622; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted estimate 0.0235**).

The appropriate conclusion is **inconclusiveness rather than equivalence**. The evidence base is too small and too imprecise to detect even a moderate true benefit, half of the identified eligible trial reports could not be pooled for lack of extractable end-of-treatment data, and clinically distinct stimulation modalities were necessarily combined. Future work should prioritise adequately powered, pre-registered sham-controlled trials with credible sham conditions, protocol-homogeneous comparisons (separating conventional rTMS, dTMS and TBS), harmonised use of a clinician-administered primary instrument such as CAPS-5, and complete reporting of baseline, end-of-treatment and follow-up means with SDs or SEs and arm-level denominators, so that future syntheses are not constrained by data availability in the way this one has been.
