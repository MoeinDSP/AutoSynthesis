# Mobile Phone Reminders to Caregivers and Completion of the Third Dose of DTP-Containing Vaccine in Low- and Middle-Income Countries: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Mobile phone reminders may improve childhood immunisation completion in low- and middle-income countries. This review evaluated reminders delivered to parents or caregivers by SMS, live telephone call, and/or automated voice call, without incentives or other co-interventions, compared with routine immunisation care.

**Methods:** Individually randomised and cluster-randomised controlled trials reporting receipt of the third dose of a DTP-containing vaccine (DTP3/DPT3/pentavalent-3/Penta-3) were eligible. The prespecified effect measure was the crude intention-to-treat Risk Ratio (RR), pooled with a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood model. The supplied dataset identified six studies, contained five study-level effect-size records, and included four studies in native RR pooling.

**Results:** Four studies comprising 2,817 participants contributed to the native random-effects analysis. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 1.1282906775232378 (95% CI 1.006–1.266; p=0.04000507803002951). Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=79.85%; τ²=0.009533; Q=16.808, p=0.0008). The supplied result classified favourability as “neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)”; therefore, no arm-favour statement was inferred independently from the numerical RR. One effect-size record (study_6_R6; n=304) was excluded from native RR pooling because the raw binary inputs required to calculate an RR were unavailable. No usable effect estimate was supplied for the sixth identified study. Egger’s intercept was 0.4986 (p=0.1409), while trim-and-fill estimated two potentially missing studies; publication-bias risk was rated moderate.

**Conclusions:** The pooled RR was slightly above the null and its confidence interval narrowly excluded 1, but substantial heterogeneity, incomplete arm-level data, unclear cluster adjustment, moderate publication-bias concern, and missing risk-of-bias information materially limit confidence in the finding. Better reported trials with complete intention-to-treat event counts and explicit clustering information are needed.

## Search/Screening

The review question was framed around children in low- and middle-income countries whose parents or caregivers received mobile phone reminders—SMS text messages, live telephone calls, and/or automated voice calls—without incentives or other co-interventions. The comparator was routine immunisation care without mobile reminders. Eligible designs were individually randomised and cluster-randomised controlled trials. The target outcome was receipt or completion of the third dose of a DTP-containing vaccine, including DTP3, DPT3, pentavalent-3, or Penta-3, at the principal follow-up assessment for that dose.

The supplied analytic dataset reports six identified studies, five study-level effect-size records, and four studies eligible for native RR pooling. No database names, search dates, complete search strings, language restrictions, deduplication counts, numbers screened at title/abstract and full-text stages, or reasons for full-text exclusion were provided. Consequently, a reproducible search strategy and numerical PRISMA flow diagram cannot be reconstructed from the available results. This reporting gap should not be interpreted as evidence that no exclusions occurred.

Outcome selection followed the stated hierarchy: overall DTP3/Penta-3 receipt or completion was preferred over timeliness or absence of delay, and dose 3 or completion of the three-dose series was preferred when dose-specific results were available. The intended denominator was every participant randomised or enrolled in the eligible arm, consistent with a crude intention-to-treat analysis.

## Included Studies

Six studies were identified by the review pipeline. Five had an effect-size record: `study_1_R1`, `study_2_R2`, `study_3_R3`, `study_4_R4`, and `study_6_R6`. Their respective sample sizes were 600, 321, 1,596, 300, and 304, totalling 3,121 participants across the five records. The four studies entering native RR pooling were `study_1_R1` through `study_4_R4`, with a combined sample size of 2,817. The sixth identified study had no effect-size record in the supplied results.

The available context does not provide authors, publication years, countries, recruitment settings, reminder modalities, scheduling or frequency of reminders, children’s ages, follow-up times, arm-level event counts, or allocation ratios. It also does not identify which studies were cluster randomised or provide numbers of clusters, cluster sizes, intracluster correlation coefficients, or design effects. Therefore, clinical comparability, intervention intensity, and the adequacy of any cluster-variance adjustment cannot be independently assessed.

No formal study-level risk-of-bias assessments were supplied. In particular, information was unavailable for random-sequence generation, allocation concealment, deviations from assigned intervention, missing outcome data, outcome ascertainment, selective reporting, or bias arising from cluster recruitment and analysis.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood included four studies and 2,817 participants. The pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** was **1.1282906775232378** (95% CI **1.006–1.266**; p=**0.04000507803002951**).

The confidence interval narrowly excluded the null value of RR=1. Nevertheless, the supplied `favors` field classified the result as **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)”**. In accordance with that explicit classification, this report does not independently assign the pooled result to the reminder or routine-care arm, despite the event of interest having been described as vaccine receipt.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- I²=**79.85%**;
- τ²=**0.009533**;
- Cochran’s Q=**16.808**;
- Q-test p=**0.0008**.

The Q test indicates that dispersion exceeded that expected from sampling error alone, and I² suggests that a large proportion of observed variability was associated with between-study heterogeneity. The heterogeneity summary figure is located at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

Potential explanations include differences in reminder modality, delivery frequency, baseline immunisation coverage, healthcare access, follow-up duration, outcome ascertainment, and trial design. These explanations remain hypotheses because the study-level characteristics needed to test them were not supplied. The report also lacks a prediction interval; consequently, the likely range of effects in a new setting cannot be quantified.

## Moderators

No valid subgroup analysis or meta-regression results were provided. Accordingly, there is no statistical evidence in the supplied results regarding whether effects differed by SMS versus live or automated voice calls, country, income classification, urban versus rural setting, baseline vaccination coverage, follow-up time, or individual versus cluster randomisation.

A bubble chart was generated at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, but neither the moderator represented nor its regression coefficient, uncertainty interval, test statistic, or p value was supplied. The chart therefore cannot support a quantitative moderator conclusion. With only four studies in the native RR analysis, any meta-regression would in any event be severely underpowered and vulnerable to false-positive or unstable estimates.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression yielded an intercept of **0.4986** with p=**0.1409**. This test did not detect statistically significant small-study asymmetry at conventional thresholds. However, with only four studies in the native RR meta-analysis, Egger’s test has very low power and its result should not be treated as evidence that publication bias is absent.

Trim-and-fill estimated **two** potentially missing studies and reported an adjusted standardized value of **0.2517**. Because this output was not labelled as an RR and appears on a standardized-effect scale, it is not directly comparable with the pooled RR and cannot be used as an adjusted native-scale treatment estimate. Taken together, the supplied analysis rated publication-bias risk as **moderate**. This agrees with the supplied interpretation that the nonsignificant Egger test provides no strong evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry, while the trim-and-fill result warrants caution.

## Discussion

This review addressed whether mobile phone reminders delivered to parents or caregivers, without incentives or other co-interventions, affect completion of the third dose of a DTP-containing vaccine relative to routine immunisation care. Four studies with 2,817 participants contributed to a native random-effects RR analysis. The pooled RR was 1.1282906775232378, with a 95% CI of 1.006–1.266 and p=0.04000507803002951. Thus, the estimate lay close to the null and statistical significance was borderline. The supplied favourability classification was “neither,” and that classification has been retained rather than overridden by an inference from the numerical RR.

Several considerations temper interpretation. First, heterogeneity was substantial (I²=79.85%), indicating that the pooled estimate may not represent a common effect across settings. Second, the lower confidence limit was only 1.006, making the conventional statistical conclusion sensitive to modest analytical or data changes. Third, one available standardized record could not enter native RR pooling because binary inputs were absent, and no effect estimate was supplied for another identified study. Fourth, publication-bias risk was rated moderate. Finally, absent arm-level counts and cluster-design information prevent independent verification of intention-to-treat denominators and standard errors.

From a programmatic perspective, mobile reminders are potentially scalable and relatively low cost, but their impact may depend on phone ownership, network reliability, literacy, language, caregiver consent, message timing, and the accessibility of vaccination services. Reminders cannot address all structural barriers, such as vaccine stock-outs, travel costs, clinic distance, or caregiver inability to attend. Because intervention and setting details were unavailable, this analysis cannot identify where or how reminders may be most useful.

The findings should therefore be understood as a pooled association from a small and heterogeneous evidence base rather than definitive evidence of a uniform effect. Future trials should prospectively register protocols; report randomised totals and vaccinated counts for every arm; distinguish receipt from timeliness; describe reminder content, timing, and delivery success; document losses to follow-up; and, for cluster trials, report cluster counts, cluster sizes, intracluster correlation assumptions, and cluster-adjusted analyses.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Although no separate `validation_flags` object was supplied, the results contain material validation issues:

1. The pipeline identified six studies but supplied only five effect-size records.
2. Only four studies were included in native RR pooling.
3. `study_6_R6` was excluded from native RR pooling because raw binary inputs were unavailable.
4. The five displayed study-level effects were labelled as Hedges’ g and therefore cannot be treated as study-specific RRs.
5. The pooled analysis was labelled as a native RR analysis, but the arm-level events and denominators needed to reproduce it were not supplied.
6. Clustering information was absent, so appropriate variance adjustment for any cluster-randomised trials could not be verified.
7. The trim-and-fill adjusted value was reported on an apparently standardized scale rather than the native RR scale.
8. The supplied favourability classification was “neither,” which is not overridden in this report.

These issues do not justify inventing missing data; instead, they reduce reproducibility and confidence in the quantitative synthesis.

## Limitations

The review has important limitations. The search and screening process was insufficiently documented to assess comprehensiveness or reproduce study identification. Study citations and core characteristics were unavailable, precluding an assessment of geographic representativeness and intervention comparability. Risk-of-bias judgments were not provided. Crude arm-level events and denominators were absent, preventing verification of the intention-to-treat calculations and percentage-to-count conversions. Information necessary to adjust cluster-randomised trials was also absent.

Only four studies contributed to the pooled RR. This small evidence base limits the precision of τ², the reliability of I², the power of publication-bias tests, and the feasibility of moderator analysis. Substantial heterogeneity further limits generalisability of the pooled estimate. No sensitivity analyses were reported using alternative estimators, confidence-interval methods, exclusion of high-risk studies, cluster-adjusted versus crude variances, or leave-one-out analyses. Finally, standardized study-level records and a standardized trim-and-fill result coexist with the native RR analysis, creating scale incompatibilities that require cautious interpretation.

## Conclusion

Across four studies and 2,817 participants, the random-effects pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** for receipt of DTP3/Penta-3 was **1.1282906775232378** (95% CI **1.006–1.266**; p=**0.04000507803002951**). Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=**79.85%**, τ²=**0.009533**, Q=**16.808**, p=**0.0008**), and publication-bias risk was rated moderate. The supplied favourability field classified the result as neither arm, so no contrary arm-favour conclusion is asserted. Given the borderline confidence interval, missing binary data, uncertain handling of clustering, unavailable risk-of-bias assessments, and incomplete search documentation, the result should be considered suggestive but not definitive.
