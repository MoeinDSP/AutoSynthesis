# Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy versus Retrograde Intrarenal Surgery for Renal Stones in Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Treatment Success at Approximately Three Months

## Abstract

**Background.** Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL/SWL) and retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS, flexible ureteroscopy with holmium or thulium laser lithotripsy) are the two principal minimally invasive options for renal calculi that do not require percutaneous access. ESWL is non-invasive and usually anaesthesia-free but frequently requires repeat sessions; RIRS is endoscopic and more invasive but permits direct fragmentation and retrieval. Their comparative efficacy for definitive stone clearance remains contested in guideline development.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of ESWL compared with RIRS on treatment success — stone-free status or freedom from clinically significant residual fragments (per each trial's own definition, e.g. complete clearance or residual fragments ≤4 mm, by CT, ultrasound or plain radiography) — at approximately three months after the full planned treatment course.

**Methods.** A systematic search and screening pipeline identified randomised comparative trials of ESWL versus RIRS in adults with renal stones reporting dichotomous treatment success at or nearest to three months. Counts of successfully treated participants were extracted against the number randomised to each arm (intention-to-treat); percentages were converted to counts using the number analysed, and reported failures or residual-stone rates were converted to successes. In three-arm trials that also included percutaneous nephrolithotomy, only the ESWL and RIRS arms were extracted. Risk ratios (RR) were pooled on the native risk-ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ²; publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Thirteen studies contributing 13 effect sizes and 1,254 randomised participants were pooled. The pooled risk ratio for treatment success with ESWL versus RIRS was **RR = 0.831 (95% CI 0.754 to 0.917; p = 0.00020; k = 13; N = 1,254; REML)**. Native risk-ratio pooling was used, so this is a confirmed native-scale result rather than a standardized fallback. The direction field returned by the analysis records the comparison as favouring **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**, reflecting that favourability for a ratio measure depends on which event was counted; under the review's prespecified direction convention (RR > 1 = higher stone-free rate with ESWL), an RR below 1 corresponds to a lower success rate in the ESWL arm. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate and statistically significant (Q = 25.486, df = 12, p = 0.0127; I² = 53.25%; τ² = 0.013611 on the log-RR scale). Eleven of 13 study-level estimates lay on the RIRS-favouring side, one was exactly null and one favoured ESWL. Egger's test showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept = −0.3261, p = 0.6318) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), indicating low risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** In adults with renal stones, ESWL is associated with an approximately 17% relative reduction in the probability of treatment success at around three months compared with RIRS (pooled RR 0.831, 95% CI 0.754–0.917). The estimate is statistically robust, directionally consistent across trials and not explained by small-study effects, but moderate heterogeneity (I² = 53.25%) and an approximate prediction interval that crosses unity indicate that the magnitude — and in some settings the presence — of the advantage is likely to vary with stone size, location, composition and the number of sessions permitted. Comparative decision-making must also weigh invasiveness, anaesthesia requirements, complications and re-treatment burden, none of which were pooled here.

# 1. Introduction

Urolithiasis affects roughly 7–13% of adults in high-income countries, with recurrence rates approaching 50% within a decade, and the therapeutic landscape for renal (as opposed to ureteric) calculi has been transformed twice in forty years: first by the introduction of extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL/SWL) in the 1980s, and then by the maturation of flexible ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy — retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) — supported by digital scopes, ureteral access sheaths, nitinol baskets and high-power holmium and thulium fibre lasers.

The two modalities embody a genuine trade-off rather than a simple hierarchy. ESWL is wholly non-invasive, is usually performed without general anaesthesia and in many systems as an outpatient procedure, and carries a low rate of serious complications; but fragmentation depends on stone composition, skin-to-stone distance and calyceal anatomy, clearance of fragments is passive and gradual, and multiple sessions plus auxiliary procedures are frequently required. RIRS achieves direct visual fragmentation and active retrieval, typically yielding higher single-session clearance, but requires anaesthesia and instrumentation of the upper urinary tract, often a pre-stent or post-operative stent, and carries risks of ureteric injury, infective complications and stent-related morbidity, along with higher capital and consumable costs.

Major guideline bodies therefore offer conditional, size- and site-stratified recommendations rather than a single preference, and the strength of those recommendations rests on a body of randomised evidence that is individually small and heterogeneous in stone selection, imaging modality and the definition of "success". A quantitative synthesis restricted to a single, clearly specified outcome — treatment success on imaging at approximately three months, after the full planned treatment course — is therefore useful both to estimate the average difference and to characterise how consistent that difference is across trials.

**Research question.** In adults with renal stones, what is the effect of ESWL compared with RIRS on treatment success (stone-free status or freedom from clinically significant residual fragments on imaging) at approximately three months?

**Prespecified direction of effect.** A risk ratio greater than 1 indicates a *higher* treatment success (stone-free) rate with ESWL than with RIRS; a risk ratio below 1 indicates a *lower* success rate with ESWL, i.e. an advantage for RIRS.

# 2. Methods

## 2.1 Design and reporting standard

This review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement for systematic reviews incorporating meta-analysis. The unit of analysis is the randomised comparison between an ESWL arm and a RIRS arm within a trial; the unit of synthesis is one effect size per study.

## 2.2 Eligibility criteria

- **Population.** Adults (≥18 years) with renal (kidney) calculi, including lower-pole and non-lower-pole locations.
- **Intervention.** Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy / shock wave lithotripsy, with any lithotripter and with repeat sessions permitted where the trial protocol allowed them.
- **Comparator.** Retrograde intrarenal surgery — flexible ureteroscopy with laser (holmium or thulium) lithotripsy, with or without basket extraction, access sheath or stenting.
- **Outcome.** Number of participants treated successfully, defined as stone-free or free of clinically significant residual fragments according to the trial's own definition (e.g. complete clearance, or residual fragments ≤4 mm), assessed by computed tomography, ultrasonography or plain radiography of the kidneys, ureters and bladder, at approximately three months.
- **Design.** Randomised comparative trials reporting extractable dichotomous success counts (or percentages convertible to counts) for both arms.

## 2.3 Outcome extraction rules (prespecified)

1. The numerator is the number of participants treated successfully; the denominator is the **number randomised** to that arm (intention-to-treat).
2. Where success was reported only as a percentage, counts were reconstructed using the number analysed in that arm.
3. Where the trial reported failures or residual-stone rates, successes were derived as total minus failures.
4. The outcome reflects the **full planned treatment course** when multiple ESWL or RIRS sessions were permitted by protocol.
5. Where three months was not reported, the **closest timepoint** (e.g. one month or six months) was used and flagged.
6. In three-arm trials that also randomised to percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), only the ESWL and RIRS arms were extracted; the PCNL arm was discarded without redistribution of its participants.
7. Operating time, hospital stay, complications, re-treatment rates, pain, stone-size reduction and quality of life were recorded descriptively where available but were **not pooled**; this synthesis addresses treatment success only.

## 2.4 Effect measure and synthesis

The prespecified effect measure was the **risk ratio (RR)** for treatment success (ESWL ÷ RIRS). Pooling was performed on the **native risk-ratio scale** (`effect_measure = RR`, `computation_scale = RR`, `native_pooling_used = true`, measure source: explicit analytic specification); no standardized fallback was substituted, and no conflict between the requested and the computed measure was recorded. Study-level log risk ratios were combined using a **random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of τ², with 95% confidence intervals on the ratio scale.

For display and cross-outcome comparability, the pipeline additionally reports each study's effect as a standardized mean-difference-type statistic (Hedges' *g*). These per-study *g* values are a **display conversion only**; the pooled inferential statistic reported throughout this paper is the Risk Ratio, and nothing in this report should be read as a Hedges' *g* result.

## 2.5 Heterogeneity, moderators and bias

Heterogeneity was quantified by Cochran's Q with its p-value, the I² statistic and τ². Publication bias and small-study effects were assessed by Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and by the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. Supporting graphics comprise a forest plot, a cumulative (precision-ordered) plot, an effect-distribution plot, a bubble chart of effect magnitude against study size, and a heterogeneity summary panel.

# 3. Search and Screening

The search-and-screening pipeline identified, de-duplicated and assessed candidate records against the eligibility criteria set out in §2.2, retaining only randomised ESWL-versus-RIRS comparisons in adults with renal stones that reported extractable dichotomous treatment-success data at or nearest to three months. Records were excluded at full-text stage for the usual reasons — non-randomised design, wrong comparator (e.g. ESWL versus PCNL only, or RIRS versus mini-PCNL), ureteric rather than renal stones, paediatric populations, absence of an imaging-based success outcome, and narrative or systematic reviews rather than primary trials.

Screening yielded **14 numbered trial records**, of which **13 contributed an analysable effect size** to the synthesis (studies labelled R1–R11, R13 and R14). One intermediate record in the numbering sequence (R12) does not appear among the contributing effect sizes and therefore did not supply poolable treatment-success data; this is noted plainly in §9 rather than imputed. The final analytic set comprises **k = 13 studies and N = 1,254 randomised participants**.

A PRISMA flow diagram is implicit in these counts; the figure assets generated for this synthesis are listed in §4.3 and §7.

## 4.2 Clinical and methodological features

All included trials compared a non-invasive shock-wave arm with an endoscopic laser-lithotripsy arm in adults with renal calculi, and all defined success as stone clearance or absence of clinically significant residual fragments on post-treatment imaging. Within those shared boundaries the trials differ in ways that matter for interpretation:

- **Success definition.** Some trials required complete absence of fragments; others accepted residual fragments ≤4 mm as clinically insignificant. Because the trial's own definition was honoured, the pooled estimate is an average across a spectrum of definitional strictness, and the strictness of the threshold generally penalises ESWL (passive fragment passage) more than RIRS (active retrieval).
- **Imaging modality.** Assessment by non-contrast CT, ultrasound or plain KUB. CT detects small residual fragments that ultrasound and KUB miss, so trials using CT systematically report lower success in both arms — again with a differential effect on the modality that relies on fragment passage.
- **Timepoint.** Three months was the target; where unavailable, the nearest timepoint (one or six months) was substituted and flagged. Earlier assessment disadvantages ESWL because passive clearance continues over weeks to months; later assessment is more favourable.
- **Treatment course.** The outcome reflects the full planned course, including protocol-permitted repeat ESWL sessions and repeat RIRS, so the comparison is of treatment *strategies*, not of single sessions.
- **Stone characteristics.** Stone burden and location (notably lower-pole versus non-lower-pole) are the dominant known modifiers of ESWL efficacy and vary across trials; arm-level stone characteristics were not available in the analytic dataset for formal moderator modelling.
- **Design features.** Three-arm trials contributed only their ESWL and RIRS arms. Blinding of participants and operators is impossible for these interventions; blinding of imaging assessors is feasible but inconsistently reported, and this constitutes the principal domain-level risk-of-bias concern alongside attrition before three-month imaging.

# 5. Effect Sizes

Thirteen studies supplied **13 effect sizes** — one per study, so no within-study dependency correction (multilevel modelling or robust variance estimation) was required and each trial enters the model once.

The distribution of study-level estimates is strikingly one-sided in direction, if not in magnitude (see `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`):

- **11 of 13 (84.6%)** estimates lie on the RIRS-favouring side of the null;
- **1 of 13** (study 10, N = 100) is exactly null (*g* = 0.000);
- **1 of 13** (study 5, N = 30 — the second-smallest trial) favours ESWL (*g* = +0.260), with a confidence interval spanning the null;
- **6 of 13** have confidence intervals excluding the null, all of them in the RIRS-favouring direction (studies 3, 6, 7, 11, 13, and study 8 at the boundary with an upper limit of 0.002).

Magnitude on the standardized display scale ranges from 0.000 to −1.686 (study 7, N = 60), the latter an outlier more than twice the pooled magnitude. Precision varies approximately eighteen-fold in sample size across the set, from N = 22 (study 9) to N = 401 (study 13); the two largest trials (studies 13 and 4) both point in the RIRS-favouring direction, though with notably different magnitudes (−0.573 and −0.187), which is itself an early signal of real between-study dispersion rather than mere sampling noise.

The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) shows that the most extreme estimates cluster among the smaller trials — the classic funnel shape expected from sampling variability alone — while the larger trials converge on a moderate RIRS-favouring difference.

**Interpretation.** The pooled **risk ratio for treatment success with ESWL relative to RIRS is 0.831 (95% CI 0.754–0.917, p = 0.00020)**. Because pooling was performed natively on the risk-ratio scale, this is a confirmed native-scale risk ratio and not a standardized surrogate.

The analysis's own direction field classifies the comparison as favouring **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"** — a caution that, for a ratio statistic, which arm is "favoured" depends on whether the event counted is a good outcome (success) or a bad one (failure/residual stone). Applying the review's prespecified direction convention (RR > 1 = higher stone-free rate with ESWL; RR < 1 = lower success with ESWL, favouring RIRS), an RR of 0.831 corresponds to ESWL achieving about **83 successes for every 100 achieved by RIRS**, i.e. an approximately **17% relative reduction** in the probability of treatment success at three months, with the confidence interval excluding no difference (upper bound 0.917).

On an absolute scale, the clinical translation depends on the baseline (RIRS) success rate. Taking plausible RIRS stone-free rates of 80–90% at three months, an RR of 0.831 implies ESWL success of roughly 66–75%, i.e. an absolute difference of about 13–15 percentage points, corresponding to a number needed to treat with RIRS rather than ESWL of approximately **7 to 8** to achieve one additional successfully treated patient. Under the lower bound of the confidence interval (RR = 0.754) the absolute gap widens to ~20–22 points; under the upper bound (RR = 0.917) it narrows to ~7 points. These derived absolute figures are illustrative, since baseline rates were not pooled.

The cumulative plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows the running estimate stabilising as trials are added in order of precision, with no late reversal of direction — the conclusion is not driven by the most recently added or the least precise studies.

# 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **moderate and statistically significant**:

- **Cochran's Q = 25.486 on 12 degrees of freedom, p = 0.0127** — the null hypothesis of a common underlying effect is rejected at conventional thresholds.
- **I² = 53.25%** — just over half of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to real between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. This falls in the conventional "moderate" band (30–60%) and sits close to the boundary with "substantial".
- **τ² = 0.013611** on the log-risk-ratio scale, i.e. a between-study standard deviation of τ ≈ 0.117 in log-RR units, corresponding to roughly a ±12% multiplicative spread in the true risk ratio across settings.

**Prediction interval.** Combining τ² with the standard error of the pooled log-RR (SE ≈ 0.050, implied by the reported confidence interval) gives an approximate 95% prediction interval for the true risk ratio in a new, exchangeable setting of roughly **0.63 to 1.10**. The practical reading is important and should not be glossed: while the *average* effect clearly favours RIRS, the predicted range for an individual future trial includes values close to and slightly above unity. In some clinical configurations — small, non-lower-pole, radio-opaque, favourable-density stones with short skin-to-stone distance, generous repeat-session protocols and ultrasound/KUB-based follow-up — ESWL may plausibly match RIRS; in others (large, lower-pole or dense stones assessed by CT) the deficit may be considerably larger than 17% relative.

**Sources of heterogeneity.** The dispersion is readily explicable on clinical grounds without invoking methodological failure. The candidate sources, in descending order of plausibility, are: (i) stone size and burden; (ii) stone location, especially lower-pole calyceal stones where fragment clearance after ESWL is anatomically impeded; (iii) stone composition and attenuation (cystine, brushite and calcium oxalate monohydrate resist shock waves); (iv) the strictness of the success definition (complete clearance versus ≤4 mm residual fragments); (v) imaging modality (CT versus ultrasound/KUB sensitivity to small fragments); (vi) the exact follow-up timepoint, since trials contributing one-month rather than three-month data systematically understate ESWL clearance; and (vii) the number of ESWL sessions permitted by protocol. The single most extreme estimate (study 7, *g* = −1.686, N = 60) is a candidate influential case; its exclusion would be expected to reduce both I² and the pooled magnitude, though the direction and statistical significance of the pooled result rest on 11 of 13 concordant estimates including the two largest trials and would not plausibly be overturned by removing any single study.

# 8. Moderator Analyses

**No formal subgroup analyses or meta-regressions were estimated in this synthesis.** The analytic dataset provides, for each study, only a total randomised sample size and an effect estimate; it does not carry the study-level covariates (mean stone diameter, proportion of lower-pole stones, stone density, imaging modality, success threshold, exact follow-up timepoint, number of permitted sessions, publication year, single- versus multi-centre status) that would be required to model the sources of heterogeneity identified in §7. Fabricating moderator coefficients from unavailable covariates would be inappropriate, and none are reported.

What can be said descriptively:

- **Study size.** The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) plots effect magnitude against study size. Larger trials cluster at moderate RIRS-favouring values (study 13, N = 401, *g* = −0.573; study 4, N = 180, *g* = −0.187), while the extremes — both the most negative (study 7, *g* = −1.686, N = 60) and the only positive estimate (study 5, *g* = +0.260, N = 30) — come from small trials. This is the pattern expected under sampling variability, and it is corroborated by the formally null Egger's test (§9), so size is unlikely to be a substantive moderator as opposed to a precision artefact.
- **Direction consistency.** With 11/13 estimates RIRS-favouring, one null and one ESWL-favouring, there is no visible clustering into opposing subgroups; the heterogeneity is in *magnitude*, not in *sign*. This is reassuring for the robustness of the qualitative conclusion even while the quantitative estimate remains setting-dependent.

Even had covariates been available, k = 13 would support at most one or two moderators (the usual rule of thumb being ≥10 studies per covariate), and any such analysis would be exploratory and observational across studies, subject to aggregation bias.

# 9. Publication Bias

Two complementary assessments were performed:

| Test | Statistic | Result |
|---|---|---|
| Egger's regression (funnel asymmetry) | intercept = **−0.3261**, **p = 0.6318** | No statistically significant asymmetry |
| Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill | **k₀ = 0** studies imputed; adjusted estimate (display *g* scale) = **−0.4984** | No adjustment required |
| Overall risk rating | — | **Low** |

Egger's regression test shows no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.6318), indicating no detectable small-study effects, and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed **no** missing studies (k₀ = 0), implying that the observed pooled estimate requires no adjustment for suspected unpublished results. The trim-and-fill adjusted value of −0.4984 is reported on the standardized display scale and, with k₀ = 0, is simply the unadjusted estimate on that scale; it is **not** a risk ratio and should not be read as one. Together these converge on a **low** risk of publication bias for this meta-analysis.

One caveat must be stated explicitly: these tests have limited statistical power, particularly when fewer than approximately ten studies are pooled or when between-study heterogeneity is high. With k = 13 and I² = 53.25%, power is modest, and a null result should be read as **"no evidence of bias"** rather than as positive proof that none exists. Two residual mechanisms are also invisible to funnel-based diagnostics: outcome-definition flexibility (the choice between complete clearance and a ≤4 mm threshold, or between CT and ultrasound assessment, can be made post hoc and shifts results in predictable directions), and the absence of trial-registry cross-checking in this pipeline, which leaves selective non-reporting of the primary timepoint undetectable.

# 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

No structured `validation_flags` were supplied with this analysis. The following observations derive from the analytic record itself and are stated plainly:

- **One screened record contributed no effect size.** The contributing study labels run R1–R11, R13 and R14 — 13 effect sizes across a numbering sequence that reaches 14. The intermediate record **R12 is absent from the pooled set** and therefore supplied no analysable treatment-success data (for example because the required counts or convertible percentages were unavailable, or because it was excluded at a late stage as a review, duplicate publication or companion report of an already-included trial). It is excluded, not imputed.
- **One effect size per study; no double-counting correction needed.** 13 studies yielded 13 effect sizes, so no trial contributes twice and no multiple-report clustering adjustment was applied.
- **Arm-level denominators are not visible in the analytic dataset.** Only study totals (N = 22 to 401, total 1,254) are carried forward. Per-arm success counts, baseline stone-free rates and the allocation ratio cannot be audited from the supplied data, which limits absolute-risk reporting (§6) to illustrative scenarios.
- **Mixed reporting conventions were harmonised during extraction.** Percentages were converted to counts using the number analysed, and failure/residual-stone rates were converted to successes. Both conversions introduce rounding error of up to one participant per arm; neither is systematically directional.
- **Nearest-timepoint substitution was permitted.** Trials reporting only one- or six-month imaging were included at their closest timepoint. Which specific trials required substitution is not recoverable from the supplied dataset, so the influence of timepoint cannot be quantified — a relevant omission given that ESWL clearance accrues over time.
- **Two reporting scales coexist in the record.** Per-study estimates are presented as standardized Hedges' *g* (display conversion) while the pooled statistic is a native Risk Ratio. These are not interchangeable; the trim-and-fill output (−0.4984) is on the *g* scale and the pooled result (0.831) on the RR scale. Readers comparing the two should not treat them as the same quantity.
- **Non-pooled outcomes.** Complications, re-treatment, auxiliary procedures, operating time, anaesthesia requirement, hospital stay, pain and quality of life were deliberately not synthesised. The review therefore quantifies efficacy only, and cannot support a net benefit–harm conclusion.

# 11. Discussion

## 11.1 Principal findings

Across 13 randomised comparisons and 1,254 adults with renal stones, ESWL produced fewer treatment successes than RIRS at approximately three months: **pooled RR 0.831 (95% CI 0.754–0.917, p = 0.00020)**, a roughly 17% relative shortfall in the probability of being stone-free or free of clinically significant residual fragments. The result is a confirmed native-scale risk ratio. The direction field supplied with the analysis declines to nominate a favoured arm — recording "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)" — because favourability for a ratio statistic is contingent on which event is counted; under the review's prespecified convention that RR > 1 means higher stone-free rates with ESWL, the observed RR of 0.831 corresponds to an advantage for RIRS on this outcome.

Three features strengthen confidence in the qualitative conclusion. First, **directional consistency**: 11 of 13 trials, including the two largest, point the same way, with the only ESWL-favouring estimate coming from the second-smallest trial and spanning the null. Second, **stability on cumulative synthesis**: the precision-ordered cumulative plot shows no reversal as evidence accrues. Third, **absence of small-study effects**: Egger's p = 0.6318 and trim-and-fill k₀ = 0 give no indication that the result is an artefact of selective publication.

## 11.2 Relation to existing knowledge and mechanism

The finding aligns with the prevailing clinical understanding and with guideline-level expectations. RIRS fragments stones under direct vision and permits active retrieval of fragments, whereas ESWL depends on fragmentation at a distance followed by *passive* passage of debris — a process that is incomplete in anatomically unfavourable calyces, particularly the lower pole, and that is further handicapped by high stone attenuation, large skin-to-stone distance and resistant compositions such as cystine, brushite and calcium oxalate monohydrate. The measurement environment compounds the biology: contemporary trials increasingly assess outcome by non-contrast CT, which detects sub-4 mm residual fragments that ultrasound and plain radiography miss, and such fragments are precisely the residue that ESWL characteristically leaves behind. Part of the pooled 17% relative difference is therefore attributable to detection sensitivity interacting with mechanism, not to fragmentation failure alone.

Importantly, the outcome synthesised here reflects the **full planned treatment course**, including protocol-permitted repeat ESWL sessions. The ESWL deficit is thus not merely a single-session artefact that additional sessions erase; it persists at the level of the treatment strategy. That said, strategies differ in what they cost the patient to deliver: achieving the ESWL figures in these trials frequently required two or three sessions, whereas the RIRS figures were typically achieved in one or occasionally two anaesthetised procedures.

## 11.3 Clinical implications

For a patient whose overriding priority is the highest probability of being stone-free at three months, these data support RIRS, with an estimated number needed to treat of approximately 7–8 (assuming RIRS success of 80–90%) to gain one additional successful outcome. But treatment success is not the only outcome that matters, and this review deliberately did not pool the counterweights: ESWL requires no anaesthesia and no instrumentation, has a low rate of serious complications, imposes no stent-related morbidity, and is typically cheaper and more accessible; RIRS entails anaesthesia, ureteric instrumentation, frequent stenting with its attendant symptoms, infective risk, and higher consumable and capital costs. A complete comparison would also weigh ESWL's higher re-treatment and auxiliary-procedure burden against RIRS's per-procedure invasiveness. The prediction interval (~0.63–1.10) reinforces that the choice should remain stratified: in favourable stone configurations the efficacy gap may be small enough that ESWL's lower invasiveness dominates, while in lower-pole, large or dense stones the gap is likely to be wide enough that RIRS is clearly preferable.

## 11.4 Certainty of the evidence

Applying GRADE-style reasoning informally: the body of evidence begins as high certainty (randomised trials) and is plausibly downgraded one level for **inconsistency** (I² = 53.25%, Q p = 0.0127, prediction interval crossing 1) and arguably one level for **risk of bias** (impossibility of participant/operator blinding, inconsistent reporting of blinded imaging assessment, heterogeneous and partly investigator-defined success thresholds, nearest-timepoint substitution). Precision is adequate (narrow confidence interval excluding the null) and publication bias is low. The resulting certainty is best described as **moderate to low** for the magnitude of the effect, while the *direction* — RIRS yielding higher three-month success than ESWL — rests on more secure ground.

# 12. Limitations

1. **Moderate, statistically significant heterogeneity.** I² = 53.25% and Q p = 0.0127 mean the pooled risk ratio describes an average across clinically dissimilar settings; the approximate prediction interval (0.63–1.10) includes unity, so the pooled estimate should not be applied uncritically to any individual patient or stone configuration.
2. **No moderator analysis was possible.** Stone size, stone location (notably lower pole), composition and density, imaging modality, success threshold, exact timepoint and number of permitted sessions — the very variables most likely to explain the observed dispersion — were not available as study-level covariates. The heterogeneity is therefore characterised but unexplained.
3. **Heterogeneous outcome definitions were pooled as reported.** Combining trials that required complete clearance with trials accepting ≤4 mm residual fragments, and CT-assessed with ultrasound- or KUB-assessed outcomes, introduces definitional noise that is probably not direction-neutral: stricter definitions and more sensitive imaging disadvantage ESWL.
4. **Timepoint substitution.** Where three-month data were unavailable, the nearest timepoint (one or six months) was used. Because passive fragment clearance after ESWL continues for weeks to months, trials contributing one-month data plausibly understate ESWL success, and the proportion of such trials cannot be recovered from the supplied dataset.
5. **Small trials dominate the count.** Median N was 60 and the smallest trial randomised 22 participants; much of the information is concentrated in two trials (N = 401 and N = 180). Small-trial estimates are volatile, as the range of study-level effects (0.000 to −1.686 on the display scale) illustrates.
6. **No formal risk-of-bias assessment is reported here.** Domain-level RoB 2 judgements (randomisation, deviations, missing data, measurement, selective reporting) were not supplied with the analytic data, so sensitivity analysis restricted to low-risk trials could not be performed. Allocation concealment and blinded outcome assessment are the critical unverified domains.
7. **Intention-to-treat denominators with reconstructed numerators.** Using the number randomised as the denominator is conservative and appropriate, but percentage-to-count and failure-to-success conversions introduce rounding error, and crossovers (ESWL failures salvaged by ureteroscopy) may have been handled inconsistently across trials — a mechanism that would bias the comparison toward the null or toward RIRS depending on the analytic convention used.
8. **Single outcome domain.** Complications, re-treatment and auxiliary procedures, operating and anaesthesia time, hospital stay, pain, stone-size reduction and quality of life were not pooled by design. This review therefore cannot support any statement about the overall net clinical benefit of one modality over the other.
9. **One screened record (R12) contributed no data**, and the reasons for its absence cannot be verified from the supplied dataset (§10).
10. **Bias diagnostics are underpowered.** With k = 13 and moderate heterogeneity, Egger's test and trim-and-fill have limited sensitivity; low *detected* bias risk is not the same as demonstrated absence of bias. Language restrictions, registry non-checking and outcome-definition flexibility remain unaddressed.
11. **Generalisability.** The pooled estimate reflects the stone sizes, locations, lithotripter generations, laser platforms and operator experience represented in the included trials. Rapid technological change — high-power holmium and thulium fibre lasers, single-use digital scopes, modern lithotripters with improved coupling and localisation — means the contemporary comparison may differ from the historical average synthesised here.

---

# 13. Conclusion

In adults with renal stones, extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy achieves fewer treatment successes than retrograde intrarenal surgery at approximately three months. Pooling 13 randomised comparisons and 1,254 participants on the native risk-ratio scale with a REML random-effects model gave a **Risk Ratio of 0.831 (95% CI 0.754 to 0.917; p = 0.00020)** — approximately a 17% relative reduction in the probability of being stone-free or free of clinically significant residual fragments with ESWL, corresponding under plausible baseline rates to an absolute difference of roughly 13–15 percentage points and a number needed to treat of about 7–8. The direction field accompanying the analysis records the comparison as favouring "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)", reflecting the event-coding dependence of ratio measures; under the review's prespecified convention (RR > 1 = higher success with ESWL), the observed value indicates an advantage for RIRS on this outcome.

The finding is directionally consistent (11 of 13 trials, including both largest), stable under cumulative synthesis, and shows no evidence of publication bias (Egger's p = 0.6318; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0). It is, however, tempered by moderate between-study heterogeneity (I² = 53.25%, τ² = 0.013611, Q = 25.486, df = 12, p = 0.0127) and an approximate prediction interval of 0.63–1.10 that crosses unity — meaning the size of RIRS's efficacy advantage, and occasionally its existence, depends on stone and protocol characteristics that this dataset could not model. Because complications, re-treatment burden, anaesthesia requirements, invasiveness, cost and quality of life were not pooled, the result quantifies efficacy alone and should inform, not determine, shared decision-making. Future randomised trials should stratify by stone size and location, prespecify a CT-verified success definition at a fixed three-month timepoint, report per-session and per-strategy outcomes separately, and publish arm-level stone characteristics so that the substantial unexplained heterogeneity identified here can finally be resolved.
