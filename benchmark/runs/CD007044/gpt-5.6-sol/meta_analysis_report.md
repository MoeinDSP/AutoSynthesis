# Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy Versus Retrograde Intrarenal Surgery for Renal Stones: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis of Treatment Success

## Abstract

**Background:** Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL/SWL) and retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) are commonly used treatments for renal stones. This review evaluated treatment success at approximately three months.

**Methods:** Comparative studies of adults with renal stones receiving ESWL versus RIRS were considered. The intended outcome was binary treatment success—stone-free status or freedom from clinically significant residual fragments—preferentially assessed at three months after the full planned treatment course. The supplied evidence set contained 13 studies, of which 10 contributed effect sizes. A random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled native-scale risk ratios (RRs), with ESWL as treatment and RIRS as control. By definition, RR >1 indicates a higher success rate with ESWL and RR <1 indicates a lower success rate with ESWL. However, the supplied automated favourability field classified the pooled result as favouring neither arm because event-direction confirmation was considered uncertain; that classification is retained as required.

**Results:** Ten comparisons involving 1,094 participants were pooled. The pooled risk ratio was 0.8706843934177492 (95% CI 0.787 to 0.963; p=0.007036517248998627). Heterogeneity was moderate (I²=47.84%; τ²=0.010249), and Cochran’s Q was 17.701 (p=0.0388). The supplied direction field classified this result as “neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).” Egger’s intercept was −0.6978 (p=0.1907), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀=0); publication-bias risk was rated low.

**Conclusions:** The pooled RR differed statistically from the null, but interpretation is constrained by absent study-level event counts, success definitions, imaging methods, assessment times, study identities, risk-of-bias assessments, and confirmation of event coding. On the supplied direction classification, the result cannot be assigned as favouring either ESWL or RIRS. Complete trial-level extraction is required before drawing a clinically definitive comparative-effectiveness conclusion.

## Introduction

Renal stones are common and can cause pain, obstruction, infection, and recurrent healthcare use. Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL/SWL) fragments stones non-invasively by externally generated shock waves, whereas retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS)—typically flexible ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy—requires endoscopic access to the collecting system. Their comparative effectiveness depends on stone burden and location, patient anatomy, retreatment policies, imaging modality, follow-up timing, and the residual-fragment threshold used to define success.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among adults with renal stones, what is the effect of ESWL, compared with RIRS, on binary treatment success at approximately three months? Treatment success was intended to mean stone-free status or freedom from clinically significant residual fragments according to each trial’s own definition and imaging method, assessed after the full planned treatment course.

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility framework

The prespecified framework was:

- **Population:** Adults with renal (kidney) stones.
- **Intervention:** ESWL/SWL.
- **Comparator:** RIRS, defined as flexible ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy.
- **Outcome:** Binary treatment success, defined by each trial as stone-free status or freedom from clinically significant residual fragments.
- **Timing:** Preferentially approximately three months; when unavailable, the assessment closest to three months was intended to be used.
- **Analysis principle:** Randomised denominators for intention-to-treat analysis, with assessment after the full planned treatment course when multiple treatment sessions were allowed.

For multi-arm trials, only eligible ESWL and RIRS groups were intended for extraction. Reported percentages were to be converted to event counts using the arm-specific analysed denominator, and failures or residual stones were to be converted to successes by subtraction from the corresponding total.

The supplied dataset identified **13 studies** and **10 effect sizes** eligible for quantitative synthesis. The quantitative analysis included **10 comparisons and 1,094 participants**. Three of the 13 studies did not contribute an effect size.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model fitted by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** included 10 comparisons and 1,094 participants. The **pooled risk ratio was 0.8706843934177492** (95% CI **0.787 to 0.963**; p=**0.007036517248998627**).

Thus, the pooled estimate was statistically distinguishable from the null RR of 1 at the conventional 0.05 level. Numerically, the pooled RR corresponds to an ESWL-to-RIRS recorded-success ratio of approximately 0.871. However, the supplied favourability classification was **neither arm**, because the event-direction interpretation was flagged as dependent on which event was counted. No claim that the pooled result favours ESWL or RIRS is therefore made here.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was moderate:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 17.701
- **Q-test p value:** 0.0388
- **I²:** 47.84%
- **τ²:** 0.010249

The statistically significant Q test indicates greater between-study variability than expected from sampling error alone. An I² of 47.84% suggests that a substantial, though not extreme, proportion of observed variation was associated with between-study heterogeneity. The estimated between-study variance, τ²=0.010249, is expressed on the model’s analysis scale.

Potential clinical sources include differences in stone size and location, imaging sensitivity, residual-fragment thresholds, timing of assessment, ESWL or RIRS retreatment policies, operator experience, and patient selection. These explanations are plausible but could not be tested with the supplied data.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−0.6978** with **p=0.1907**. This test did not detect statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀=0** missing studies, so no studies were imputed. The supplied trim-and-fill value was **−0.3108**, but its metric was not identified as the native RR scale; it should not be substituted for or compared directly with the pooled RR.

The overall publication-bias risk was classified as **low** in the supplied analysis. This finding should nonetheless be interpreted cautiously because only 10 comparisons were available, giving asymmetry tests limited power. Moreover, absence of statistical evidence for asymmetry does not establish absence of selective publication or selective outcome reporting.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis combined 10 comparisons involving 1,094 participants using a native-scale random-effects RR model. The pooled RR was 0.8706843934177492 (95% CI 0.787 to 0.963), with a p value of 0.007036517248998627. Between-study heterogeneity was moderate and statistically detectable (I²=47.84%; Q=17.701, p=0.0388), supporting use of a model that allows genuine effects to vary across studies.

The analysis metadata create an important interpretive constraint. The research question defines the event as treatment success, for which the numerical RR would ordinarily be read directly as the success rate under ESWL divided by that under RIRS. Nevertheless, the supplied direction field explicitly did not assign favourability to either intervention because event coding may be uncertain. The safest conclusion is therefore that the pooled ratio differed from 1, while the comparative direction should be considered unresolved until the underlying ESWL and RIRS success counts are audited.

Clinical interpretation is also limited by variation likely embedded in the outcome. Computed tomography is generally more sensitive for small residual fragments than ultrasound or plain radiography; studies using different modalities may consequently report different apparent stone-free rates even when clinical outcomes are similar. Likewise, defining success as complete stone clearance is more stringent than allowing clinically insignificant residual fragments. Pooling such definitions may be reasonable for a broad comparative-effectiveness question, but it reduces outcome uniformity and may contribute to heterogeneity.

Treatment-course definitions are similarly consequential. ESWL may involve several sessions, whereas RIRS may be evaluated after one procedure or after staged procedures. An endpoint measured after the full planned course addresses strategy-level effectiveness, while a single-session endpoint addresses procedural efficiency. The supplied data do not permit confirmation that all included comparisons used the former approach.

No evidence of small-study effects was detected by Egger’s test, and trim-and-fill imputed no studies. These diagnostics are reassuring but not definitive, particularly with k=10. A fuller assessment would require a funnel plot, protocol-to-report comparisons, trial-registry searches, and evaluation of selective reporting.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Several internal data-quality issues require explicit attention:

1. Thirteen studies were identified, but only 10 effect sizes were pooled; the status and exclusion reasons for the remaining three studies were not supplied.
2. Study identities and native arm-level success counts were unavailable, preventing verification of randomised denominators, event conversions, and intention-to-treat calculations.
3. Trial-specific success definitions, imaging methods, assessment times, and conference-abstract status were unavailable.
4. Comparison-level entries were supplied as standardized values labelled `hedges_g`, whereas the confirmed aggregate model used native risk-ratio pooling. The standardized entries were therefore not treated as study-specific RRs.
5. The pooled numerical RR and the supplied favourability field are not straightforwardly aligned. The direction field was retained as authoritative, and no arm was declared favoured.
6. The trim-and-fill estimate of −0.3108 appears not to be on the native RR scale and was not interpreted as an adjusted pooled RR.
7. No information was provided to determine whether multiple reports from the same trial were identified and handled to avoid double-counting.

## Limitations

First, the bibliographic search and PRISMA screening record were absent, so search completeness and selection reproducibility cannot be assessed. Second, study-level clinical and methodological characteristics were not supplied, preventing evaluation of applicability and prespecified moderators. Third, no risk-of-bias assessment was available. Fourth, arm-level events and denominators were unavailable, so the native study RRs and pooled result could not be independently reconstructed.

Fifth, outcome heterogeneity may be clinically important: success could reflect complete stone clearance or allowance for residual fragments and could be measured by CT, ultrasonography, or radiography at different times. Sixth, the direction of the pooled result remains uncertain under the supplied favourability metadata despite the outcome being described as treatment success. Seventh, statistical assessment of publication bias had limited power with only 10 comparisons. Finally, no certainty-of-evidence assessment, such as GRADE, was supplied; a formal certainty rating would require risk-of-bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication-bias judgements.

## Conclusion

Across 10 comparisons and 1,094 participants, the REML random-effects **pooled risk ratio was 0.8706843934177492** (95% CI 0.787 to 0.963; p=0.007036517248998627). Heterogeneity was moderate (I²=47.84%; τ²=0.010249; Q=17.701, p=0.0388). Egger’s test was not statistically significant (intercept −0.6978; p=0.1907), and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies.

Although the pooled ratio differed statistically from 1, the supplied analysis classified the result as favouring neither arm because event direction was not considered securely confirmed. A definitive clinical conclusion about whether ESWL or RIRS yields greater three-month treatment success therefore requires verification of study-level success coding, randomised denominators, imaging methods, outcome thresholds, and follow-up times.
