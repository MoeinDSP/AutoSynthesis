# Cotrimoxazole Prophylaxis Plus an Additional Antimalarial (Mefloquine or Dihydroartemisinin–Piperaquine) versus Cotrimoxazole Prophylaxis Alone for the Prevention of Low Birth Weight in HIV-Positive Pregnant Women: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** HIV-positive pregnant women in malaria-endemic settings are routinely given daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis (CTXp), which has partial antimalarial activity and has therefore displaced intermittent preventive treatment with sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine in this population. Whether adding a further antimalarial agent — mefloquine (MQ) or dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine (DP) — to CTXp improves birth outcomes remains uncertain. Low birth weight (LBW, <2500 g) is the most consistently reported and clinically consequential birth outcome in this literature.

**Objective.** To determine whether daily CTXp combined with an additional antimalarial reduces the risk of LBW compared with daily CTXp alone (with or without placebo) in HIV-positive pregnant women.

**Methods.** We systematically searched for randomised controlled trials in which HIV-positive pregnant women receiving daily CTXp were allocated to an additional antimalarial (MQ or DP) or to CTXp alone/placebo, with LBW (<2500 g among live births) reported as counts of events and denominators per arm. Risk ratios (RR) were computed from the extracted 2×2 data and pooled on the native RR scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, τ² and I². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** Five randomised comparisons contributed data on 2,915 women/infants. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for LBW was 1.159 (95% CI 0.952 to 1.411; p = 0.1427; k = 5; N = 2,915), a result that was not statistically significant and that, on the basis of the direction field carried through the analysis, favours **neither** arm — the two strategies were essentially tied on this outcome. Native RR pooling was used, so this is a confirmed native-scale result, not a standardized fallback. Between-study heterogeneity was absent: Q = 1.105 (p = 0.8934), I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0. Egger's test showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept 0.2214; p = 0.2664), but trim-and-fill imputed k₀ = 2 potentially missing studies (adjusted standardized estimate 0.1021), yielding an overall "moderate" risk-of-bias-due-to-small-study-effects rating. With only five trials, no moderator analysis could be meaningfully fitted.

**Conclusions.** In HIV-positive pregnant women already receiving daily CTXp, adding mefloquine or dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine did not reduce the risk of low birth weight. The pooled estimate was fully compatible with no effect (CI spanning a 4.8% relative reduction to a 41.1% relative increase), heterogeneity was nil, and the finding was robust to trim-and-fill adjustment. These data do not support the addition of a second antimalarial to CTXp for the specific purpose of preventing low birth weight, although they do not exclude benefits on other malaria-specific endpoints.

## 1. Introduction

Malaria in pregnancy is a major determinant of adverse birth outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa, acting principally through placental sequestration of *Plasmodium falciparum*, maternal anaemia and fetal growth restriction. HIV infection amplifies this risk: HIV-positive pregnant women experience higher parasite densities, more frequent placental infection, and a greater burden of low birth weight (LBW, <2500 g) than their HIV-negative counterparts.

For HIV-positive pregnant women, the standard of care in most endemic programmes is **daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis (CTXp)**, given primarily for the prevention of opportunistic infections. Because cotrimoxazole (trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole) is an antifolate with intrinsic antimalarial activity, intermittent preventive treatment in pregnancy with sulfadoxine–pyrimethamine (IPTp-SP) is contraindicated during CTXp, both for pharmacological redundancy and for concerns about additive toxicity and antifolate resistance selection. This leaves an important clinical question: is CTXp alone sufficient, or does the addition of a second, non-antifolate antimalarial — such as **mefloquine (MQ)** or **dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine (DP)** — confer additional protection against adverse birth outcomes?

Several randomised controlled trials have addressed this question, randomising women on CTXp to an additional antimalarial or to CTXp alone/placebo. Individual trials have generally been powered for malaria-specific endpoints (peripheral or placental parasitaemia) rather than for birth weight, and their LBW results have been individually inconclusive. A quantitative synthesis is therefore warranted.

**Research question.** In HIV-positive pregnant women, does daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis combined with an additional antimalarial (mefloquine or dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine) reduce the risk of low birth weight (<2500 g) compared with CTXp alone (with or without placebo, the current standard of care)?

**Pre-specified effect direction.** For this review, a *positive* (beneficial) effect corresponds to a risk ratio **below 1.0** after signing — that is, fewer low-birth-weight babies in the arm receiving the additional antimalarial. A risk ratio above 1.0 corresponds to more low-birth-weight babies in that arm. This convention is stated explicitly because the raw statistical sign convention used throughout the analysis is treatment-minus-control, and for an adverse-event outcome such as LBW the lower raw value is the favourable one.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Information sources and strategy

The review followed PRISMA 2020 reporting guidance. Electronic bibliographic sources were searched for randomised controlled trials comparing an added antimalarial plus CTXp against CTXp alone or CTXp plus placebo in HIV-positive pregnant women. The search combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms across three concept blocks:

1. **Population** — "HIV", "HIV-infected", "pregnan*", "antenatal", "gravid*";
2. **Intervention/comparator** — "cotrimoxazole", "co-trimoxazole", "trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole", "CTXp", "mefloquine", "dihydroartemisinin-piperaquine", "piperaquine", "intermittent preventive treatment", "IPTp", "chemoprophylaxis";
3. **Outcome** — "low birth weight", "birth weight", "birthweight", "pregnancy outcome", "preterm", "malaria in pregnancy".

No language restriction was applied. Reference lists of included trials and of prior systematic reviews on malaria chemoprevention in pregnancy were hand-searched, and trial registries were checked for completed-but-unpublished studies.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Included:** (i) randomised controlled trials; (ii) enrolling HIV-positive pregnant women receiving daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis; (iii) comparing CTXp plus an additional antimalarial (mefloquine or dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine, by any dosing schedule) against CTXp alone, with or without a matched placebo; (iv) reporting low birth weight among live-born singletons as the number of events and the number of women/infants randomised or evaluated per arm.

**Excluded:** narrative or systematic reviews, editorials and commentaries; observational designs; trials in HIV-negative or mixed populations where HIV-positive women could not be separated; trials in which the comparator arm did not receive CTXp; secondary or duplicate reports of an already-included trial (retained only once, with the most complete report used as the index publication); and trials reporting birth weight only as a continuous mean without LBW counts.

### 2.3 Screening and data extraction

Records were de-duplicated and screened on title and abstract, then at full text against the criteria above. Review articles and duplicate publications of the same trial were removed at this stage so that no participant cohort contributed more than once to the pooled estimate. From each eligible comparison we extracted: trial identifier, the additional antimalarial and its dosing schedule, the comparator formulation (placebo-controlled versus open CTXp-alone), the number of LBW events per arm, and the number of women/infants evaluated per arm.

### 2.4 Screening outcome

Screening yielded **five eligible randomised comparisons**, contributing **five effect sizes** and **2,915 women/infants** to the analysis. Because each eligible trial contributed exactly one LBW comparison, the number of effect sizes equals the number of studies (k = 5), and no within-study dependency correction or multilevel model was required. All five comparisons reported the same outcome construct at the same timepoint (birth weight recorded at delivery, dichotomised at 2500 g), so a **single meta-analytic group** was formed; no separate outcome-or-timepoint subgroups were necessary.

**Common features of the evidence base.** All five comparisons shared the same clinical architecture: a population of HIV-positive pregnant women in malaria-endemic settings, universal daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis as the background standard of care in both arms, randomisation to an additional antimalarial (mefloquine given as intermittent preventive doses, or dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine given as intermittent preventive courses) versus CTXp alone or CTXp plus matching placebo, and ascertainment of birth weight at delivery with dichotomisation at the conventional 2500 g threshold. Trial sizes ranged over a five-fold span (197 to 975 analysed participants), giving a mix of two moderately large comparisons (975 and 855), one mid-sized comparison (643) and two small comparisons (245 and 197). This distribution is relevant to the publication-bias assessment reported in Section 7, since funnel-based diagnostics depend on having a spread of study precisions.

**Risk of bias considerations.** The principal domain-level concerns across this evidence base are (i) **blinding of the intervention**, which differed between placebo-controlled trials and those using an open CTXp-alone comparator — mefloquine in particular has a well-documented tolerability profile (dizziness, vomiting) that can unblind participants and may reduce adherence; (ii) **outcome ascertainment**, which is relatively robust for birth weight when measured on calibrated scales at facility delivery, but is vulnerable to missingness when a proportion of women deliver at home; and (iii) **attrition**, since LBW can only be ascertained among live births with a recorded weight, so denominators reflect evaluated rather than randomised women in some trials. Birth weight was a secondary endpoint in most of these trials, which were powered for parasitological outcomes; the LBW comparisons are therefore individually underpowered by design, which is precisely the rationale for pooling.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Primary (native-scale) effect measure

The pre-specified and analysed effect measure for this review is the **Risk Ratio (RR)** for low birth weight, computed from the event counts and denominators in each arm. The effect measure was set by explicit user request and **native RR pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`): the pooled result reported in Section 5 is a confirmed native-scale risk ratio, computed and combined on the RR scale, not a standardized surrogate back-transformed from another metric. No conflict between the requested and the detected effect measure was recorded.

In addition to the native risk ratios, the pipeline produced a standardized (Hedges' *g*-scale) representation of each comparison. Four of the five standardized study-level estimates are positive (i.e. pointing towards *more* LBW in the added-antimalarial arm under the signing convention of Section 1) and one (study_5_R5) is marginally negative. ## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was analysed (all five comparisons measure LBW <2500 g at delivery). A random-effects model with **REML** estimation of between-study variance was fitted on the native RR scale.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Risk Ratio (RR)** (native pooling confirmed) |
| Number of studies (k) | 5 |
| Total participants (N) | 2,915 |
| **Pooled Risk Ratio** | **1.1586** |
| 95% confidence interval | **0.952 to 1.411** |
| p-value | **0.1427** |
| Favours | **Neither arm** (essentially a tie; for a ratio measure, favourability depends on which event was counted) |
| τ² | 0.0 |
| I² | 0.0% |
| Cochran's Q | 1.105 (df = 4), p = 0.8934 |
| Estimation method | REML |

**Interpretation.** The pooled risk ratio for low birth weight was **1.159 (95% CI 0.952–1.411, p = 0.1427)**. The confidence interval comfortably includes the null value of 1.0, and the result is not statistically significant at any conventional threshold. Per the direction field carried with the analysis, this result **favours neither arm** — the comparison is, in effect, a tie on this outcome. Translated into clinical language, the interval is compatible with anything from a **4.8% relative reduction** to a **41.1% relative increase** in the risk of delivering a baby weighing less than 2500 g when an additional antimalarial is given on top of daily CTXp. Under the signing convention specified for this review (RR < 1 = benefit), the point estimate of 1.159 lies on the non-beneficial side of the null, but it cannot be distinguished statistically from no difference.

Crucially, the data provide **no support for the hypothesis that adding mefloquine or dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine to CTXp reduces low birth weight**. The upper bound of the interval also means that a modest increase in LBW risk cannot be excluded — a consideration of some importance given the known tolerability problems of mefloquine in pregnancy and the possibility that treatment-related vomiting or reduced CTXp adherence could offset any antiparasitic gain.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **absent**:

- **Cochran's Q = 1.105** on 4 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.8934** — far from significance, and in fact Q is smaller than its expected value under the null of homogeneity (E[Q] = df = 4), indicating that the observed scatter of effects is, if anything, *less* than would be expected from sampling error alone.
- **I² = 0.0%** — none of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to true between-study differences.
- **τ² = 0.0** — the REML estimate of between-study variance is zero, so the random-effects model collapses to a fixed-effect weighting in practice.

This is an unusually homogeneous evidence base. Despite differences in the specific antimalarial used (mefloquine vs dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine), in comparator design (placebo-controlled vs open CTXp-alone), and in trial size (197 to 975), all five comparisons point to a small effect close to the null. The practical consequence is that the pooled estimate is a well-behaved summary of a consistent set of findings rather than an average over conflicting results, and that the confidence interval is driven almost entirely by sampling precision rather than by inter-study variance inflation.

A caveat is warranted: with only k = 5 studies, Q has very low power to detect moderate heterogeneity and I² is estimated with wide uncertainty. "I² = 0%" should therefore be read as "no detectable heterogeneity in a small set of trials" rather than as positive proof of perfect exchangeability. Graphical heterogeneity diagnostics are summarised in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 7. Moderators

No moderator (meta-regression or subgroup) analysis was fitted. Two considerations make this the appropriate decision rather than an omission:

1. **Insufficient studies.** With k = 5, conventional guidance (a minimum of roughly 10 studies per covariate for meta-regression) is not remotely met. Any subgroup split — for example mefloquine versus dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine, or placebo-controlled versus open-label comparator — would leave two or three studies per cell and produce estimates with confidence intervals too wide to be informative, with a high risk of spurious subgroup findings.
2. **No heterogeneity to explain.** With τ² = 0.0 and I² = 0.0%, there is no residual between-study variance for a moderator to account for. Moderator analysis is motivated by unexplained dispersion; here there is none.

The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) displays effect magnitude against study precision and shows no visually apparent gradient of effect with sample size beyond that expected from random error. We therefore report **no moderator results**, rather than reporting an underpowered model as if it were informative.

## 8. Publication Bias

Small-study effects were examined using Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure, both computed on the standardized study-level estimates.

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.2214 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.2664 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 2 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted standardized estimate | 0.1021 |
| Overall risk rating | **Moderate** |

**Interpretation.** Egger's regression test showed no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept 0.2214, p = 0.2664), so there is no formal statistical evidence of small-study effects or publication bias in this meta-analysis. However, the trim-and-fill procedure imputed **two potentially missing studies (k₀ = 2)**, indicating some degree of funnel plot asymmetry that the Egger test may have been underpowered to detect — a common limitation when the number of included studies is small. The "moderate" risk rating reflects this mixed signal: the pooled estimate is unlikely to be severely distorted, but it should be interpreted with some caution.

Reassuringly, the trim-and-fill adjusted standardized estimate (0.1021) lies squarely within the range of the observed study-level standardized estimates (−0.0158 to 0.1529) and remains small and null-adjacent. Imputing the two hypothetically missing studies therefore does **not** change the direction of the finding, nor does it convert a null result into a significant one. The substantive conclusion — that adding an antimalarial to CTXp does not reduce low birth weight — is unchanged by adjustment for possible small-study effects.

Two further points temper the bias concern in this particular literature. First, these trials were powered for parasitological endpoints, so LBW is typically a secondary outcome reported irrespective of its statistical significance, which weakens the usual selective-reporting mechanism for this specific outcome. Second, with k = 5, funnel-based methods have very low power and trim-and-fill is known to impute studies spuriously in small, homogeneous datasets; the k₀ = 2 imputation should not be over-interpreted.

## 9. Discussion

This meta-analysis of five randomised comparisons in 2,915 HIV-positive pregnant women found that adding mefloquine or dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine to daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis **did not reduce the risk of low birth weight**. The pooled Risk Ratio was 1.159 (95% CI 0.952–1.411, p = 0.1427), favouring neither arm, with zero detectable heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q = 1.105, p = 0.8934).

Three features make this a comparatively trustworthy null result. First, the **consistency** is remarkable: all five comparisons, spanning two different drug classes and both placebo-controlled and open designs, produced small effects clustered near the null, and Cochran's Q was actually below its expected value. Second, the **sample size is substantial** for this population (N = 2,915), so the confidence interval, while not narrow, is informative enough to exclude any large protective effect. Third, the conclusion is **robust to bias adjustment**: trim-and-fill imputation of two studies left the estimate small and non-significant.

Why might additional chemoprevention fail to improve birth weight in this group? Several explanations are plausible and mutually compatible. (i) **Background CTXp is already effective.** Daily cotrimoxazole provides continuous antifolate pressure and substantial protection against *P. falciparum*; the marginal antiparasitic benefit of an added intermittent agent may be small, leaving little room for improvement in a downstream outcome such as birth weight. (ii) **Birth weight is multifactorial in HIV-positive women.** HIV itself, antiretroviral regimens, maternal anaemia, undernutrition, co-infections and socioeconomic factors all contribute to fetal growth restriction and preterm delivery; malaria may account for only a modest fraction of attributable LBW once CTXp is in place, so even a genuine reduction in parasitaemia need not translate into a measurable shift in the LBW proportion. (iii) **Tolerability offsets.** Mefloquine in particular causes dizziness and vomiting in a substantial minority of pregnant women; vomiting of concomitant medication and reduced adherence to CTXp or antiretrovirals could plausibly counterbalance any antimalarial gain — a mechanism consistent with the point estimate sitting marginally above 1.0 rather than below it. (iv) **Statistical power for a secondary endpoint.** These trials were sized for parasitological outcomes; even pooled, the analysis cannot exclude a small (<5%) relative reduction in LBW.

It is important to delimit the scope of this conclusion. The outcome analysed here is **low birth weight only**. These same trials have reported effects on peripheral and placental parasitaemia, maternal anaemia and clinical malaria episodes, and a null finding for LBW does not imply a null finding for those malaria-specific endpoints — indeed, added antimalarials may well reduce parasitaemia without a detectable birth-weight signal. Decisions about chemoprevention policy in HIV-positive pregnancy should weigh the full outcome set, including safety and tolerability, HIV-related outcomes, resistance selection, and cost, rather than the LBW result in isolation. On the specific question posed, however, the evidence is reasonably clear: **the addition of a second antimalarial to CTXp cannot be justified on the grounds of preventing low birth weight.**

## 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

No formal validation flags were returned by the analysis pipeline for this dataset. For completeness and transparency, the following data-handling points are recorded:

- **Review and duplicate records.** Narrative and systematic reviews, commentaries, and secondary or duplicate reports of already-included trials were excluded during screening, so that no participant cohort contributes more than once to the pooled estimate. This safeguard is important in the malaria-in-pregnancy literature, where multi-outcome trials are frequently reported across several papers.
- **Outcome and timepoint homogeneity.** All five included comparisons reported the identical outcome construct (live-born infant weighing <2500 g, measured at delivery). Consequently only one meta-analytic group was formed, and no group was set aside as having insufficient data for pooling.
- **Effect measure provenance.** The effect measure was Risk Ratio (RR), set by explicit user request, with the computation scale also RR and `native_pooling_used = true`. No conflict between requested and detected measures was recorded. The pooled value is therefore a confirmed native-scale risk ratio.
- **Dual-scale reporting.** The analysis object reports `pooled_effect` and `pooled_g` with the same numeric value (1.1586). This value is the pooled **risk ratio**; the `pooled_g` field in this instance carries the native-scale quantity rather than a standardized mean difference. The separate study-level standardized estimates tabulated in Section 4.2 (range −0.0158 to 0.1529) and the trim-and-fill adjusted value (0.1021) are on the standardized scale and are used only for funnel-plot diagnostics. Readers should not interpret the pooled 1.159 as a Hedges' *g*, nor the trim-and-fill 0.1021 as a risk ratio.
- **Denominators.** LBW is ascertainable only among live births with a recorded weight; per-arm denominators therefore reflect evaluated rather than strictly randomised women in some trials, a standard but non-trivial deviation from intention-to-treat.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Small number of trials.** With k = 5, the analysis has limited power for heterogeneity detection (Q, I², τ² are all imprecisely estimated), no capacity for meta-regression, and low power for funnel-based bias diagnostics. The I² of 0.0% should be read as "no detectable heterogeneity", not as proven homogeneity.
2. **Residual uncertainty around the null.** The 95% CI (0.952–1.411) excludes any large protective effect but is compatible with a small reduction or a moderate increase in LBW risk. The study is not an equivalence trial, and absence of evidence for benefit is not conclusive evidence of exact equivalence.
3. **Clinical and pharmacological pooling.** Mefloquine and dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine differ substantially in pharmacokinetics, tolerability and dosing schedule, and placebo-controlled trials differ from open CTXp-alone comparisons in their susceptibility to performance bias. These sources of clinical diversity were combined in a single pooled estimate because heterogeneity was nil and k was too small for subgroup analysis; a larger evidence base should examine them separately.
4. **Secondary-outcome status and ascertainment.** LBW was a secondary endpoint in most contributing trials, with denominators restricted to live births with a recorded weight. Home deliveries, stillbirths and losses to follow-up introduce potential informative missingness that a count-based synthesis cannot correct.
5. **Dichotomisation.** The 2500 g threshold discards information on the birth-weight distribution. A mean-difference analysis of continuous birth weight, or an analysis of small-for-gestational-age accounting for gestational age, might be more sensitive to a modest shift in fetal growth; such data were not uniformly available.
6. **Moderate small-study-effect rating.** Trim-and-fill imputed two studies despite a non-significant Egger test. Although adjustment did not change the conclusion, the possibility of modest funnel asymmetry cannot be formally excluded with this number of trials.
7. **Generalisability.** The included trials were conducted in malaria-endemic African settings with particular transmission intensities, resistance profiles and antiretroviral coverage. Findings may not extrapolate to settings with markedly different antifolate resistance prevalence or to contemporary dolutegravir-based ART cohorts.
8. **De-identified study-level reporting.** Contributing trials are carried in the analytic dataset under pipeline identifiers rather than author–year citations, which limits the granularity of the study-characteristics and risk-of-bias description that can be presented here.

---

## 12. Conclusion

Among HIV-positive pregnant women receiving daily cotrimoxazole prophylaxis, the addition of mefloquine or dihydroartemisinin–piperaquine **did not reduce the risk of low birth weight**. Across five randomised comparisons and 2,915 women/infants, the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was **1.159 (95% CI 0.952–1.411; p = 0.1427)** — a non-significant result that favours **neither** arm — with no detectable between-study heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0; Q = 1.105, p = 0.8934). Native RR pooling was used, so this is a confirmed native-scale estimate. Publication-bias diagnostics were mixed (Egger p = 0.2664; trim-and-fill k₀ = 2; adjusted standardized estimate 0.1021), giving a moderate risk rating, but adjustment left the direction and non-significance of the finding unchanged.

The consistency of these results across drug classes and trial designs argues that CTXp alone, as currently delivered, is not meaningfully improved upon by a second antimalarial with respect to this particular birth outcome. Future research should focus on continuous birth-weight and gestational-age-adjusted endpoints, on adherence and tolerability as mediators, and on whether any parasitological benefit of added chemoprevention translates into clinical gains in contemporary ART-era cohorts.
