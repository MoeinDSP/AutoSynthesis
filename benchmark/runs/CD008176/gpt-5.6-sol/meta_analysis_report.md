# Antioxidant or Nrf2-Activating Therapy and Cardiovascular Mortality in Adults With Chronic Kidney Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Oxidative stress is implicated in the high cardiovascular risk associated with chronic kidney disease (CKD), but the effect of antioxidant or Nrf2-activating therapy on cardiovascular mortality remains uncertain.

### Objective
To assess, among adults with CKD stages 3–5, receiving dialysis, or living with a kidney transplant, the effect of antioxidant or Nrf2-activating therapy versus placebo, usual care, or no treatment on cardiovascular death over the whole reported follow-up period.

### Methods
The supplied evidence set contained 7 studies and 4 quantitative effect-size records. Three studies (1,323 participants) contributed to the requested random-effects Risk Ratio (RR) meta-analysis; one additional study (2,185 participants) was kept separate because its source result was identified as a hazard ratio (HR), creating a measure-classification conflict. Random-effects pooling used restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Cardiovascular death was restricted to deaths explicitly classified as cardiovascular by the study; all-cause mortality, nonfatal events, and composite cardiovascular outcomes were not eligible.

### Results
Across the 3 studies eligible for RR pooling, the pooled Risk Ratio was 0.9454435092901602 (95% CI 0.702–1.273; p = 0.7116885643343955). The supplied directional classification was “neither (tie or undetermined).” Heterogeneity was not detected (Q = 1.604, p = 0.4485; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). The fourth quantitative study could not support a separate meta-analysis (k = 1). Egger’s intercept was 0.1723 (p = 0.4004), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0); nevertheless, these diagnostics were severely underpowered because very few studies were available.

### Conclusions
The available RR evidence does not demonstrate a statistically significant effect of antioxidant or Nrf2-activating therapy on cardiovascular mortality in adults with CKD. The confidence interval remains compatible with potentially meaningful benefit or harm. Interpretation is limited by sparse eligible evidence, incomplete reporting details, and an unresolved effect-measure conflict in one additional study.

## Introduction

Adults with chronic kidney disease (CKD), including those with advanced nondialysis CKD, those receiving dialysis, and kidney-transplant recipients, experience substantially elevated cardiovascular morbidity and mortality. Oxidative stress, inflammation, endothelial dysfunction, and disordered redox signaling have been proposed as contributors to this excess risk. Accordingly, antioxidant interventions and agents that activate nuclear factor erythroid 2–related factor 2 (Nrf2) have been evaluated as potential strategies for improving cardiovascular outcomes.

The intervention scope for this review comprised antioxidant or Nrf2-activating therapy, including vitamin E and other vitamins, N-acetylcysteine, selenium, bardoxolone methyl, pentoxifylline, and other agents with an antioxidant or Nrf2-activating action. Eligible comparators were placebo, usual care, or no treatment.

The prespecified question was: among adults with CKD stages 3–5, receiving dialysis, or with a kidney transplant, what is the effect of antioxidant or Nrf2-activating therapy compared with placebo, usual care, or no treatment on cardiovascular death over the whole reported follow-up period? The requested effect measure was the Risk Ratio (RR), with an RR below 1 conventionally corresponding to fewer cardiovascular deaths in the antioxidant arm. Directional conclusions in this report follow the classifications supplied with the analysis results rather than being reconstructed from the numerical sign or magnitude.

## Search and Screening

The supplied dataset identified 7 studies. Four studies yielded quantitative effect-size records, while 3 did not contribute an extractable estimate to the presented quantitative synthesis. Three studies were included in the RR meta-analysis, and one additional study was retained as a separate outcome–measure group because the source paper was identified as reporting an HR rather than an RR.

Eligibility was defined using the following framework:

- **Population:** Adults with CKD stages 3–5, receiving dialysis, or with a kidney transplant.
- **Intervention:** Antioxidant or Nrf2-activating therapy.
- **Comparator:** Placebo, usual care, or no treatment.
- **Outcome:** Cardiovascular mortality over the whole reported follow-up period.
- **Outcome restrictions:** Only deaths explicitly classified as cardiovascular were eligible. All-cause deaths, nonfatal cardiovascular events, and composite cardiovascular outcomes were excluded. Components such as fatal myocardial infarction, fatal stroke, or sudden death could be combined only when the study itself defined them as cardiovascular death. Cardiovascular deaths were not inferred from all-cause deaths, and unreported event counts were not estimated.

Database names, search dates, complete strategies, deduplication counts, title/abstract screening counts, full-text exclusion reasons, reviewer procedures, protocol registration, and study-level risk-of-bias judgments were not supplied. A complete PRISMA flow diagram therefore cannot be reconstructed from the available information. No study citations were provided, so the report uses the supplied identifiers.

## Included Studies

The evidence set comprised 7 studies, of which 4 had quantitative records. The 3 studies in the RR synthesis included 1,323 participants in total:

- `study_1_R1`: 134 participants;
- `study_2_R2`: 993 participants; and
- `study_5_R5`: 196 participants.

A fourth study, `study_4_R4`, included 2,185 participants and was assigned to a separate group identified by the source-result type as HR. Because this group contained only one study, it was not pooled. Thus, 3,508 participants were represented across the four quantitative records, but only 1,323 contributed to the pooled RR.

No information was supplied on CKD stage distribution, dialysis modality, transplant status, participant age or sex, specific antioxidant agent and dose, treatment duration, length of follow-up, control regimen, geographic setting, or baseline cardiovascular risk. Consequently, clinical comparability across the included studies cannot be assessed fully.

## Meta-Analytic Results

### Cardiovascular mortality: RR group

Three studies with 1,323 participants were synthesized using a REML random-effects model. The pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** was **0.9454435092901602** (95% CI **0.702–1.273**; p = **0.7116885643343955**). The confidence interval includes the null value of 1, and the result was not statistically significant.

The supplied favorability field classified this result as **“neither (tie or undetermined)”**. Accordingly, the analysis does not establish that antioxidant or Nrf2-activating therapy reduces cardiovascular mortality relative to placebo, usual care, or no treatment. The confidence interval is also sufficiently broad to remain compatible with clinically meaningful benefit or harm.

The cumulative meta-analysis is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`.

### Cardiovascular mortality: source-HR group

One additional study with 2,185 participants formed a separate group identified as `cardiovascular mortality::measure=HR`. With **k = 1**, there were insufficient data to calculate a pooled result, and no pooled effect should be inferred. Its supplied single-study standardized value was 0.200969. The metadata explicitly note a conflict: the user-requested RR was retained as authoritative in the analysis labeling, although the paper reportedly presented an HR. Because an HR and an RR are not generally interchangeable, this study was appropriately not merged with the RR group.

No overall estimate combining the RR and source-HR groups is presented.

## Heterogeneity

For the three-study RR synthesis, Cochran’s Q was **1.604** with 2 degrees of freedom (p = **0.4485**). Estimated inconsistency was **I² = 0.0%**, and the estimated between-study variance was **τ² = 0.0**.

These values provide no statistical evidence of between-study heterogeneity in the pooled RR group. However, I² and τ² are imprecisely estimated when only three studies are available. The absence of detected heterogeneity should therefore not be interpreted as proof that the studies are clinically or methodologically homogeneous. A graphical heterogeneity summary is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## Publication Bias

The supplied small-study-effect analysis reported an Egger intercept of **0.1723** with p = **0.4004**. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies and produced an adjusted diagnostic estimate of **0.0249** on the supplied scale. The pipeline classified publication-bias risk as **low**, and the formal test did not identify statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry.

These findings should be interpreted very cautiously. Funnel-plot methods, Egger’s regression, and trim-and-fill have little power and can be unstable with so few studies. A nonsignificant Egger test does not establish the absence of publication bias, selective nonreporting, or small-study effects. Moreover, because the available evidence included only three poolable RR studies and one separately classified record, the assumptions underlying these diagnostics are difficult to verify.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

The random-effects synthesis of 3 studies involving 1,323 participants found a pooled Risk Ratio of 0.9454435092901602 (95% CI 0.702–1.273) for cardiovascular mortality. The result was not statistically significant (p = 0.7116885643343955), and its supplied directional classification was neither arm favored. Statistical heterogeneity was not detected (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), although the small number of studies limits the informativeness of heterogeneity statistics.

One additional study involving 2,185 participants could not be incorporated because its source estimate was identified as an HR. As only one study was available in that measure group, no separate pooled estimate was possible. Combining it with the RR studies would have obscured the distinction between cumulative risk and instantaneous event-rate measures and could have produced a misleading summary.

### Clinical interpretation

The pooled estimate does not provide evidence that antioxidant or Nrf2-activating therapy changes cardiovascular mortality in adults with CKD. Importantly, lack of statistical significance is not evidence of equivalence. The 95% CI extends from 0.702 to 1.273 and therefore leaves open both a potentially important relative reduction and a potentially important relative increase in cardiovascular death.

The broad intervention category may encompass agents with substantially different mechanisms, pharmacologic potency, safety profiles, and clinical indications. Similarly, patients with moderate nondialysis CKD, kidney failure treated by dialysis, and kidney transplants may differ greatly in oxidative stress, competing mortality risks, comorbid cardiovascular disease, immunosuppressive exposure, and treatment responsiveness. Because agent-level and population-level information was unavailable, the pooled result should not be assumed to apply uniformly across all antioxidant therapies or CKD populations.

### Relation to the evidence base

Only 4 of the 7 identified studies yielded quantitative records, and only 3 were eligible for the requested RR synthesis. This sparse outcome-specific evidence is plausible because cardiovascular death is often reported within broader composites or as part of all-cause mortality. The strict outcome definition used here avoids incorrectly treating nonfatal outcomes or inferred cardiovascular deaths as observed fatal cardiovascular events, but it also reduces the number of studies available for synthesis.

### Implications for research

Future trials and reports should provide cardiovascular-death counts by randomized arm, the corresponding analyzed denominators, explicit cause-of-death definitions, adjudication procedures, and complete follow-up durations. Investigators should distinguish cardiovascular mortality from all-cause mortality and composite major cardiovascular outcomes. Where time-to-event analyses are reported, both the HR and sufficient cumulative event information for risk-based analyses would improve comparability. Larger datasets are also needed to evaluate whether effects vary by antioxidant class, CKD stage, dialysis or transplant status, and baseline cardiovascular risk.

## Data Quality and Validation Notes

The supplied evidence structure raises several validation issues that materially affect interpretation:

1. Seven studies were identified, but only 4 quantitative effect-size records were available; reasons why the other 3 studies did not contribute were not supplied.
2. Only 3 records, totaling 1,323 participants, were included in the requested RR meta-analysis.
3. One record with 2,185 participants was classified by its outcome-group key as originating from an HR, while the analysis metadata retained the requested “Risk Ratio (RR)” label. This measure conflict was not resolved, and the study was kept separate to avoid inappropriate pooling.
4. The four record-level values were supplied on a standardized pipeline scale and were not accompanied by arm-level cardiovascular-death counts. They therefore cannot be independently reconstructed or verified as native study-level RRs.
5. No study citations, intervention details, follow-up durations, risk-of-bias assessments, or outcome-adjudication information were provided.

These issues do not alter the reported numerical pooled result, but they substantially constrain verification, reproducibility, and clinical interpretation.

## Limitations

This review has several important limitations.

First, the search and screening record was incomplete. Search sources, dates, search strings, language restrictions, duplicate removal, reviewer procedures, reasons for full-text exclusion, and protocol registration were unavailable. Full compliance with PRISMA reporting cannot therefore be demonstrated.

Second, cardiovascular mortality was informed by few studies. Only three studies were pooled, limiting precision, heterogeneity assessment, subgroup analysis, and evaluation of publication bias.

Third, arm-level event counts and denominators were not supplied. The eligibility of individual deaths, handling of participants lost to follow-up, and consistency of denominators could not be independently audited.

Fourth, the intervention category was clinically broad. Pooling diverse antioxidant and Nrf2-activating agents may conceal treatment-specific benefit or harm. No agent-specific analyses were possible.

Fifth, one study was reported as supplying an HR while the requested effect measure was RR. Its exclusion from the RR pool avoided conflating measures, but it also meant that 2,185 participants did not contribute to the principal pooled estimate.

Sixth, no risk-of-bias assessments or certainty-of-evidence ratings were available. The strength of inference cannot be judged solely from statistical precision and heterogeneity.

Finally, Egger’s test and trim-and-fill are unreliable with such a small evidence base. The “low” publication-bias classification should not be interpreted as conclusive evidence that reporting bias is absent.

## Conclusion

In 3 studies involving 1,323 adults with CKD, antioxidant or Nrf2-activating therapy was not associated with a statistically significant difference in cardiovascular mortality compared with placebo, usual care, or no treatment (pooled Risk Ratio **0.9454435092901602**, 95% CI **0.702–1.273**; p = **0.7116885643343955**). The supplied directional classification favored neither arm. No heterogeneity was detected, but the evidence base was too small for this finding to be reassuring.

A fourth study involving 2,185 participants was not pooled because it formed a single-study source-HR group and had an unresolved effect-measure conflict. Overall, the evidence is insufficient to establish cardiovascular mortality benefit or harm. Better-reported, adequately powered trials with explicit cardiovascular-death counts and consistent effect measures are required.
