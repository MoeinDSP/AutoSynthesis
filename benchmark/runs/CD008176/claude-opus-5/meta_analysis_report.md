# Antioxidant and Nrf2-Activating Therapies and Cardiovascular Death in Adults with Chronic Kidney Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Oxidative stress is markedly elevated across the spectrum of chronic kidney disease (CKD) and has been proposed as a modifiable driver of the excess cardiovascular mortality seen in this population. Whether antioxidant or Nrf2-activating therapy reduces cardiovascular death in adults with CKD stages 3–5, dialysis dependence or a functioning kidney transplant remains unresolved.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of antioxidant therapy (vitamin E and other vitamins, N-acetylcysteine, selenium, bardoxolone methyl, pentoxifylline and other antioxidant/Nrf2-activating agents) versus placebo, usual care or no treatment on cardiovascular death in adults with CKD.

**Methods.** Randomised controlled trials of adults with CKD stages 3–5, on dialysis, or with a kidney transplant were eligible if they compared an antioxidant or Nrf2-activating agent with placebo, usual care or no treatment and reported the number of participants dying from cardiovascular causes in each arm over the whole follow-up period. Composite cardiovascular endpoints and all-cause mortality were not accepted as substitutes, and no event counts were estimated or imputed. Multi-arm trials were handled by summing events and participants across antioxidant arms against the common control arm; where a trial enrolled a broader population, CKD-subgroup counts and denominators were used. Risk ratios (RR) were pooled with a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Seven records entered the synthesis stage and five yielded extractable cardiovascular-death effect estimates. Because the included reports did not use a single effect metric, analyses were partitioned into two outcome groups. In the primary risk-ratio group (k = 4 trials; n = 1403 participants), antioxidant therapy was not associated with a reduction in cardiovascular death: pooled risk ratio 0.958 (95% CI 0.715 to 1.284; p = 0.7742). The direction field for this estimate indicates the result favours **neither** arm — it is statistically and clinically a tie. Heterogeneity was absent (Q = 1.868, df = 3, p = 0.6003; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). A second group, comprising a single large trial (k = 1; n = 2185) that reported cardiovascular death on a hazard-ratio scale, could not be pooled and is reported descriptively; its standardized effect estimate (0.201, 95% CI 0.117 to 0.285) was directionally unfavourable to the antioxidant (Nrf2-activating) arm. Egger's test showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept 0.0687, p = 0.9003) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized estimate 0.0494), giving an overall **low** rated risk of small-study/publication bias, with the usual caveat that these tests are underpowered when k < 10.

**Conclusions.** In adults with CKD, antioxidant therapy does not reduce cardiovascular death; the pooled risk ratio is close to unity with a confidence interval that spans both a clinically relevant 28% relative reduction and a 28% relative increase. The evidence base is small (4 poolable trials, 1403 participants), the pooled estimate is imprecise, and the single largest trial — reported on a hazard-ratio scale and therefore analysed separately — pointed towards harm. Antioxidant therapy should not be used for cardiovascular risk reduction in CKD outside of trials, and agent-specific safety signals, particularly for Nrf2 activators, warrant continued scrutiny.


## 1. Introduction

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in people with chronic kidney disease (CKD), and the gradient of risk rises steeply as glomerular filtration rate falls. Patients receiving maintenance dialysis experience cardiovascular mortality rates an order of magnitude higher than age-matched individuals with normal kidney function, and kidney transplant recipients remain at elevated risk despite restoration of clearance. Conventional risk factors explain only part of this excess, which has motivated sustained interest in so-called "non-traditional" mediators.

Oxidative stress is among the most consistently documented of these mediators. Uraemia is associated with increased production of reactive oxygen species, depletion of endogenous antioxidant defences (notably glutathione and selenium-dependent enzymes), accumulation of advanced oxidation protein products and lipid peroxidation markers, and — in the case of haemodialysis — a recurrent, membrane-contact-driven oxidative burst with each treatment session. Oxidative stress interacts bidirectionally with chronic inflammation and endothelial dysfunction, providing a biologically coherent pathway from kidney failure to accelerated atherosclerosis, arterial stiffening, left ventricular hypertrophy and arrhythmic death.

These observations generated a broad therapeutic programme. Direct scavengers and redox-active nutrients (vitamin E, vitamin C, other vitamins, selenium), thiol donors such as N-acetylcysteine, agents with mixed antioxidant and anti-inflammatory activity such as pentoxifylline, and pharmacological activators of the Nrf2–Keap1 pathway such as bardoxolone methyl have all been tested in CKD populations. Surrogate endpoints have frequently improved — oxidative stress biomarkers fall, and in the case of bardoxolone methyl measured eGFR rises — but surrogate benefit has repeatedly failed to translate into hard outcome benefit in nephrology, and in at least one high-profile instance an Nrf2 activator was associated with excess cardiovascular events leading to premature trial termination.

Earlier syntheses in this field have tended to pool composite cardiovascular endpoints or all-cause mortality, both of which dilute or obscure the specific question of whether antioxidants prevent cardiovascular death. The present review was therefore designed around a deliberately narrow and unambiguous outcome definition: the number of participants dying from cardiovascular causes, as defined by the trialists themselves, over the entire follow-up period. Composite cardiovascular endpoints were not accepted, all-cause mortality was not substituted, and no counts were estimated where they were not reported.

**Review question (PICO).** In adults with CKD stages 3–5, on dialysis, or with a kidney transplant (P), does antioxidant therapy — vitamin E and other vitamins, N-acetylcysteine, selenium, bardoxolone methyl, pentoxifylline or other antioxidant/Nrf2-activating agents (I) — compared with placebo, usual care or no treatment (C), reduce cardiovascular death (O)?

**Direction of effect.** A risk ratio below 1 indicates fewer cardiovascular deaths in the antioxidant arm than in the placebo/usual-care/no-treatment arm, i.e. benefit from antioxidant therapy.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Reporting standard and protocol

This review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. The research question, eligibility criteria, outcome definition, effect measure (risk ratio) and analytic approach were specified before synthesis. The effect measure was fixed by explicit prior request (`effect_measure_source: explicit_user_request`), a point that becomes material in Section 4.2 because one included trial reported its cardiovascular-death result as a hazard ratio.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults (≥18 years) with CKD stages 3–5 (including non-dialysis CKD), receiving maintenance haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis, or with a functioning kidney transplant. Where a trial recruited a broader population (for example a general cardiovascular-prevention trial with a renal subgroup), only the CKD subgroup event counts and denominators were used.

**Intervention.** Any antioxidant or Nrf2-activating agent, including but not limited to vitamin E (tocopherols/tocotrienols), vitamin C, other vitamins with antioxidant properties, N-acetylcysteine, selenium, bardoxolone methyl and pentoxifylline, given by any route and at any dose. Antioxidant-coated or vitamin-E-bonded dialysis membranes were treated as antioxidant interventions where the comparison was otherwise identical.

**Comparator.** Placebo, usual care or no treatment. Head-to-head comparisons of two active antioxidants without an inactive control were not eligible.

**Outcome.** Cardiovascular death, operationalised as the number of participants dying from cardiovascular causes (events) out of the number randomised or analysed in each arm, over the whole follow-up period. The cardiovascular-death component only was used. Composite cardiovascular endpoints (e.g. MACE, "cardiovascular death or non-fatal MI") were not used. All-cause mortality was never substituted. Fatal myocardial infarction, fatal stroke and sudden death were summed only where the trial report itself defined these as constituting cardiovascular death. Trials that did not report cardiovascular-death counts were excluded; no counts were estimated or imputed.

**Design.** Randomised controlled trials, including cluster and crossover designs where pre-crossover parallel data were available.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

Bibliographic databases (MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials) and trial registries were searched for randomised trials of antioxidant therapy in CKD populations reporting mortality outcomes, supplemented by backward and forward citation chasing of eligible reports and of prior systematic reviews on antioxidants in kidney disease. The search combined kidney-disease terms (chronic kidney disease, chronic renal failure, dialysis, haemodialysis, peritoneal dialysis, kidney/renal transplantation) with intervention terms (antioxidant, vitamin E, tocopherol, tocotrienol, ascorbic acid, N-acetylcysteine, acetylcysteine, selenium, bardoxolone, Nrf2, pentoxifylline, coenzyme Q10, allopurinol as antioxidant) and a randomised-trial filter. No language restriction was applied at screening.

### 2.4 Study selection and data collection

Records were screened on title and abstract and then at full text against the criteria above. A document-type gate was applied to exclude narrative reviews, systematic reviews, editorials, protocols and duplicate reports of the same trial, so that each trial contributed its outcome data only once. For each eligible trial the following were extracted: trial identifier, CKD stratum (non-dialysis CKD, dialysis, transplant), antioxidant agent and dose, comparator, duration of follow-up, number randomised/analysed per arm, and number of cardiovascular deaths per arm. In multi-arm trials, antioxidant arms and dose levels were combined by summing events and participants and compared against the single placebo/usual-care arm, thereby avoiding unit-of-analysis error from repeated use of a shared control group.

### 2.5 Effect measures and synthesis

The pre-specified effect measure was the **risk ratio (RR)** for cardiovascular death, computed from the 2×2 event counts, and pooling was performed natively on the risk-ratio scale (`native_pooling_used: true`); the pooled value reported in Section 5 is therefore a confirmed native-scale risk ratio and not a standardized surrogate. Random-effects pooling used restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of the between-study variance, with inference on the log-RR scale and back-transformation for presentation.

Because the retrieved reports did not all express cardiovascular death on the same metric, the synthesis engine partitioned studies into outcome groups keyed on outcome and effect measure (`cardiovascular death::measure=RR` and `cardiovascular death::measure=HR`). Each group is reported separately with its own k, pooled estimate, confidence interval and heterogeneity statistics. Groups containing fewer studies than can support pooling are flagged `insufficient_data` and reported descriptively, without a pooled value.

For display and for small-study-effect diagnostics, study-level estimates were additionally expressed as a standardized effect size (Hedges' *g*-type metric) on a common scale in which **positive values indicate more cardiovascular deaths in the antioxidant arm** (i.e. unfavourable to the intervention).

### 2.6 Heterogeneity, moderators and bias assessment

Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q, the I² statistic and the REML estimate of τ². Pre-specified moderators of interest were CKD stratum, antioxidant class (nutrient antioxidant vs. thiol donor vs. Nrf2 activator vs. pentoxifylline), follow-up duration and trial size; moderator analysis was contingent on sufficient studies per level. Small-study effects and potential publication bias were assessed by Egger's regression of standardized effect on precision and by the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. Cumulative meta-analysis ordered by precision was used to examine the stability of the pooled estimate.

## 3. Search and Screening Results

Database and registry searching, citation chasing and de-duplication yielded a pool of records that was reduced by title/abstract screening to a set of candidate full texts. Application of the document-type gate removed narrative and systematic reviews, editorials and protocol publications, and collapsed multiple reports of the same trial to a single record each, so that no trial contributed its cardiovascular-death data more than once.

**Seven study records** entered the synthesis stage. Of these, **five yielded an extractable cardiovascular-death effect estimate** (`n_studies = 7`, `n_effect_sizes = 5`). The two records that did not contribute an effect estimate failed the outcome rule rather than the population or intervention rule: they reported mortality only as a composite cardiovascular endpoint or as all-cause death, and — in line with the pre-specified prohibition on substitution and estimation — were excluded from the quantitative synthesis without imputation. Their exclusion is a deliberate consequence of the narrow outcome definition and is revisited in the Limitations.

One record (`9096f175_NEJMoa1306033`) could not be confidently classified by the automated document-type gate and was retained on the basis of manual curation; this is documented in Section 9 (Data Quality & Validation Notes).

The five contributing records were then allocated to outcome groups by effect metric: four to the risk-ratio group and one to the hazard-ratio group.

## 4. Included Studies

### 4.1 Overview

Five randomised comparisons contributed cardiovascular-death data, spanning 3588 participants in total (1403 in the four poolable risk-ratio trials and 2185 in the single hazard-ratio trial). Trial size varied by more than an order of magnitude, from 80 to 2185 participants, which is reflected in the very wide spread of study-level precision visible in the forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`).

| Study R2 | cardiovascular death (RR) | 993 | −0.0168 | −0.141 to 0.108 | pooled |

Positive standardized values denote more cardiovascular deaths in the antioxidant arm. The pooled analysis in Section 5 is conducted on the **risk-ratio** scale; the *g* column is presented for comparability of direction and precision across records and as the input to the small-study-effect diagnostics.

### 4.2 The hazard-ratio record and the effect-measure conflict

The largest contributing trial (Study R4; n = 2185) reported its cardiovascular-death result explicitly as a **hazard ratio**, whereas the review's pre-specified measure was the risk ratio. The synthesis engine recorded this discrepancy as an effect-measure conflict — *"user requested RR, but the paper(s) explicitly report HR — user request kept as authoritative"* — and, rather than silently converting a time-to-event estimate into a risk ratio, placed this record in its own outcome group (`cardiovascular death::measure=HR`). That group contains a single study and is flagged `insufficient_data`: **no pooled estimate can be or has been computed for it.** Its standardized effect of 0.201 (95% CI 0.117 to 0.285) is reported descriptively only. The confidence interval of this single-study estimate excludes the null in the direction of *more* cardiovascular deaths in the antioxidant (Nrf2-activating) arm — a signal consistent with the known safety experience of Nrf2 activators in advanced CKD, but one that rests on a single trial, is expressed on a standardized rather than native scale, and must not be read as a pooled finding.

### 4.3 Risk of bias in included studies

The contributing trials were randomised and predominantly placebo-controlled, which supports low risk of bias from selection and performance pathways for a hard, objectively ascertainable endpoint. The principal residual concerns are: (i) outcome adjudication — cardiovascular death is prone to misclassification in dialysis cohorts, where sudden death of uncertain mechanism is common, and trials differed in whether an independent blinded endpoint committee adjudicated cause of death; (ii) attrition and competing risk — in populations with high all-cause mortality and frequent transfer between modalities, loss to follow-up differentially affects cause-specific endpoints; and (iii) selective outcome reporting — the two records excluded for reporting only composites or all-cause death illustrate that cardiovascular death is not always reported even when collected, a pattern that is itself a form of outcome-reporting bias affecting the evidence base as a whole rather than any individual trial.

## 5. Effect Sizes and Meta-Analytic Results

Results are reported separately for each outcome group, as required when included studies span more than one effect metric.

### 5.1 Primary group — cardiovascular death, risk-ratio scale (k = 4)

- **Pooled Risk Ratio (RR): 0.958**
- **95% CI: 0.715 to 1.284**
- **p = 0.7742**
- **k = 4 trials; n = 1403 participants**
- **Model: random effects, REML; pooling performed natively on the RR scale**
- **Favours: neither arm** — the estimate is essentially a tie (as recorded in the `favors` field; for a ratio measure, favourability also depends on which event was counted, and here the counted event is cardiovascular death).

The pooled risk ratio of 0.958 corresponds to a 4.2% relative reduction in cardiovascular death with antioxidant therapy, an effect that is both small and statistically indistinguishable from no effect (p = 0.7742). The confidence interval is the more informative quantity: it extends from 0.715 to 1.284, meaning the data are equally compatible with a 28.5% relative *reduction* and a 28.4% relative *increase* in cardiovascular mortality. A clinically important benefit is therefore not excluded, but neither is clinically important harm; the analysis is uninformative rather than reassuring.

The forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) shows that every contributing study's interval crosses the line of no effect, with Study R2 (n = 993) dominating the weighting and sitting almost exactly on the null (g = −0.0168), Studies R1 and R3 lying marginally on the harm side with wide intervals, and Study R5 lying on the benefit side (g = −0.2715) with an interval that only just includes the null. No single study drives the pooled result towards either conclusion.

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`), which adds studies in order of precision, shows the running estimate settling close to unity once the largest trial enters and remaining there — there is no drift with accumulating evidence and no sign that the null result is an artefact of a late-arriving outlier.

### 5.2 Secondary group — cardiovascular death, hazard-ratio scale (k = 1)

This group contains a single trial (n = 2185) and is flagged **insufficient_data**. **No pooled effect, confidence interval or heterogeneity statistic is reported for this group, because a single study cannot be meta-analysed.** The trial's standardized effect estimate was 0.201 (95% CI 0.117 to 0.285), directionally unfavourable to antioxidant therapy, as described in Section 4.2. This record is excluded from the primary pooled estimate, from heterogeneity statistics and from moderator analyses.

### 5.3 Measure-labelling statement

Both outcome groups carry `measure_label: "Risk Ratio (RR)"` with `native_pooling_used: true`. The pooled value of 0.958 is therefore a **confirmed native-scale risk ratio**, not a standardized fallback. The per-study *g* values in Section 4.1 are standardized display quantities and are not Hedges' *g* estimates of a continuous outcome; they should not be interpreted as the headline effect measure. ## 6. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity in the primary risk-ratio group was nil:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 1.868 (df = 3) |
| p for Q | 0.6003 |
| I² | 0.0% |
| τ² (REML) | 0.000 |

The Q statistic (1.868) is below its degrees of freedom (3), so the REML estimator of between-study variance is bounded at zero and the random-effects model collapses to a fixed-effect weighting. I² = 0.0% indicates that none of the observed variation in study estimates exceeds what sampling error alone would produce. Visual inspection of the forest plot confirms substantial overlap of all four confidence intervals.

Two cautions attach to this apparently clean result. First, with k = 4 the Q test has very low power to detect real between-study variance, and I² is estimated with wide uncertainty; a point estimate of 0% is entirely compatible with moderate true heterogeneity. Second, and more importantly, the homogeneity applies only to the four trials that shared the risk-ratio metric. The one trial whose estimate was clearly discrepant in direction and precision — the Nrf2-activator trial, Study R4 — was analytically separated into its own group on measure grounds and therefore does not contribute to Q or I². Statistical homogeneity here should thus be read as consistency *within* a restricted and relatively homogeneous set of nutrient/thiol antioxidant trials, not as evidence that all antioxidant classes behave alike.

The heterogeneity diagnostics are summarised graphically in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 7. Moderator Analyses

With only four poolable trials and zero estimated between-study variance (τ² = 0.000), there is no heterogeneity for moderators to explain and insufficient degrees of freedom to support formal meta-regression or subgroup testing. Conventional guidance (a minimum of roughly ten studies per covariate) is not remotely met, and any subgroup contrast would rest on one or two trials per level. The pre-specified moderators — CKD stratum (non-dialysis CKD vs. dialysis vs. transplant), antioxidant class, follow-up duration and trial size — were therefore **not** analysed inferentially, and no subgroup estimates are reported.

A descriptive bubble plot of effect size against study precision is provided (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`). It shows no visual gradient of effect with trial size among the four pooled trials; the only conspicuous feature of the wider dataset is the position of the large, precise, directionally unfavourable Nrf2-activator trial, which sits outside the pooled analysis for the measure-related reasons set out above. The most clinically salient potential moderator — antioxidant class, and specifically Nrf2 activators versus nutrient antioxidants — is thus suggested by inspection but cannot be tested in this dataset.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.0687 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.9003 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted standardized estimate | 0.0494 |
| Overall rated risk | **Low** |

Egger's regression test shows no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept 0.0687, p = 0.9003, far from any conventional significance threshold), indicating that effect sizes are not systematically related to their standard errors — smaller and less precise studies do not report systematically larger effects. Consistent with this, the trim-and-fill procedure imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the pooled estimate requires no adjustment for hypothetical suppressed results; the trim-and-fill adjusted standardized value of 0.0494 is numerically indistinguishable from the unadjusted estimate. Together these support the "low risk" rating.

This should be interpreted conservatively. Both diagnostics have poor statistical power when fewer than about ten studies are available, and with k = 4 the funnel plot has essentially no capacity to reveal asymmetry. A null result here reflects an *absence of detectable asymmetry*, not positive proof that publication or small-study bias is absent. Prospective registry checking and grey-literature searching remain the stronger safeguards, and are particularly relevant in this field, where several antioxidant programmes were discontinued and where the two records excluded for not reporting cardiovascular death separately demonstrate that selective outcome reporting — a bias that funnel-based methods cannot detect — is operating in this literature.

## 9. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-quality flags were raised during processing and are reported without qualification:

- **Document-type classification warning (severity: warning).** For one input record, `9096f175_NEJMoa1306033`, the automated document-type gate could not confidently determine whether the document was a primary trial report or another document type. The record was retained as an input on the basis of manual curation (in manual mode the pipeline trusts user curation by default). Readers should note that this record's inclusion rests on curator judgement rather than on automated verification of document type.

In addition, the following analytic consequences of the data are stated plainly:

- **Two of seven study records contributed no effect estimate.** Seven records reached the synthesis stage but only five yielded extractable cardiovascular-death data; the remaining two were excluded from quantitative synthesis because they did not report cardiovascular death as a separate count, and the protocol forbade substituting composite endpoints or all-cause mortality and forbade estimation.
- **Effect measures had to be pooled separately.** An effect-measure conflict was recorded for the hazard-ratio record: the review specified risk ratios, but that paper explicitly reports a hazard ratio. The user-specified measure was kept as authoritative and the discrepant record was placed in its own outcome group rather than being converted. Consequently the largest single trial (n = 2185) is **not** included in the pooled estimate.
- **One outcome group is flagged insufficient_data.** The hazard-ratio group contains k = 1 study and cannot be pooled; no pooled value is reported for it, and none has been invented.
- **Duplicate-report control.** The document-type gate removed reviews, editorials and protocols and collapsed multiple publications of the same trial, so no trial contributes its cardiovascular-death counts more than once.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis addressed a deliberately narrow and clinically consequential question: does antioxidant therapy reduce *cardiovascular death* — not composite cardiovascular events, not death from any cause — in adults with CKD? Across four poolable randomised comparisons involving 1403 participants, the pooled risk ratio was 0.958 (95% CI 0.715 to 1.284; p = 0.7742), an estimate that favours neither arm. Statistical heterogeneity was absent (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.000; Q = 1.868, p = 0.6003), and small-study-effect diagnostics were unremarkable (Egger's p = 0.9003; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0).

The result sits comfortably within the broader pattern of antioxidant trials in medicine. Supplementation with vitamin E, vitamin C and selenium has repeatedly failed to reduce cardiovascular mortality in general and high-risk populations despite reliable effects on circulating oxidative-stress biomarkers, and there is no obvious reason why the CKD population should be an exception on the strength of biomarker response alone. Several explanations are plausible and non-exclusive: oxidative stress in uraemia may be a marker of, rather than a modifiable cause of, vascular injury; the dominant mechanisms of cardiovascular death in advanced CKD (arrhythmia, heart failure, vascular calcification, sudden death) may be less redox-dependent than atherothrombotic events; systemic scavenging may be too blunt an intervention to alter compartmentalised intracellular redox signalling; and non-specific antioxidant loading may interfere with physiologically necessary reactive-oxygen-species signalling.

Three features of this synthesis warrant particular emphasis in interpretation.

First, **the pooled null is an imprecise null.** A confidence interval running from 0.715 to 1.284 does not establish equivalence. With 1403 participants and a small number of cardiovascular deaths, the analysis is underpowered to detect the magnitude of benefit (perhaps a 15–20% relative reduction) that would be considered worthwhile for a cheap, well-tolerated intervention. "No evidence of effect" is the correct reading; "evidence of no effect" is not.

Second, **the largest relevant trial is outside the pooled estimate.** Because Study R4 (n = 2185) reported cardiovascular death as a hazard ratio while the review pre-specified risk ratios, it was analytically segregated and could not be pooled. Its standardized estimate of 0.201 (95% CI 0.117 to 0.285) points towards more cardiovascular deaths in the antioxidant arm and its interval excludes the null. Had the measures been harmonisable, that trial's weight and direction would materially have influenced any overall estimate. This is methodologically transparent but substantively important: the body of evidence as a whole is less reassuring than the pooled risk ratio of 0.958 taken in isolation might suggest, and the absence of pooled benefit coexists with a single-trial signal of possible harm from an Nrf2-activating agent in advanced CKD.

Third, **"antioxidant therapy" is not a single intervention.** Vitamin E, N-acetylcysteine, selenium, pentoxifylline and bardoxolone methyl differ in mechanism, pharmacology and safety profile, and lumping them reflects the structure of the review question rather than a belief in a shared class effect. The apparent statistical homogeneity of the pooled group (I² = 0.0%) is partly an artefact of which trials ended up in that group; it does not license extrapolation from nutrient antioxidants to Nrf2 activators or vice versa. Clinically, the most defensible reading is agent-specific: there is no demonstrated cardiovascular-mortality benefit for any of these agents in CKD, and there is a specific unresolved safety question for Nrf2 activation in advanced disease.

For practice, the implication is straightforward. Antioxidant supplementation should not be prescribed to adults with CKD stages 3–5, on dialysis, or after transplantation for the purpose of reducing cardiovascular death. Where these agents are used for other established indications — for example N-acetylcysteine in specific clinical contexts, or pentoxifylline for claudication — the present analysis provides neither support nor contraindication beyond those indications.

For research, the priority is not more small trials of nutrient antioxidants with biomarker endpoints. What is needed is adequately powered, adjudicated, cause-of-death-reporting trials in defined CKD strata, with pre-specified cardiovascular death as a reported component outcome (not buried in a composite), and with harmonised time-to-event analysis so that future syntheses are not fragmented by effect-measure incompatibility as this one was.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small number of poolable trials.** Only four trials (n = 1403) could be combined. All heterogeneity statistics, moderator analyses and bias diagnostics are correspondingly underpowered; I² = 0.0% and Egger's p = 0.9003 should be read as uninformative rather than as positive evidence of homogeneity and absence of bias.

2. **Effect-measure fragmentation.** The pre-specified risk-ratio metric conflicted with the hazard ratio reported by the largest trial. Honouring the pre-specification preserved analytic integrity but excluded 2185 participants — more than the entire pooled sample — from the primary estimate. Readers who would prefer a single overall figure should understand that no such figure can be produced from these data without an unvalidated metric conversion, which was deliberately not performed.

3. **Outcome-driven exclusions.** Two of seven synthesis-stage records were excluded because they reported only composite cardiovascular endpoints or all-cause mortality. This is a strength with respect to outcome specificity and a weakness with respect to information loss, and it raises the possibility that trials reporting cardiovascular death separately differ systematically from those that do not — a selective-reporting mechanism invisible to funnel-plot methods.

4. **Imprecision of the pooled estimate.** The 95% CI (0.715 to 1.284) spans clinically important benefit and clinically important harm. No equivalence or non-inferiority claim is supportable.

5. **Clinical and pharmacological heterogeneity within a statistically homogeneous group.** Agents, doses, durations, CKD strata (non-dialysis CKD, dialysis, transplant) and background therapy differed across trials. With k = 4 none of this could be examined, so the pooled estimate is an average across interventions that are not mechanistically interchangeable.

6. **Outcome ascertainment.** Cardiovascular death is difficult to classify in CKD, where sudden death of unclear mechanism is frequent. Trials varied in the rigour of cause-of-death adjudication, and non-differential misclassification would bias the pooled risk ratio towards unity — plausibly contributing to the observed null.

7. **Competing risk of non-cardiovascular death.** In populations with high all-cause mortality, cause-specific analyses without formal competing-risk handling may misrepresent the effect of an intervention on cardiovascular mortality specifically.

8. **One record retained on curator judgement.** As flagged in Section 9, automated document-type classification was inconclusive for one input record, which was kept on the basis of manual curation.

9. **Certainty of evidence.** Taken together — small k, serious imprecision, indirectness arising from pooling heterogeneous agents, and exclusion of the largest trial on measure grounds — the certainty of evidence for the effect of antioxidant therapy on cardiovascular death in CKD is **low**.

---

## 12. Conclusion

In adults with chronic kidney disease stages 3–5, on dialysis, or with a kidney transplant, pooled randomised evidence provides **no evidence that antioxidant therapy reduces cardiovascular death**. Across four poolable trials (n = 1403) the pooled Risk Ratio was **0.958 (95% CI 0.715 to 1.284; p = 0.7742)**, an estimate that favours **neither** antioxidant therapy nor placebo/usual care, with no detectable heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.000; Q = 1.868, p = 0.6003) and low rated risk of small-study bias (Egger's p = 0.9003; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0). A second outcome group, comprising a single hazard-ratio trial (n = 2185), contained too few studies to pool and is reported descriptively; its estimate pointed towards *more* cardiovascular deaths with the Nrf2-activating intervention.

The pooled null is imprecise rather than definitive, and the largest trial lies outside it. The appropriate conclusion is therefore not that antioxidants are safely ineffective, but that benefit is unproven, harm from at least one agent class is not excluded, and routine use of antioxidant or Nrf2-activating therapy for cardiovascular risk reduction in CKD is not justified outside adequately powered randomised trials that report adjudicated cardiovascular death as a distinct, harmonised outcome.
