# Autoinflation versus watchful waiting for persistent middle ear effusion in children with otitis media with effusion: a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of short-term (≤6 weeks) outcomes

## Abstract

**Background.** Otitis media with effusion (OME, "glue ear") is the commonest cause of acquired hearing impairment in childhood. Autoinflation — Valsalva-type insufflation of the middle ear through the nose using a balloon, a carnival blower or a commercial device — is a low-cost, non-surgical, self-administered option that is frequently offered while awaiting spontaneous resolution. Its short-term efficacy relative to watchful waiting remains contested.

**Objective.** To determine whether, in children with OME, autoinflation compared with watchful waiting/no treatment reduces the proportion with persistent middle ear effusion at the first follow-up at or before 6 weeks.

**Methods.** Systematic search and structured screening identified randomised comparisons of autoinflation versus watchful waiting/no treatment/standard care in children with OME. The outcome was the number of children (or ears) with persistent effusion — flat (type B) tympanogram, type B or C2 tympanogram, or effusion visible on pneumatic otoscopy — at the follow-up closest to 4 weeks within a 2–6 week window (latest time point if several were reported). Where only per-ear data were available for bilateral disease, events and totals were divided by the design effect 1 + (average ears per child − 1) × 0.5. Effects were pooled on the native risk-ratio scale with a random-effects (REML) model; heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ²; publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Seven trials contributing seven effect estimates and 625 randomised children were pooled. The pooled **Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.791 (95% CI 0.571 to 1.096; p = 0.1582; k = 7; n = 625)**, computed natively on the RR scale (native pooling confirmed). On the direction field carried with the analysis, this result **favours neither arm** — it is effectively a statistical tie, with a confidence interval spanning both a clinically worthwhile ~43% relative reduction in persistent effusion and a ~10% relative increase. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial to considerable (Q = 26.163, df = 6, p = 0.0002; I² = 89.42%; τ² = 0.155731), and the individual standardized study estimates ranged from g = −2.088 to g = +0.677 with several non-overlapping confidence intervals. Publication-bias diagnostics were reassuring: Egger's intercept −0.0692 (p = 0.8186) and trim-and-fill imputing zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), giving an unchanged adjusted estimate (−0.2793 on the standardized scale).

**Conclusions.** At the first follow-up at or before 6 weeks, pooled evidence does not establish a benefit of autoinflation over watchful waiting for clearing middle ear effusion in children: the point estimate is compatible with a moderate reduction in persistence, but the confidence interval includes no effect and the result is not statistically significant. Extreme heterogeneity (I² = 89.42%) — spanning both directions of effect — means the single pooled number should not be read as a stable summary of a homogeneous treatment effect. Device type, age, adherence and outcome ascertainment are plausible but untested sources of this inconsistency. Larger, adequately powered trials with standardised devices, verified adherence and child-level tympanometric outcomes are needed.


## Introduction

Otitis media with effusion (OME) is defined by the presence of fluid in the middle ear cleft without the acute signs and symptoms of infection. It is extremely common: the majority of children experience at least one episode before school age, and a substantial minority develop persistent, bilateral disease with conductive hearing loss, which has been linked to difficulties with listening in noise, speech and language development, behaviour and school performance. Because most episodes resolve spontaneously within approximately three months, a period of watchful waiting is recommended by major guidelines before considering ventilation tube insertion or adenoidectomy.

This "watchful waiting" interval creates a therapeutic vacuum that clinicians and families are often reluctant to accept, and it has motivated interest in simple, non-surgical, non-pharmacological adjuncts. **Autoinflation** — the forced insufflation of air through the nose into the Eustachian tube and middle ear, performed by inflating a purpose-made nasal balloon, blowing a carnival blower, or using a commercial autoinflation device — is the best known of these. It is inexpensive, has no known serious adverse effects, can be taught in a single consultation, and is physiologically plausible: raising nasopharyngeal pressure may open a functionally obstructed Eustachian tube, equalise middle ear pressure, and promote drainage and ventilation of the middle ear cleft.

The evidence base, however, is fragmented. Trials have used different devices, different age ranges, different durations of pre-randomisation effusion, different intensities of supervised practice, and — critically — different outcome definitions and follow-up schedules, ranging from a few weeks to a year. Earlier syntheses have concluded that autoinflation may be beneficial but that the evidence is of low quality and short-term. The present meta-analysis addresses one narrowly specified and clinically meaningful question:

> In children with OME, does autoinflation, compared with watchful waiting or no treatment, reduce the proportion with **persistent middle ear effusion at the first follow-up at or before 6 weeks**?

Restricting attention to a single early time window avoids the statistical hazard of pooling heterogeneous follow-up points and isolates the question most relevant to a clinician deciding what, if anything, to offer at the index consultation.

---

## Search and Screening

A systematic search of the electronic literature was performed for randomised and quasi-randomised controlled trials evaluating autoinflation in children with OME, supplemented by examination of the reference lists of retrieved reports and prior systematic reviews. Records were screened in two stages — title/abstract, then full text — against pre-specified eligibility criteria:

**Inclusion criteria**
- **Population:** children (paediatric populations) with a clinical or tympanometric diagnosis of OME.
- **Intervention:** autoinflation by any Valsalva-type nasal route — nasal balloon (e.g. Otovent-type), carnival blower, or commercial autoinflation device.
- **Comparator:** watchful waiting, no treatment, or standard care alone (no active middle ear intervention).
- **Outcome:** number of children (or ears) with persistent middle ear effusion at a follow-up at or before 6 weeks, with a denominator equal to the number randomised in each arm.
- **Design:** randomised or quasi-randomised parallel-group or within-patient comparison.

**Exclusion criteria** comprised narrative and systematic reviews, editorials and commentaries; studies in adults only; trials of autoinflation versus an active comparator without a no-treatment arm; studies reporting no outcome within the 2–6 week window; and duplicate or companion reports of an already-included trial (retained once, to avoid double-counting participants).

After screening and data extraction, **seven eligible trials contributing seven independent effect estimates and 625 randomised children** entered the quantitative synthesis. The analysis pipeline's internal study identifiers run R1–R4 and R6–R8; the absence of an R5 among the extracted effect estimates reflects a record that was indexed during screening but did not survive to the analysis stage (i.e. it was excluded or could not be converted into an eligible effect estimate) and it therefore contributes no data to any pooled figure reported below. No formal automated validation flags were returned by the extraction pipeline for this analysis.

The PRISMA 2020 reporting framework was followed throughout.

## Effect Sizes

The primary outcome is binary (persistent effusion: yes/no), and the pre-specified effect measure for pooling was therefore the **risk ratio (RR)**, with RR < 1 corresponding to less persistent effusion in the autoinflation arm. The analysis confirms that pooling was carried out **natively on the RR scale** (`native_pooling_used = true`; effect-measure source: explicit user request; computation scale: RR), so the pooled summary below is a genuine native-scale risk ratio and not a back-transformed or standardized surrogate.

Study-level estimates are additionally displayed as Hedges' g (a standardized mean-difference-equivalent transformation) in the table above and in the forest plot, because that is the common metric in which the pipeline carried the individual studies. Two features of the study-level estimates deserve explicit comment:

1. **The individual g values span both directions and are mutually inconsistent.** Four estimates are negative (study_1_R1, study_4_R4, study_6_R6, study_8_R8) and three are positive (study_2_R2, study_3_R3, study_7_R7). Three of the negative estimates and one positive estimate exclude zero, and the confidence intervals of study_8_R8 (−2.088, 95% CI −2.814 to −1.362) and study_7_R7 (+0.677, 95% CI +0.220 to +1.134) do not overlap at all. This is direct, model-free evidence of genuine disagreement among the trials.
2. **Sign conventions differ between the standardized study-level column and the native pooled RR.** The study-level standardized values retain the raw statistical convention (treatment minus control) in which the pipeline computed them, and their arithmetic average is negative, whereas the pooled native risk ratio is below 1. The two scales therefore do **not** map onto one another in a transparent way in this dataset, and readers should not attempt to infer direction of benefit from the sign of an individual g. For that reason, directionality in this report is taken exclusively from the analysis's own `favors` field, as described next, rather than inferred from the arithmetic sign of any pooled or individual statistic.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A single pre-specified outcome/time-point group was analysed (there were no multiple `meta_analysis_groups`), so one pooled estimate is reported. Pooling used a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of the between-study variance.

The pooled risk ratio of 0.791 corresponds, taken at face value, to roughly a 21% relative reduction in the proportion of children with persistent effusion at ≤6 weeks. However, **this estimate is not statistically significant (p = 0.1582) and its 95% confidence interval (0.571 to 1.096) includes the null value of 1**, so it is equally compatible with a clinically important 43% relative reduction and with a 10% relative *increase* in persistence. In accordance with the direction field carried with the analysis, the result **favours neither autoinflation nor watchful waiting**; it is best described as an inconclusive, essentially tied comparison rather than as positive or negative evidence. The caveat embedded in that field — that for a ratio measure, favourability depends on which event was counted — is pertinent here given the mixed signs of the study-level standardized estimates noted above, and reinforces that the direction of the pooled estimate should not be over-interpreted.

Because τ² is large, the confidence interval around the *mean* effect understates the range of effects a new trial might observe. An approximate 95% prediction interval, computed from the pooled log-RR, its standard error and τ² with a t-multiplier on 5 degrees of freedom, spans roughly **RR 0.26 to RR 2.4** — that is, a future trial of comparable design could plausibly observe either a large benefit or a substantial harm. This is the single most important quantitative statement in the analysis.

## Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity was **substantial to considerable and statistically unambiguous**:

- **Cochran's Q = 26.163 on 6 degrees of freedom, p = 0.0002** — the null hypothesis of a common underlying effect is decisively rejected.
- **I² = 89.42%** — approximately nine-tenths of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to real between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. Under the Cochrane Handbook's rubric, values of 75–100% indicate "considerable" heterogeneity.
- **τ² = 0.155731**, i.e. a between-study standard deviation of about 0.395 on the log-RR scale. For a pooled effect whose log-RR is only about −0.23 in magnitude, the between-study spread is therefore larger than the average effect itself.

This degree of inconsistency is not a technical nuisance; it is a substantive finding. It means the seven trials are not estimating the same quantity. Inspection of the forest plot shows that the disagreement is qualitative (sign-discordant), not merely quantitative: at least one trial reports a strong effect in one direction with a confidence interval that excludes the estimate of another trial entirely. Where heterogeneity is this large and directionally mixed, the random-effects mean is a poor summary of the evidence, and the prediction interval and the individual study estimates deserve at least as much weight in interpretation as the pooled point estimate. A graphical summary of the heterogeneity statistics is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

No single study is responsible for the heterogeneity: removing the most extreme estimate (study_8_R8, g = −2.088) would reduce but not abolish the inconsistency, because sign-discordance persists among the remaining six trials (e.g. study_7_R7 at +0.677 versus study_6_R6 at −0.567).

---

## Moderators

Given the magnitude of the unexplained heterogeneity, exploration of moderators was a priority. However, **no formal moderator analysis or meta-regression could be estimated** in this dataset. With k = 7 studies, the conventional minimum of approximately 10 studies per covariate for meta-regression is not met, and any single-covariate model would be severely overfitted and uninterpretable; no coded study-level covariates (device type, mean age, duration of effusion prior to randomisation, supervised versus unsupervised practice, adherence, tympanometry versus otoscopy for outcome ascertainment, exact week of follow-up within the 2–6 week window) were available in the analysis dataset for formal testing. The relationship between study size and effect magnitude is displayed descriptively in the bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, in which marker area is proportional to study weight.

Informally, and with the explicit caveat that these are hypothesis-generating observations rather than tested effects:

- There is no clean monotonic relationship between sample size and effect direction. The largest trial (n = 263) and the second largest (n = 108) both yield negative standardized estimates, as do study_6_R6 (n = 73) and study_8_R8 (n = 45); the three positive estimates come from trials of n = 18, 40 and 78. Effect direction is therefore not simply a function of trial size.
- The most extreme estimate in the set (study_8_R8, g = −2.088, n = 45) is from a small trial, which is the configuration most vulnerable to small-study effects, chance imbalance and outcome-definition artefacts.
- Differences in device (balloon vs carnival blower vs commercial device), in the intensity and supervision of use, and in whether persistence was defined as type B only or type B/C2, are all biologically and methodologically plausible explanations for the observed inconsistency, and all remain untested here.

**These moderators should be regarded as unresolved.** Attributing the heterogeneity to any of them on the basis of the present data would be speculation.

---

## Publication Bias

Small-study and publication-bias diagnostics were performed on the standardized study-level estimates:

- **Egger's regression test:** intercept = −0.0692, **p = 0.8186**. There is no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry; the intercept is close to zero and the p-value is far from conventional significance.
- **Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill:** **k₀ = 0 studies imputed**. The procedure identified no missing studies on either side of the funnel, so the bias-adjusted estimate (−0.2793 on the standardized scale) is numerically identical to the unadjusted standardized estimate, and the funnel is already symmetric.
- **Overall risk classification: low.**

Interpretation (as carried with the analysis): Egger's regression showed no evidence of funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.8186), indicating no statistical signal of small-study effects; consistently, trim-and-fill imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), meaning the adjusted pooled effect is identical to the original estimate. Together these converge on a **low risk of publication bias**, so the meta-analytic estimate is unlikely to be materially inflated by selective reporting.

Two important qualifications apply. First, both tests have **very limited statistical power when k < 10**, as here (k = 7); a "negative" Egger test in seven studies is weak reassurance, not a clean bill of health. Second, neither test can detect bias that affects an entire literature uniformly — for example if whole bodies of null evidence on autoinflation were never written up. Third, in the presence of I² = 89.42%, funnel-plot asymmetry tests are known to behave unreliably, because genuine heterogeneity can both mask and mimic asymmetry. Note also that these diagnostics were computed on the standardized scale, whereas the primary pooled estimate is a native risk ratio; the trim-and-fill value (−0.2793) is therefore not directly comparable with RR 0.791 and should not be read as an adjusted risk ratio.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis of seven randomised comparisons and 625 children found a pooled risk ratio of **0.791 (95% CI 0.571 to 1.096, p = 0.1582)** for persistent middle ear effusion at the first follow-up at or before six weeks with autoinflation versus watchful waiting. On the direction field accompanying the analysis, the comparison **favours neither arm**. The honest summary is that short-term autoinflation has **not been shown** to clear middle ear effusion faster than watchful waiting, while also not having been shown to be useless: the point estimate is in the direction of a moderate benefit, but the interval includes no effect, and the evidence is too inconsistent to support a confident claim either way.

Three aspects of the result deserve emphasis.

**First, the inconsistency dominates.** With I² = 89.42%, τ² = 0.155731 and Q p = 0.0002, the trials disagree qualitatively — some report markedly less persistent effusion with autoinflation, others markedly more. The approximate 95% prediction interval (≈0.26 to 2.4) illustrates that a new, well-conducted trial could land almost anywhere. Reporting only "RR 0.79" from this evidence base would misrepresent its reliability.

**Second, mechanism and adherence plausibly explain part of the divergence.** Autoinflation is an effort-dependent intervention. Young children may be unable to generate or sustain the required nasopharyngeal pressure; older or coached children may do so reliably. Trials that supervised technique, required demonstrated competence before randomisation, or verified use (e.g. balloon-inflation diaries) might therefore be expected to show larger effects than trials that handed over a device with brief instruction. Likewise, children with longstanding, thick ("glue") effusions may be physiologically less responsive to insufflation than those with recent, serous effusions and negative middle ear pressure. These remain hypotheses: with seven trials, none could be tested.

**Third, outcome definition is not neutral.** Classifying persistence as "type B only" versus "type B or C2" changes the event count substantially, and a true mechanistic effect of autoinflation — normalising middle ear pressure — would be expected to move children from C2 to A/C1 before frank fluid resolves. A trial using the broader B-or-C2 definition could thus record an apparent benefit that a B-only trial would not, independently of any difference in actual fluid clearance. Where per-ear data had to be converted to child-level data via the design-effect divisor, a further layer of approximation was introduced. Together, these measurement differences could generate a meaningful share of the observed heterogeneity.

Clinically, the implications are modest but real. Autoinflation is cheap, non-invasive, free of serious adverse effects, and can be offered during the recommended watchful-waiting interval at essentially no risk. Nothing in this analysis argues against a trial of autoinflation in a motivated, suitably aged child; equally, nothing in it supports promising families that autoinflation will clear the effusion within six weeks, nor does it justify deferring audiological assessment or specialist referral on the expectation of early resolution. Outcomes beyond six weeks, hearing thresholds, quality of life and eventual need for ventilation tubes were outside the scope of this question and are not addressed by these results; a short-term null for effusion persistence does not exclude benefit on those endpoints.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

- **No automated validation flags** were returned by the extraction and analysis pipeline for this dataset.
- **One indexed record did not reach analysis.** The pipeline's study identifiers run R1–R4 and R6–R8; no effect estimate was extracted for the R5 slot. That record therefore contributes nothing to the pooled estimates. This is stated for transparency rather than inferred as any particular category of exclusion.
- **Per-ear to per-child conversion was applied where required.** For trials reporting ears rather than children in bilateral disease, both events and denominators were divided by the design effect 1 + (average ears per child − 1) × 0.5. This is a pre-specified approximation; it alters the effective sample size and hence trial weights relative to the raw published figures.
- **Derived persistence counts.** Where trials reported resolution or improvement, persistence was derived as total minus resolved. This assumes complete outcome ascertainment in the reported denominator and may misclassify losses to follow-up.
- **Scale mismatch between pooled estimate and bias diagnostics.** The primary pooled estimate is a native risk ratio (RR 0.791); Egger's test and trim-and-fill were computed on the standardized study-level scale (trim-and-fill adjusted value −0.2793). These values are on different scales and are not interconvertible here.
- **Discordant sign conventions at study level.** The standardized study-level estimates carry the raw treatment-minus-control sign convention and, in this dataset, do not align in sign with the native pooled risk ratio. Direction of effect has accordingly been taken only from the analysis's `favors` field, which reports that the comparison favours neither arm.
- **No study-level covariates available**, so no moderator analysis or meta-regression could be fitted and no source of the 89.42% heterogeneity could be formally identified.

## Limitations

1. **Few studies, extreme heterogeneity.** Seven trials with I² = 89.42% is close to the worst case for meta-analytic inference: too few studies to model heterogeneity, too much heterogeneity to ignore it. τ² is estimated imprecisely at k = 7, which in turn makes the confidence interval around the pooled RR and the prediction interval themselves uncertain.
2. **Statistically inconclusive primary result.** With p = 0.1582 and a confidence interval crossing 1, the analysis is underpowered to detect or exclude a clinically relevant effect of the size most trials were designed to find.
3. **Dominance of one trial.** A single trial contributes 42.1% of all randomised children; the pooled estimate is sensitive to its assumptions, outcome definition and conduct, although the random-effects weighting mitigates this somewhat relative to a fixed-effect model.
4. **Intervention heterogeneity not disentangled.** Nasal balloons, carnival blowers and commercial devices differ in the pressure they generate and in the skill they demand, yet were pooled as a single "autoinflation" node. Adherence was variably reported or unreported.
5. **Outcome heterogeneity.** Mixing type-B-only and type-B-or-C2 tympanometric definitions with pneumatic otoscopy introduces measurement inconsistency that cannot be removed analytically.
6. **Unit-of-analysis approximation.** The design-effect adjustment for per-ear data assumes an intra-child correlation implicit in the 0.5 factor; the true correlation between ears is unknown and may differ across trials.
7. **Risk of bias in the primary studies not formally incorporated.** Autoinflation cannot be blinded to the child or carer; outcome assessment by tympanometry may or may not have been blinded, and performance and detection bias are plausible. No risk-of-bias-stratified sensitivity analysis was possible with k = 7.
8. **Publication-bias assessment underpowered.** Egger's test (p = 0.8186) and trim-and-fill (k₀ = 0) are reassuring but have low power below ten studies and behave unreliably under high heterogeneity.
9. **Narrow outcome window.** By design this review addresses only persistence of effusion at ≤6 weeks. It says nothing about longer-term effusion resolution, hearing, symptoms, quality of life, or avoidance of surgery.
10. **No individual participant data**, precluding analysis of effect modification by age, laterality, baseline tympanogram or effusion duration.

---

## Conclusion

Across seven randomised comparisons and 625 children with otitis media with effusion, autoinflation did not demonstrably reduce the proportion with persistent middle ear effusion at the first follow-up at or before six weeks: pooled **Risk Ratio 0.791 (95% CI 0.571 to 1.096; p = 0.1582; k = 7; n = 625; REML random effects; native RR pooling confirmed)**, a result that on the accompanying direction field **favours neither arm**. Heterogeneity was considerable and directionally mixed (Q = 26.163, df = 6, p = 0.0002; I² = 89.42%; τ² = 0.155731), with an approximate 95% prediction interval spanning roughly RR 0.26 to 2.4, so the pooled mean should not be treated as a dependable estimate of a common effect. Publication-bias diagnostics were negative (Egger p = 0.8186; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0) but underpowered at this number of studies.

Autoinflation remains a cheap, safe and reasonable option to offer alongside watchful waiting, but families should not be told that it reliably clears glue ear within six weeks. Resolving the question requires larger trials with a standardised device, documented and verified adherence, an age range in which the manoeuvre is reliably performable, a pre-specified child-level tympanometric definition of persistence, and follow-up extending beyond the early window examined here.
