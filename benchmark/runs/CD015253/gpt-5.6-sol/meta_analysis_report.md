# Autoinflation for Persistent Otitis Media With Effusion in Children at 2–6 Weeks: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Otitis media with effusion (OME) is common in childhood. Autoinflation may promote middle-ear ventilation, but its short-term effect on persistent effusion requires quantitative assessment.

### Objective
To estimate the effect of autoinflation using a nasal balloon, carnival blower, or commercial device, compared with watchful waiting, no treatment, or standard care alone, on persistent OME at the selected first follow-up between 2 and 6 weeks.

### Methods
Eligible participants were children younger than 18 years with OME. The binary outcome was persistent middle-ear effusion, preferentially measured at child level, at the observation closest to 4 weeks; where several eligible observations were otherwise available, the latest within the 2–6-week window was selected. Persistence could be defined by a type B tympanogram, type B or C2 tympanogram, or persistent effusion on pneumatic otoscopy; where only resolution or improvement was reported, persistence was to be derived by subtraction from the analyzed total. Risk ratios (RRs) were pooled using a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model.

### Results
Seven studies were represented in the review pipeline, but only five effect sizes (451 participants or analyzed units in total) contributed to the quantitative synthesis. The pooled RR was 0.7654751293746681 (95% CI 0.446–1.313; p = 0.33149882131551683). The supplied direction field classified this result as favoring neither arm—essentially a tie, or indeterminate because favorability depends on the event counted. Heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 94.12%; τ² = 0.330225; Q = 22.654, p = 0.0001). Egger’s intercept was −0.8035 (p = 0.7498), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0); the supplied publication-bias assessment was low risk.

### Conclusions
The pooled analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in persistent OME at 2–6 weeks. Very high heterogeneity, a small evidence base, incomplete study-level descriptive information, and inconsistencies between the reported study-level standardized outputs and the native RR synthesis substantially limit interpretation. The result should not be treated as evidence of equivalence or no effect.

## Introduction

Otitis media with effusion (OME) is the presence of middle-ear fluid without signs or symptoms of acute ear infection. Persistent effusion can impair hearing and may affect communication, behavior, and learning, although the present review was restricted to persistence of effusion and did not evaluate those downstream outcomes.

Autoinflation is a non-surgical intervention intended to ventilate the middle ear by increasing nasopharyngeal pressure and opening the Eustachian tube. Devices include nasal balloons, carnival blowers, and purpose-designed commercial systems. Because OME often resolves spontaneously, an appropriate comparison is watchful waiting, no active treatment, or standard care alone.

The review question was: among children younger than 18 years with OME, what is the effect of autoinflation, compared with watchful waiting, no treatment, or standard care alone, on persistent middle-ear effusion at the selected first follow-up between 2 and 6 weeks?

## Search/Screening

### Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible for the target synthesis when they met the following criteria:

- **Population:** children younger than 18 years with OME.
- **Intervention:** autoinflation using a nasal balloon, carnival blower, or commercial autoinflation device.
- **Comparator:** watchful waiting, no treatment, or standard care alone.
- **Outcome:** binary persistence of OME, expressed as the number of children—or ears when child-level data were unavailable—with persistent middle-ear effusion out of the total analyzed in each arm.
- **Outcome definitions:** a type B (flat) tympanogram, a type B or C2 tympanogram, or effusion still present on pneumatic otoscopy. When only resolution or improvement was reported, persistence was to be derived as the analyzed total minus the number resolved or improved.
- **Timing:** the selected first follow-up within 2–6 weeks. The time closest to 4 weeks was preferred; if several observations were eligible and no single observation was otherwise preferred, the latest within the window was selected.
- **Data hierarchy:** child-level results were preferred over ear-level results.

Hearing, symptoms, quality of life, adverse events, and persistence assessed after 6 weeks were outside the target outcome.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled **5 studies/effect sizes** with a total reported sample of **451**.

The **pooled Risk Ratio (RR)** was **0.7654751293746681** (95% CI **0.446 to 1.313**; p = **0.33149882131551683**). The confidence interval included the null value of 1, and the model did not show a statistically significant difference between groups at the conventional 0.05 threshold.

The supplied `favors` field classified the result as **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)”**. This classification is retained as required and is not replaced by a direction inferred from the numerical point estimate. Given the mismatch between the stated persistence event and the supplied directional classification, the pooled result is best presented as statistically inconclusive rather than as definitively favoring either autoinflation or control.

Failure to reject the null does not establish equivalence, non-inferiority, or absence of a clinically meaningful effect. The wide confidence interval remains compatible with both appreciable benefit and possible harm.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was very high:

- **I² = 94.12%**
- **τ² = 0.330225**
- **Cochran’s Q = 22.654**
- **Q-test p = 0.0001**

Thus, most of the observed dispersion was attributed to between-study differences rather than sampling error under the assumptions of the model. The significant Q test also rejects a common-effect explanation, although the test should be interpreted cautiously with only five studies.

Potential sources of heterogeneity could include differences in autoinflation device, administration schedule, adherence, participant age, duration or laterality of OME, diagnostic criteria, follow-up timing, and child-level versus ear-level analysis. These explanations are clinically plausible but could not be tested because study-level characteristics were not provided.

This finding should be interpreted cautiously. With only five studies, Egger’s test has low power, and very high heterogeneity can distort funnel-plot-based procedures. Moreover, the trim-and-fill estimate was provided on a scale that was not explicitly reconciled with the pooled native RR, so it should not be interpreted as an adjusted RR. Absence of detected asymmetry is not proof that reporting or publication bias is absent.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across five quantitatively synthesized studies or effect sizes totaling 451 participants or analyzed units, the REML random-effects model produced a pooled RR of **0.7654751293746681** (95% CI **0.446–1.313**; p = **0.33149882131551683**) for persistent OME at the selected follow-up between 2 and 6 weeks. The result was not statistically significant, and its confidence interval was broad. The supplied direction classification favored neither arm.

The dominant feature of the analysis was extreme inconsistency (**I² = 94.12%**). A single pooled average may therefore have limited applicability to any particular clinical setting, device, age group, or diagnostic definition. Before drawing clinical conclusions, the reasons for this heterogeneity should be investigated using verified study-level data.

### Clinical interpretation

The available synthesis does not provide firm evidence that autoinflation changes the short-term persistence of OME compared with watchful waiting, no treatment, or standard care alone. However, the analysis also does not rule out a potentially important effect. Because the outcome was restricted to effusion persistence at 2–6 weeks, the findings do not address hearing, symptoms, quality of life, adverse events, or longer-term recurrence and persistence.

Clinical decisions should also account for feasibility, age-dependent ability to perform autoinflation, adherence, acceptability, cost, safety, and spontaneous resolution. Those considerations were not evaluated in this analysis.

### Certainty and applicability

A formal certainty-of-evidence assessment was not supplied and cannot be completed reliably from the available aggregate context. Confidence is reduced by substantial unexplained heterogeneity, imprecision, the small number of contributing studies, absent risk-of-bias information, and uncertainty about units of analysis and outcome definitions. Applicability may vary if studies differed in age, device, adherence, disease chronicity, or diagnostic criteria.

## Data Integrity and Reporting Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` object was supplied, the reported outputs contain issues that require explicit attention:

1. **Seven studies but five pooled effects:** the pipeline identified seven studies, while only five effect sizes entered the synthesis. Reasons for the two non-contributing studies were not supplied.
2. **Scale mismatch:** study-level effects were presented as standardized values labeled `hedges_g`, but the aggregate result was identified as a natively pooled RR. Without event counts or study-level RRs, these outputs cannot be reconciled.
3. **Direction mismatch:** the target event was persistence of OME, yet the supplied `favors` field classified the pooled result as favoring neither arm or as directionally indeterminate. Direction was therefore reported exactly as supplied rather than inferred.
4. **Unverifiable outcome hierarchy:** the context did not identify whether each estimate used child-level or ear-level data, the precise diagnostic definition, or the exact selected follow-up.
5. **Unverifiable effective sample:** the reported total of 451 is the sum of the five supplied `n` values, but it is unclear whether every value represents children, ears, or another analyzed unit.

These points should be resolved against trial reports and extracted 2×2 tables before the findings are used for guideline development or definitive clinical inference.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations:

- The search strategy, databases, dates, screening process, and exclusion reasons were not provided, preventing assessment of search completeness and full PRISMA flow reporting.
- Bibliographic and design details for the seven studies were unavailable.
- Only five studies/effect sizes contributed to the pooled result.
- Arm-level events and denominators were absent, preventing independent calculation of study-specific and pooled RRs.
- It was not possible to verify use of the preferred child-level outcome, selected timing, or persistence definition for each study.
- No study-level risk-of-bias assessment was available.
- Heterogeneity was extreme and unexplained.
- The study-level standardized outputs did not match the native RR scale reported for pooling.
- Publication-bias tests were underpowered because k = 5, and the trim-and-fill estimate was not clearly on the RR scale.
- No prediction interval was supplied; such an interval would be particularly informative given the high heterogeneity.
- No sensitivity analyses were reported for outcome definition, follow-up time, unit of analysis, model estimator, influential studies, or continuity corrections.
- No formal GRADE assessment was available.

## Conclusion

In five pooled studies/effect sizes totaling 451 reported participants or analyzed units, autoinflation versus watchful waiting, no treatment, or standard care alone yielded a pooled **Risk Ratio of 0.7654751293746681** (95% CI **0.446–1.313**; p = **0.33149882131551683**) for persistent OME at 2–6 weeks. The supplied direction field classified the finding as favoring neither arm, and the result was not statistically significant. Heterogeneity was very high (**I² = 94.12%**), substantially limiting confidence in the pooled average.

Accordingly, the evidence is inconclusive rather than evidence of equivalence or no effect. Verification of study-level event data, outcome coding, analysis unit, follow-up selection, and the discrepancy between standardized study outputs and native RR pooling is required. Future syntheses should provide complete search and screening records, risk-of-bias judgments, 2×2 outcome data, device and adherence details, sensitivity analyses, and certainty-of-evidence assessment.
