# Single-incision mini-slings versus transobturator mid-urethral slings for stress urinary incontinence in women: a systematic review and meta-analysis of patient-reported cure or improvement at 12 months (TVT-Secur excluded)

## Abstract

**Background.** Single-incision mini-slings (SIS) were introduced to reduce the morbidity associated with the passage of trocars through the obturator foramen, but early-generation devices — most notably TVT-Secur — performed poorly and were withdrawn, contaminating the evidence base for the device class as a whole. Whether contemporary single-incision devices deliver patient-reported continence outcomes equivalent to standard transobturator mid-urethral slings (TOT/TVT-O) remains the clinically decisive question.

**Objective.** To determine whether single-incision mid-urethral slings (any device except TVT-Secur) achieve higher rates of subjective (patient-reported) cure or improvement of stress urinary incontinence at 12 months than transobturator mid-urethral slings in women operated on for stress or stress-predominant urinary incontinence.

**Methods.** A systematic search of randomised and quasi-randomised comparative trials was screened against pre-specified eligibility criteria; arms were assigned by device description rather than column position, with SIS always treated as the index arm and any TVT-Secur arm ignored. The pre-specified outcome was the number of women reporting subjective cure or improvement, as defined by each source report (e.g. no stress leakage on ICIQ-UI/UDI-6/ISI, "much better" or "very much better" on a global impression-of-improvement scale, or satisfaction-based success), divided by the number analysed in each arm at 12 months; objective measures (cough stress test, pad test, urodynamics) were not used. Where a trial reported longer follow-up, 12-month figures were extracted from tables or figures; where 12 months was unavailable, the closest earlier assessment of at least 6 months was used. Multi-arm trials contributed each eligible SIS arm against the shared TOT group with events and sample size divided equally. Multiple reports of the same trial were extracted once. Risk ratios (RR) were pooled on the native risk-ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ²; small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Fourteen studies entered the review and 12 yielded an extractable 12-month subjective effect estimate; 11 trials (n = 1,629 women) supplied the raw event and denominator data required for native risk-ratio pooling. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for subjective cure or improvement with SIS versus TOT/TVT-O was 0.992 (95% CI 0.960 to 1.024; p = 0.6136), a result that favours **neither** arm — it is essentially a tie, and the confidence interval excludes any clinically meaningful advantage in either direction. Heterogeneity was absent: Q = 5.19 on 10 degrees of freedom (p = 0.8781), I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0. Egger's regression showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = 0.1362, p = 0.3461) and trim-and-fill imputed a single hypothetical missing study (k₀ = 1, adjusted standardized estimate −0.0582), yet overall publication-bias risk was rated **moderate** because these tests are underpowered with eleven studies.

**Conclusions.** Contemporary single-incision mini-slings, with TVT-Secur excluded, do **not** achieve higher rates of patient-reported cure or improvement at 12 months than transobturator slings; nor are they detectably worse. The pooled estimate is a tight null (RR 0.992, 95% CI 0.960–1.024) with no between-study heterogeneity, supporting equivalence of the two approaches on this outcome at one year. Because the analysis is confined to 12-month subjective success and does not address pain, mesh exposure, re-operation or durability beyond one year, device choice should continue to be guided by those domains, and by the recognition that bias was "not detected" rather than demonstrably absent.

## 1. Introduction

Mid-urethral synthetic slings have been the reference surgical treatment for female stress urinary incontinence (SUI) for two decades. The retropubic tension-free vaginal tape (TVT) and the transobturator routes (TOT, outside-in; TVT-O, inside-out) both achieve high continence rates, but each requires blind passage of a trocar through tissue planes beyond the vagina — retropubically, with its risk of bladder and vascular injury, or through the obturator foramen and adductor compartment, with its characteristic groin and thigh pain.

Single-incision slings (SIS, "mini-slings") were developed to retain the mid-urethral support principle while eliminating the exit wounds and the trans-adductor passage: the tape is anchored into the obturator internus membrane or fascia through a single anterior vaginal incision. The first widely marketed device, TVT-Secur, performed substantially worse than standard slings in multiple randomised trials and was withdrawn from the market. Its inclusion in earlier pooled analyses has systematically disadvantaged the single-incision class, because the device-specific failure of one anchoring design was averaged with the performance of structurally different later devices (Ajust, Needleless/Contasure, MiniArc, Ophira, Altis, Solyx). The clinically relevant question for contemporary practice is therefore narrower and sharper: **among the devices still in use, do single-incision slings deliver better patient-reported continence at one year than transobturator slings?**

Patient-reported outcomes are the appropriate primary lens for this question. Objective tests (cough stress test, 1-hour or 24-hour pad test, urodynamic stress incontinence) measure a different construct, correlate imperfectly with what women experience, and are not what drives satisfaction or re-operation. This review was therefore restricted *a priori* to subjective cure or improvement as each trial itself defined it, at the 12-month time point, with TVT-Secur arms excluded by design.

**Effect direction convention.** Throughout this report, the effect measure is the risk ratio for subjective cure or improvement. A risk ratio greater than 1 indicates that a *greater* proportion of women were subjectively cured or improved with the single-incision sling than with the transobturator sling; a risk ratio below 1 indicates the reverse.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Information sources and search

A systematic search of the comparative-trial literature was executed to identify randomised and quasi-randomised controlled trials comparing single-incision mid-urethral slings with transobturator mid-urethral slings in adult women undergoing surgery for stress or stress-predominant urinary incontinence. Retrieved records were de-duplicated and passed through a staged screening pipeline: (i) a document-type gate that removed narrative reviews, prior systematic reviews, editorials, conference abstracts without extractable arm-level data, and near-duplicate or split fragments of a single underlying report; (ii) a population/intervention/comparator gate; and (iii) an outcome gate requiring an extractable count of women reporting subjective cure or improvement, together with the number analysed, in each arm.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

| Domain | Inclusion | Exclusion |
|---|---|---|
| Population | Adult women undergoing surgery for stress or stress-predominant urinary incontinence | Pure urgency incontinence; male patients; pelvic organ prolapse surgery without a concomitant SUI sling comparison |
| Intervention | Single-incision mini-sling of any design — Ajust, Needleless/Contasure, MiniArc, Ophira, Altis, Solyx and comparable devices | **TVT-Secur arms, which were ignored by protocol** |
| Comparator | Transobturator mid-urethral sling, inside-out (TVT-O) or outside-in (TOT) | Retropubic TVT, colposuspension, bulking agents, autologous fascial sling as the sole comparator |
| Outcome | Number of women reporting subjective cure **or** improvement out of number analysed, at 12 months | Objective-only reporting (cough stress test, pad test, urodynamics) with no patient-reported counterpart |
| Design | Randomised or quasi-randomised comparative trial with arm-level numerators and denominators | Single-arm series; purely narrative or review documents |

### 2.3 Time-point and arm-assignment rules (pre-specified)

* The target time point was **12 months**. Where a report covered 24- or 36-month follow-up, the 12-month figures were extracted from its tables or figures rather than the terminal time point.
* Where 12 months was genuinely unavailable, the **closest earlier assessment of at least 6 months** was substituted.
* **Arms were assigned by device description, not by column position**, with the single-incision device always designated the treatment (index) arm, so that the sign of every effect estimate is directionally comparable.
* For trials with **more than one eligible single-incision arm**, each SIS arm was compared against the shared TOT group, whose events and sample size were **divided equally** across the resulting comparisons to avoid unit-of-analysis double-counting of controls.
* **Any TVT-Secur arm was ignored** entirely, including in the computation of shared-control denominators.
* **Multiple reports of the same trial were extracted once**, preferring the report that gave the 12-month subjective result.

### 2.4 Screening outcome

Fourteen studies satisfied all eligibility criteria and entered the review. One further document was removed at the document-type gate as a near-duplicate/split fragment of an already-included report, detected by full-text content similarity (see §9, Data Quality & Validation Notes). Of the 14 included studies, **12 yielded an extractable 12-month subjective effect estimate**; two contributed no usable 12-month patient-reported numerator/denominator pair and are carried in the review narratively only. Of those 12, **11 trials supplied the raw event counts and denominators required for pooling on the native risk-ratio scale**; one (study_5) lacked the raw inputs and was consequently excluded from the native RR synthesis, although its standardized estimate is reported below for completeness.

## 3. Included Studies

Fourteen trials were included in the review; twelve contributed a quantitative estimate of subjective cure or improvement at 12 months. The eleven trials contributing to the primary pooled risk ratio comprised **1,629 randomised women analysed** (study_1, study_3, study_4, study_6, study_7, study_8, study_10, study_11, study_12, study_13, study_14). Arm sizes in the contributing trials ranged from 42 to 368 women analysed, with a median of approximately 137; the single largest trial (study_14, n = 368) contributed roughly 23% of the analysed sample, and the smallest (study_8, n = 42) roughly 3%.

The single-incision devices represented across the evidence base span the contemporary families named in the protocol — adjustable anchored tapes (Ajust, Altis), fixed-anchor polypropylene mini-slings (MiniArc, Ophira, Solyx) and anchorless/pocket-fixation designs (Needleless/Contasure). Comparators were transobturator tapes placed either inside-out (TVT-O) or outside-in (TOT). Subjective success definitions were, as permitted by protocol, those used by each source report, and included (a) absence of stress leakage on a validated questionnaire (ICIQ-UI short form, UDI-6, Incontinence Severity Index), (b) a rating of "much better" or "very much better" on a Patient Global Impression of Improvement or equivalent global scale, and (c) satisfaction-based composite success. This definitional pluralism is a recognised source of clinical heterogeneity and is addressed in §6 and §11.

Because the protocol combined **cure or improvement** into a single success event, event rates in both arms were high in most trials (typically in the 80–95% range), which has direct implications for the precision and interpretation of the ratio measure: with success common in both arms, the risk ratio is compressed toward 1 and even substantial differences in *complete dryness* can be masked by the inclusion of "improved" responders.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Results

A single outcome/time-point group was synthesised (subjective cure or improvement at 12 months); the context data contain no `meta_analysis_groups` with multiple entries, so no separate per-group pooling was required.

The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for patient-reported cure or improvement at 12 months was **0.992 (95% CI 0.960–1.024, p = 0.6136)**. Per the `favors` field of the analysis, this result favours **neither** arm: it is essentially a tie. The point estimate lies 0.8% below unity, which is not distinguishable from no difference, and the confidence interval is narrow enough to be informative in its own right — it is compatible, at the extremes, with at most a 4.0% *relative* reduction and at most a 2.4% *relative* increase in the probability of subjective success with the single-incision sling.

Translated to an absolute scale at a representative transobturator success rate of 90%, the pooled estimate corresponds to a single-incision success rate of 89.3%, with 95% compatibility limits of 86.4% to 92.2% — an absolute risk difference of −0.7 percentage points (95% CI −3.6 to +2.2). Even the least favourable boundary of this interval is below the threshold commonly nominated as clinically important for subjective continence success in sling trials, so these data are more consistent with **equivalence** than with an undetected difference.

The direct answer to the research question is therefore negative: single-incision mid-urethral slings (TVT-Secur excluded) **do not** achieve higher rates of subjective cure or improvement at 12 months than transobturator slings. They also do not achieve detectably lower rates.

The forest plot for the primary synthesis is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. A cumulative meta-analysis, ordered to display the evolution of the pooled estimate as evidence accrued, is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; the pooled estimate stabilises close to the null and the confidence interval narrows monotonically without any directional drift, indicating that no single trial or era of trials is driving the conclusion.

**Note on the excluded comparison.** One trial (study_5, n = 183, standardized *g* = −0.1958) could not be pooled natively because it lacked the raw event and denominator inputs required for a risk-ratio computation. Its standardized estimate sits within the range spanned by the pooled trials and on the same side of the null as the (non-significant) pooled point estimate, so its omission is very unlikely to have altered the conclusion; it does, however, mean the primary analysis rests on 11 of the 12 available comparisons and on 1,629 of the 1,812 women for whom a 12-month subjective estimate existed.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **absent by every available metric**:

* Cochran's **Q = 5.19** on 10 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.8781** — far from significance, and in fact *below* its expected value under the null (E[Q] = k − 1 = 10), indicating less dispersion among the study estimates than sampling error alone would predict;
* **I² = 0.0%** — none of the observed variability is attributable to true between-study differences rather than chance;
* **τ² = 0.0** under REML — the estimated between-study variance is at the boundary of the parameter space, so the random-effects model collapses to a fixed-effect weighting in practice and no prediction interval wider than the confidence interval can be constructed.

This is an unusually clean heterogeneity profile for a surgical meta-analysis and it has two readings. The benign reading is that the device class behaves consistently: once TVT-Secur is removed, contemporary single-incision slings produce the same one-year subjective result as transobturator slings across different devices, surgeons, countries and questionnaires. The more cautious reading is that the outcome definition itself is **ceiling-limited** — combining "cure" with "improvement" produces success rates in the high 80s and low 90s in both arms of nearly every trial, which mechanically compresses risk ratios toward 1 and suppresses the between-study variance that would otherwise be visible in a stricter, cure-only endpoint. A Q statistic that undershoots its degrees of freedom is consistent with this compression. Both readings support the same conclusion about the pooled effect, but only the first supports extrapolating the finding to stricter definitions of success.

## 7. Moderators

No moderator or subgroup analysis was performed, and none would be interpretable on these data. With **τ² = 0.0 and I² = 0.0%**, there is no between-study variance left for any covariate to explain: a meta-regression on device family (adjustable-anchor vs fixed-anchor vs anchorless), transobturator direction (inside-out vs outside-in), subjective success definition (questionnaire-based vs global-impression vs satisfaction-based), trial size, or follow-up completeness would be fitting a model to residual sampling noise. Any apparently "significant" moderator recovered in that setting would be an artefact.

The bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png` displays effect size against study precision and is presented descriptively: it shows the characteristic symmetric funnel shape with no systematic gradient of effect across the precision range, consistent with the absence of both heterogeneity and small-study effects.

Two moderator questions that the data cannot answer, but that readers should hold in mind, are (i) whether the equivalence holds in women with intrinsic sphincter deficiency or prior failed continence surgery, who are under-represented in these trials, and (ii) whether it holds for individual devices rather than the class — the pooled estimate is a class-level average, and the protocol's exclusion of TVT-Secur is itself proof that device-level differences within this class can be large.

## 8. Publication Bias

Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.1362 |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.3461** |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **1** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (standardized scale) | −0.0582 |
| Overall assessed risk | **Moderate** |

Egger's regression showed **no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry** (intercept = 0.1362, p = 0.3461), and trim-and-fill imputed only a **single** hypothetical missing study (k₀ = 1), implying that an asymmetry-adjusted pooled effect would differ little from the observed one (adjusted standardized estimate −0.0582, still a null-centred value). Taken together, these diagnostics provide **no strong statistical evidence of small-study effects or publication bias**.

Nevertheless, the overall risk is rated **moderate**, not low. The reason is statistical power, not a positive signal of bias: with eleven trials, Egger's test has low sensitivity, and a non-significant result **cannot confirm the absence** of bias. The appropriate reading is therefore "**bias not detected**" rather than "bias absent." Several contextual features reinforce this caution in the device-surgery literature specifically: single-incision slings are proprietary products whose trials are frequently industry-sponsored or industry-supported; neutral or unfavourable device trials are historically slower to reach full publication; and the withdrawal of TVT-Secur demonstrates that device-class evidence can be reshaped retrospectively by post-marketing information that never enters the randomised record. Conclusions should remain appropriately cautious pending additional evidence, including unpublished and grey-literature sources.

## 9. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The analysis pipeline raised the following validation flags, which are reported here in full and without softening:

1. **One document excluded as a near-duplicate / split fragment (document-type gate, severity: warning).** A record (internal identifier `manual:93aff17d`) was excluded after full-text content-similarity screening identified it as a near-duplicate or split fragment of an already-included report (`1e73d17a_jwh.2015.5416`, internal identifier `manual:70a7ff2c`). This exclusion implements the protocol rule that multiple reports of the same trial are extracted only once, and prevents the same participants from contributing twice to the pooled estimate. No participant data were lost, because the retained report is the one carrying the eligible result.

In addition, two features of the synthesis warrant explicit statement:

2. **One comparison was excluded from native risk-ratio pooling for lack of raw inputs.** Study_5 (n = 183) reported no extractable arm-level event counts and denominators and therefore could not be pooled on the native RR scale. It is included in Table 1 as a standardized estimate only. The primary meta-analysis consequently includes 11 of the 12 available comparisons (1,629 of 1,812 women). This is a genuine, if modest, loss of information from the primary synthesis rather than a presentational choice.

3. **Two of the fourteen included studies yielded no quantitative 12-month subjective estimate at all** and contribute to the review narratively only (14 studies screened in; 12 effect sizes computed).

4. **Effect-measure provenance is clean.** The risk ratio was specified explicitly, `native_pooling_used` is true, and the computation and reporting scales are both RR. The standardized (Hedges' *g*) values in Table 1 and in the trim-and-fill output are display and diagnostic quantities; no result in this report is a standardized fallback substituting for an unconfirmed native measure.

5. **No insufficient-data groups.** Only one outcome/time-point group exists, and it was adequately populated for pooling; no group had to be reported as unpoolable.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis of eleven randomised comparisons in 1,629 women gives a precise and unambiguous answer to the question posed: with TVT-Secur excluded, single-incision mini-slings do **not** deliver superior patient-reported cure or improvement of stress urinary incontinence at 12 months compared with transobturator slings (pooled Risk Ratio 0.992, 95% CI 0.960–1.024, p = 0.6136, favouring neither arm). The result is a tight null with zero measurable heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q = 5.19, p = 0.8781).

The clinical value of this finding lies less in the point estimate than in the width of the interval. A confidence interval of 0.960 to 1.024 around a high baseline success rate rules out differences that would matter to a woman choosing between the two operations: at a 90% transobturator success rate, the compatible range for the single-incision sling is 86.4% to 92.2%. This converts a historically contested comparison into one in which **equivalence on 12-month subjective success can reasonably be asserted**, rather than merely "no significant difference found."

The contrast with earlier syntheses is instructive and is attributable to the protocol's device-level exclusion. Pooled analyses that incorporated TVT-Secur arms reported inferiority of the single-incision class, and that inferiority has sometimes been generalised to all mini-slings. The present analysis shows that once the one device with a demonstrably deficient anchoring mechanism — and one that is no longer available — is removed, the residual class is indistinguishable from the transobturator standard on patient-reported outcomes at one year. That is a statement about device engineering rather than about the single-incision *concept*, and it argues strongly against class-level reasoning in mesh sling evaluation. The corollary is also true and should temper enthusiasm: because device-level differences within this class have already proved large enough to drive a market withdrawal, a class-level equivalence result does not license the assumption that *every* single-incision device is equivalent to TOT. The pooled estimate is an average across Ajust, Needleless/Contasure, MiniArc, Ophira, Altis and Solyx-type devices, and the data contain no power to resolve differences among them.

Three further caveats bear on how far this result should travel. First, the endpoint is deliberately permissive: "cure **or** improvement," as each trial defined it. This is the outcome that best captures the patient's own verdict, but it is also the outcome most likely to show equivalence, because partially effective surgery still registers as "improved." A stricter endpoint — complete dryness, or "very much better" alone — could plausibly separate the arms, and this analysis does not exclude that possibility. Second, the time horizon is 12 months. Concerns about single-incision slings have historically centred on **durability**, specifically anchor pull-out and progressive loss of tension; a one-year equivalence says nothing about five- or ten-year outcomes, and the protocol's permission to take 12-month figures from longer-follow-up reports means that longer data existed for some trials but were deliberately not analysed here. Third, the analysis addresses efficacy only. The entire rationale for single-incision slings is **reduced morbidity** — less groin and thigh pain, faster return to activity, fewer exit-wound complications — and the entire counter-argument concerns mesh exposure and re-operation. On the efficacy outcome examined here the arms are tied; the decision between them must therefore be made on the harm and recovery domains that this review did not assess.

Finally, the publication-bias picture should be read as it is stated: diagnostics were negative, but with eleven studies they were also underpowered, and the risk rating remains moderate. In a literature dominated by proprietary devices and industry-supported trials, "bias not detected" is a weaker claim than it may appear.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Outcome-definition heterogeneity.** Subjective success was accepted as each report defined it — absence of stress leakage on ICIQ-UI/UDI-6/ISI, "much better"/"very much better" on a global impression scale, or satisfaction-based success. These are not interchangeable constructs, and their pooling introduces clinical heterogeneity that the statistical heterogeneity metrics (I² = 0.0%) cannot detect and should not be taken to exclude.
2. **Ceiling effect on the composite endpoint.** Combining cure with improvement produces very high event rates in both arms, compressing the risk ratio toward 1 and plausibly contributing to the near-zero τ². The observed Q (5.19) falling below its expected value (10) is consistent with such compression.
3. **One eligible comparison excluded from the primary pool.** Study_5 (n = 183) lacked raw event counts and denominators and could not be pooled natively; 11 of 12 comparisons and 1,629 of 1,812 women inform the primary estimate.
4. **Two included studies contributed no quantitative estimate** at the 12-month subjective endpoint.
5. **Time-point substitution.** The protocol permitted use of the closest earlier assessment of at least 6 months where 12-month data were unavailable, so a minority of comparisons may reflect a shorter horizon than the nominal one-year target.
6. **Shared-control splitting in multi-arm trials.** Dividing TOT events and denominators equally across multiple SIS comparisons controls unit-of-analysis error but artificially halves control-arm precision and assumes exchangeability of the SIS arms against a common control.
7. **No risk-of-bias-weighted or sensitivity analysis is reported here.** Blinding of participants and assessors is difficult in sling surgery, outcome ascertainment is frequently by unblinded questionnaire, and allocation concealment and loss-to-follow-up were not formally graded in this synthesis. Residual performance and detection bias cannot be quantified.
8. **Underpowered small-study diagnostics.** Egger's test and trim-and-fill are unreliable with k = 11; the moderate bias rating reflects this limitation rather than a detected signal.
9. **No moderator analysis possible.** With τ² = 0.0 there is no explainable between-study variance, so device-level, surgical-route-level and definition-level questions remain open.
10. **Scope restrictions.** Pain, mesh exposure, voiding dysfunction, de novo urgency, operative time, re-operation and outcomes beyond 12 months were outside the review question and are not addressed. Populations with intrinsic sphincter deficiency, prior failed continence surgery, or concomitant prolapse repair are under-represented.
11. **One record excluded as a duplicate fragment** (§9); while this correctly prevents double-counting, duplicate-detection by content similarity is not infallible and a residual risk of either over- or under-merging reports of the same trial exists.

## 12. Conclusion

In women undergoing surgery for stress or stress-predominant urinary incontinence, single-incision mid-urethral slings (TVT-Secur excluded) **do not achieve higher rates of subjective cure or improvement at 12 months** than transobturator mid-urethral slings. Across 11 randomised comparisons in 1,629 women, the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was **0.992 (95% CI 0.960 to 1.024; p = 0.6136)** — a result that favours **neither** arm — with no detectable between-study heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q = 5.19, p = 0.8781). The confidence interval is narrow enough to exclude any clinically meaningful advantage in either direction, supporting **equivalence** of contemporary single-incision and transobturator slings on patient-reported continence at one year.

This equivalence emerges only when TVT-Secur is excluded, which underscores that single-incision slings should be evaluated device by device rather than as a class. Because the present analysis is confined to 12-month subjective success and does not examine pain, mesh exposure, re-operation or durability beyond one year, and because publication-bias diagnostics were negative but underpowered (risk rated moderate), the choice between these two operations should be governed by those unexamined harm, recovery and longevity domains — not by any expectation of superior one-year continence from either approach.
