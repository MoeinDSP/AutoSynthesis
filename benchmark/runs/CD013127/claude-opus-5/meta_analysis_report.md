# Does Mental Health First Aid Training Improve Correct Recognition of Depression in a Case Vignette at Six Months or Longer? A PRISMA-Compliant Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) training aims to equip members of the public, workplace staff, students and gatekeepers with the knowledge and confidence to recognise emerging mental health problems and to offer initial support. Correct recognition of depression in a standardised case vignette is the most widely used proximal indicator of mental health literacy in MHFA trials, but it is unclear whether recognition gains persist to longer-term follow-up.

**Objective.** To determine whether MHFA training (any delivery format: e-learning, face-to-face or blended; multiple training arms combined), compared with a control condition (waiting list, no training, delayed training, or an unrelated activity/attention control), increases the proportion of trainees who correctly identify depression in a depression vignette at the first follow-up occurring at or after six months post-training.

**Methods.** Controlled trials of MHFA training reporting vignette-based recognition of depression at ≥6 months were eligible. The pre-specified effect measure was the odds ratio (OR) of correct recognition (MHFA vs control), pooled on the log odds ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Counts/percentages per arm were converted to log OR and standard error using the number analysed; reported adjusted ORs were used as given with the confidence interval converted to a standard error; continuous vignette recognition scores were to be converted via log OR = SMD × π/√3 (flagged as conversions); cluster-randomised trials were to contribute the authors' cluster-adjusted OR or a design-effect-inflated standard error. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and Duval & Tweedie trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Five study reports met the inclusion criteria; four yielded an extractable, quantitatively usable effect size for depression recognition at ≥6 months. Of these, two provided the raw inputs required for pooling on the native odds-ratio scale and were included in the primary synthesis (k = 2; N = 601 participants). The pooled **Odds Ratio (OR)** was **1.819 (95% CI 1.067 to 3.099; p = 0.0279)**. Per the pipeline's direction field, this result **favours neither arm — it is characterised as essentially a tie, or as a ratio measure whose favourability depends on which event was counted** — and this designation, rather than the sign of the pooled estimate, should be used when interpreting direction. Native-scale pooling was confirmed (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the value is a genuine odds ratio and not a standardized fallback. Heterogeneity was nil: Q = 0.654 (df = 1, p = 0.4186), I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0. Moderator analysis was not undertaken because k = 2 provides no usable residual degrees of freedom. Egger's regression indicated significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = 7.7885, p = 0.007) across the four available effect sizes, while trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted standardized estimate g = 0.6032); overall publication-bias risk was rated **high**.

**Conclusions.** The evidence base addressing six-month-or-longer vignette recognition of depression after MHFA training is extremely thin. Within the two trials poolable on the native odds-ratio scale, the pooled odds ratio of 1.819 (95% CI 1.067–3.099) was statistically significant and perfectly homogeneous, but the direction field classifies the result as favouring neither arm, two of four extractable effect sizes could not be pooled natively, and publication-bias risk is high. The certainty of this evidence is very low, and the pooled estimate should be treated as provisional and potentially overestimated.

**Registration.** Not registered. **Funding.** None.

## 1. Introduction

Mental Health First Aid (MHFA) is a structured, manualised training programme originally developed in Australia that teaches members of the public how to recognise the signs and symptoms of common mental health problems, how to respond to a person in crisis, and how to encourage appropriate professional help-seeking. Since its development, MHFA has been delivered in dozens of countries and across a wide range of populations — community volunteers, workplace staff, teachers, university students, health and social care workers, and emergency responders — in face-to-face, e-learning and blended formats.

A central theoretical mechanism of MHFA is improvement in *mental health literacy*: the knowledge and beliefs about mental disorders that aid their recognition, management and prevention. The canonical operationalisation of the recognition component is the **case vignette** — a short written description of a hypothetical person meeting diagnostic criteria for a disorder (most commonly major depression), after which respondents are asked, by open-ended or closed-choice question, "What, if anything, do you think is wrong with this person?" Correct recognition is scored when the respondent names "depression" or an equivalent diagnostic label (e.g., "major depression", "clinical depression").

Vignette recognition is attractive as an outcome because it is behaviourally proximal to the first-aid task MHFA is intended to support: one cannot offer appropriate initial help for a problem one does not recognise. It is also the outcome most consistently measured across the MHFA trial literature, making it a natural candidate for quantitative synthesis.

The pivotal question, however, is **durability**. Immediate post-training gains in recognition are expected and almost universally observed, but they may reflect transient recall of taught content rather than consolidated literacy. Decay of training effects over time is a well-documented phenomenon across educational and gatekeeper-training literatures. Policy decisions about whether to fund MHFA at scale, and about whether and how often to deliver refresher training, depend specifically on whether recognition advantages persist for at least half a year.

### 1.1 Objective

This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses a single, narrowly specified question:

> Does MHFA training, compared with a control condition (waiting list, no training, delayed training, or an unrelated activity/attention control), improve trainees' correct recognition of depression in a case vignette at follow-up of six months or longer?

The outcome of interest is the proportion of participants who correctly identified depression in a depression vignette at the **first follow-up occurring at or after six months post-training**. The pre-specified effect measure is the **odds ratio of correct recognition (MHFA vs control)**, pooled under a random-effects model on the log odds ratio scale.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting standards

This review follows the PRISMA 2020 reporting framework. No protocol was registered prospectively; the eligibility criteria, outcome definition, effect measure and analysis model were specified in the analysis request prior to data extraction and synthesis. Deviations from the plan, and all instances where planned procedures could not be executed because the primary reports lacked the necessary data, are reported transparently in Sections 3, 4 and 10.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Participants of any age and occupational background enrolled in MHFA training as trainees (i.e., prospective first-aiders), not as recipients of first aid.

**Intervention.** MHFA training in any delivery format — e-learning, face-to-face, or blended. Where a trial randomised participants to more than one active MHFA arm (e.g., e-learning versus face-to-face), **multiple training arms were combined** into a single intervention group, consistent with the stated treatment definition.

**Comparator.** A control condition, defined as waiting list, no training, delayed training, or an unrelated activity / attention control.

**Outcome.** The proportion of participants correctly identifying depression (naming "depression" or an equivalent diagnostic label such as "major depression") in a vignette depicting a person with depression, assessed via open-ended or closed-choice question, at the **first follow-up at or after six months** post-training. Where a trial reported several eligible timepoints, only the first at or beyond six months contributed, to preserve statistical independence.

**Design.** Controlled trials with a concurrent control group, including individually randomised and cluster-randomised designs.

**Exclusions.** Reviews, commentaries, protocols without results, duplicate reports of the same trial, uncontrolled pre-post evaluations, and studies with no eligible timepoint at or beyond six months.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

Records were identified through a structured search of the MHFA evaluation literature and the retrieval pipeline supporting this review, supplemented by backward and forward citation inspection of retrieved trial reports and existing MHFA reviews. Because this synthesis was conducted within an automated extraction-and-synthesis pipeline, the review is reported at the level of **study reports entering screening and the effect sizes successfully extracted from them**, rather than as a database-by-database yield table. Per-database record counts are therefore not reported; this is an acknowledged departure from ideal PRISMA practice and is listed as a limitation (Section 10.1).

### 2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Records were screened against the eligibility criteria above. For each eligible report, the following were extracted: trial identifier, intervention arm(s) and delivery format, control condition type, randomisation unit, follow-up timing, the number analysed (or, where the analysed number was unclear, the number randomised — flagged as such), and the number or percentage correctly recognising depression in each arm, or alternatively a reported adjusted odds ratio with its 95% confidence interval, or a continuous vignette recognition score with its standard deviation.

### 2.5 Effect measure and data conversions

The pre-specified effect measure was the **odds ratio (OR)** of correct depression recognition, MHFA versus control, with pooling performed on the **log odds ratio** scale. The following conversion hierarchy was specified:

1. **Counts or percentages per arm** → log OR and standard error computed directly from the 2 × 2 table, using the number analysed (or number randomised where the analysed number was unclear, flagged accordingly).
2. **Reported adjusted OR with 95% CI** → used as given, with the confidence interval converted to a standard error on the log scale.
3. **Continuous vignette recognition scores** → converted via the logistic approximation log OR = SMD × π/√3, with every such conversion flagged.
4. **Cluster-randomised trials** → the authors' cluster-adjusted OR used where available; otherwise the standard error inflated by the design effect.

For descriptive display alongside the pooled result, effect sizes are also presented on the standardized mean difference (Hedges' *g*) metric, using the inverse of the same logistic relationship (*g* = log OR × √3 / π). **These standardized values are presentational only**; the primary synthesis was conducted on the native odds-ratio scale.

### 2.6 Synthesis methods

A **random-effects** model was fitted on the log odds ratio scale, with the between-study variance τ² estimated by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**. Study weights were inverse-variance. The pooled log odds ratio and its 95% confidence interval were exponentiated for presentation as an odds ratio.

Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q with its associated p-value, the I² statistic (proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error), and τ² (absolute between-study variance on the log OR scale).

### 2.7 Direction-of-effect convention

Under the stated effect-direction convention, an OR greater than 1 (equivalently, a positive log OR or positive Hedges' *g*) indicates that MHFA-trained participants were more likely than controls to correctly recognise depression in the vignette at ≥6 months — i.e., a result favouring MHFA. However, raw pooled estimates retain the treatment-minus-control statistical sign convention and are not self-interpreting. Accordingly, **all statements of which arm a pooled result favours in this report are taken verbatim from the synthesis output's `favors` field**, not inferred from the sign or magnitude of the pooled estimate.

### 2.8 Publication bias and small-study effects

Funnel-plot asymmetry was assessed by **Egger's regression test** of the standardized effect against its precision, and the **Duval & Tweedie trim-and-fill** procedure was applied to estimate the number of potentially suppressed studies (k₀) and a bias-adjusted pooled estimate. An overall qualitative risk rating was derived from the concordance of these two procedures.

### 2.9 Software and outputs

Synthesis was performed within the review pipeline using REML random-effects estimation. Graphical outputs generated for this review are:

The absence of a formal assessment is treated as a limitation (Section 10.2), and no study was excluded or down-weighted on risk-of-bias grounds.

| `study_2_R2` | 0.621 | [0.384, 0.857] | 327 | ≈ 3.1 |
| `study_3_R3` | 0.436 | [0.191, 0.681] | 262 | ≈ 2.2 |

Two features of this table merit comment.

**First, all four estimates are positive and all four confidence intervals exclude zero.** Taken purely descriptively, every study with an extractable estimate showed a recognition advantage for MHFA trainees over controls at six months or longer, and in every case that advantage was individually statistically distinguishable from no effect. This descriptive consistency of *sign* is the most robust observation available from this evidence base.

**Second, the estimates vary nearly fourfold in magnitude**, from *g* = 0.279 (Study 5) to *g* = 1.068 (Study 1) — i.e., from a small-to-modest effect to a very large one. Notably, the two studies excluded from native pooling (Studies 1 and 3) bracket the extremes of the distribution, with Study 1 supplying the single largest effect in the dataset. The ordering of effect magnitudes against study size is also worth flagging: the largest study (Study 1, N = 358) reports the largest effect, which is the opposite of the classical small-study-effect pattern, yet Egger's test on this set returns significant asymmetry (Section 8) — a tension discussed below.

Only `study_2_R2` and `study_5_R5` carried the raw inputs needed to construct a log odds ratio and standard error on the native scale. These two studies, totalling N = 601, constitute the primary synthesis set. Their standardized estimates (*g* = 0.621 and *g* = 0.279) are the two smallest of the four available — a composition effect that is relevant when comparing the pooled odds ratio against the full descriptive set, and which plausibly accounts for the pooled native estimate sitting toward the lower end of the extractable range.

A single pre-specified synthesis was performed; the context data contain **no `meta_analysis_groups` entry**, so there is one outcome/timepoint group only (depression vignette recognition at the first follow-up ≥6 months) and no separate per-group results to report.

The pooled **Odds Ratio (OR)** for correct recognition of depression in a vignette at six months or longer, MHFA versus control, was **1.819 (95% CI 1.067 to 3.099), p = 0.0279**, based on **k = 2 studies and N = 601 participants** under a REML random-effects model.

Because `native_pooling_used = true` and the effect measure was set by explicit specification (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`), this value is a **confirmed native-scale odds ratio**, not a standardized fallback or a back-converted approximation. It may be reported and interpreted directly on the odds scale.

### 6.2 Direction of effect

Per the synthesis output's `favors` field, this result **favours neither arm**: it is characterised as *"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"*. This designation governs the interpretation of direction in this report.

This requires careful statement, because the two pieces of information sit in tension and readers deserve both without editorialising. On the one hand, the pooled estimate of 1.819 lies above the null value of 1, its confidence interval excludes 1, and p = 0.0279 — on the pre-specified direction convention (OR > 1 favours MHFA), those numbers would conventionally be read as a statistically significant recognition advantage for MHFA trainees. On the other hand, the authoritative direction field does not attribute the result to either arm, noting that for a ratio measure the favourable direction depends on which event was counted. The practical implication is that **the direction of benefit cannot be asserted as confirmed from this synthesis**; what can be asserted is the magnitude and precision of the ratio itself (1.819; 95% CI 1.067–3.099) and that it is statistically distinguishable from 1. Any claim that MHFA "improves" six-month depression recognition goes beyond what the direction field supports, and should not be made on the strength of this pooled estimate alone.

### 6.3 Magnitude in interpretable terms

On the odds scale, 1.819 corresponds to a log odds ratio of approximately 0.598, equivalent to a standardized mean difference of roughly *g* ≈ 0.33 — a small-to-moderate standardized effect. The confidence interval spans odds ratios from 1.067 to 3.099, i.e., from a marginal 7% relative increase in the odds of correct recognition to a more-than-threefold increase. **This interval is wide and its lower bound sits very close to the null**, which is the expected consequence of pooling only two studies. The point estimate should not be treated as a stable quantity: a single additional trial of comparable size could move it materially.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity in the primary synthesis was **nil**:

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 0.654 (df = 1) | Observed dispersion is smaller than expected under the null of homogeneity |
| Q p-value | 0.4186 | No evidence against homogeneity |
| I² | **0.0%** | None of the observed variability attributable to between-study differences |
| τ² | **0.0** | Zero estimated between-study variance on the log OR scale |

With τ² estimated at exactly zero, the random-effects model collapses to a fixed-effect model: the two contributing studies receive pure inverse-variance weights, and the pooled confidence interval contains no inflation for between-study variance.

Three cautions apply to this apparently reassuring picture.

**(1) Q with one degree of freedom has almost no power.** With k = 2, Cochran's Q is tested on a single degree of freedom, and the probability of detecting even substantial true heterogeneity is very low. A non-significant Q (p = 0.4186) is therefore uninformative about the presence of real between-study variation; it is the expected result whether or not heterogeneity exists.

**(2) I² = 0% and τ² = 0 are boundary estimates, not precise findings.** REML estimation of τ² is bounded below at zero and is highly unstable with two studies; a zero estimate here is best read as "no heterogeneity could be detected", not as "studies are homogeneous". The confidence interval around τ² would be extremely wide and is not reportable from the available output.

**(3) The homogeneity applies only to the two poolable studies, and these are the two most similar of the four.** The full extractable set ranges from *g* = 0.279 to *g* = 1.068 — a spread that, had all four studies been poolable on the native scale, would plausibly have produced non-trivial heterogeneity. The observed I² = 0% is therefore partly an artefact of which studies could be pooled, and should not be generalised to the MHFA recognition literature as a whole.

## 8. Moderator Analyses

**No moderator analyses were performed, and none are interpretable from this dataset.**

With k = 2 studies in the primary synthesis, a meta-regression with even a single covariate would have zero residual degrees of freedom and would be fully saturated — any covariate would "explain" all of the (zero) heterogeneity by construction, producing a meaningless perfect fit. Subgroup analyses are equally impossible: any binary split places one study in each subgroup.

Several moderators were of substantive interest and are reported here as unaddressed evidence gaps rather than as null findings:

- **Delivery format** (e-learning vs face-to-face vs blended) — a question of direct policy relevance given the post-pandemic shift toward online MHFA delivery. Format-level data were not available at the required granularity, and multiple active arms within trials were combined by design, which further precludes this contrast.
- **Exact follow-up duration beyond six months** — the hypothesis of recognition decay predicts attenuation with increasing follow-up length, but the extracted dataset records only whether the ≥6-month threshold was met, not the specific interval.
- **Control condition type** (waiting list vs no training vs delayed training vs attention control) — attention controls would be expected to yield more conservative estimates than pure no-treatment controls.
- **Vignette assessment method** (open-ended vs closed-choice) — closed-choice questions typically produce higher baseline recognition in both arms and thus compressed odds ratios.
- **Population and setting** (workplace, educational, community, health-care).
- **Risk-of-bias domains**, which could not be scored (Section 4).

A moderator bubble chart was generated (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`); with k = 2 contributing to the synthesis and k = 4 effect sizes overall, any trend line in that figure is a geometric artefact of a handful of points and **must not be interpreted as evidence of moderation**.

## 9. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

| Procedure | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | **7.7885** |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.007** |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **0** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (standardized) | *g* = 0.6032 |
| Overall publication-bias risk rating | **High** |

**Egger's regression test indicates statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept = 7.7885, p = 0.007), consistent with small-study effects** — that is, smaller or less precise studies reporting systematically larger effects than larger ones. **Trim-and-fill, however, imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the bias-adjusted pooled estimate is identical to the observed one.**

This discordance between the two procedures is common and does not resolve in favour of the reassuring result. Trim-and-fill has low statistical power, imputes studies on only one side of the funnel, and asymmetry can arise from between-study heterogeneity, differing study quality, or genuine effect modification rather than from suppressed negative results. Given the significant Egger's result, **the overall risk of publication bias is rated high, meaning the pooled effect should be regarded as potentially overestimated despite the null trim-and-fill adjustment.** The absence of imputed studies should not be taken as reassurance. In a GRADE assessment, the certainty of evidence would reasonably be downgraded for publication bias, and the findings would warrant confirmation via alternative approaches such as PET-PEESE, formal selection models, or restriction to larger or pre-registered trials.

Three further caveats are essential to an honest reading of this section.

**First, these bias diagnostics were computed across the four extractable effect sizes, not the two studies in the primary synthesis.** They therefore characterise the broader extractable evidence base rather than the pooled estimate itself. Note also that the adjusted estimate is reported on the standardized (*g* = 0.6032) metric, not the native odds-ratio scale, and so is not directly comparable to the pooled OR of 1.819.

**Second, with k = 4, Egger's test is severely underpowered and its type I error rate is unreliable.** Methodological guidance generally discourages funnel-plot asymmetry testing with fewer than ten studies. The Egger intercept of 7.7885 is very large, which with four points can be driven by a single influential observation. The formal p-value of 0.007 should thus be read as a flag warranting caution, not as a precise quantification of bias.

**Third, the asymmetry does not follow the classical small-study pattern.** In this dataset the *largest* study (Study 1, N = 358) reports the *largest* effect (*g* = 1.068), and the smallest contributing study reports a mid-range effect — the inverse of the usual signature of selective suppression of small null trials. This pattern is more consistent with genuine between-study differences in population, vignette format, or training intensity than with publication bias per se, though with four studies the two explanations cannot be distinguished empirically. The high-risk rating stands, but its most likely practical reading is that **the recognition effect is unstable across study contexts and the true average is probably smaller than the largest reported estimates**, rather than that a hidden file drawer of null MHFA trials has been definitively demonstrated.

## 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-quality issues were recorded during synthesis and are stated plainly here, as they materially constrain every result above.

1. **Two of four extractable effect sizes were excluded from native odds-ratio pooling for lacking the required raw inputs: Study 1 (`study_1_R1`) and Study 3 (`study_3_R3`).** Neither supplied arm-level counts with usable denominators, nor a reported odds ratio with a confidence interval, and so no log odds ratio and standard error could be constructed on the native scale. These studies are described narratively in Section 5 but contribute **nothing** to the pooled estimate. Because Study 1 carried the largest effect in the dataset (*g* = 1.068) and the largest sample (N = 358), its exclusion is not neutral with respect to the pooled value.

2. **The primary synthesis rests on two studies.** k = 2 is at the absolute floor of what can be meta-analysed. All derived quantities — the pooled OR, its confidence interval, τ², I², and Q — are correspondingly fragile, and the synthesis is better understood as a formal combination of two trials than as a stable estimate of a population-average effect.

3. **One eligible study report (Study 4) yielded no usable effect size** for depression vignette recognition at ≥6 months and contributes to no quantitative result. The gap between 5 included studies and 4 effect sizes is attributable entirely to this report.

4. **The effect measure was set by explicit specification** (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`) rather than inferred from the primary data. Native-scale pooling was nonetheless achieved and confirmed (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the reported odds ratio is a genuine native-scale statistic.

5. **Publication-bias statistics were computed on a different and larger set (k = 4) than the pooled estimate (k = 2)**, and the trim-and-fill adjusted value is expressed on the standardized metric. The two sets of numbers are not interchangeable and are not directly comparable.

6. **Planned conversions and adjustments could not be verified as applied.** The protocol provided for flagging continuous-to-log-OR conversions, for substituting randomised denominators where analysed numbers were unclear, and for cluster-adjustment of cluster-randomised trials. The extracted dataset does not record which, if any, of these provisions were invoked for the contributing studies, so the presence of any such conversion in the pooled estimate cannot be confirmed or excluded.

7. **No formal risk-of-bias assessment and no PRISMA database-level flow counts are available** from the pipeline metadata (Sections 2.3 and 4).

## 11. Discussion

### 11.1 Principal findings

This review set out to determine whether MHFA training produces a durable improvement in the recognition of depression from a case vignette at six months or longer. The honest headline is that **the evidence base capable of answering this question quantitatively consists of two poolable trials and 601 participants.**

Within that narrow base, the pooled **Odds Ratio (OR)** was **1.819 (95% CI 1.067–3.099, p = 0.0279)**, with zero detectable heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q = 0.654, p = 0.4186). The direction field classifies this result as favouring **neither arm**, noting that for a ratio measure the favourable direction depends on which event was counted; accordingly, this synthesis does **not** establish a confirmed direction of benefit, even though the ratio itself is above 1 and statistically distinguishable from the null.

Set against that formal result, the descriptive pattern across the wider extractable set is notably consistent: **all four extractable effect sizes were positive with confidence intervals excluding zero**, ranging from *g* = 0.279 to *g* = 1.068. Every study that measured depression recognition at six months or longer and yielded an analysable estimate found trained participants out-recognising controls. That consistency of sign across four independent samples is a meaningful descriptive observation and is arguably more informative than the two-study pooled estimate — but it is descriptive only, it is not a confirmed pooled finding, and it is tempered by a high publication-bias rating and by the fact that the two largest descriptive effects are precisely the ones that could not be pooled.

### 11.2 Relation to the wider literature

The magnitude of the pooled estimate (OR ≈ 1.82, equivalent to *g* ≈ 0.33) is broadly consistent with the modest-but-positive recognition effects reported in prior reviews of MHFA and other gatekeeper-training programmes, which have generally found knowledge and recognition outcomes to be the most reliably improved domain — more so than stigma-related attitudes or actual helping behaviours. The observation that the pooled long-term estimate sits toward the lower end of the range of individual estimates is also consistent with the decay hypothesis: immediate post-training recognition gains in MHFA trials are typically substantially larger than those observed at six to twelve months.

It bears emphasis that this synthesis deliberately restricted attention to follow-ups at or beyond six months and to a single, behaviourally proximal outcome. The resulting small k is a direct consequence of that restriction, not an indication that the MHFA literature is small overall. Many MHFA trials measure recognition only immediately post-training or at short follow-up, and those trials — however numerous — are silent on durability.

### 11.3 Implications for practice and policy

Three implications follow, each appropriately hedged.

**For commissioners,** this review does not provide a sufficient evidential basis for claiming that MHFA delivers durable improvements in depression recognition. The pooled result derives from two trials, has a confidence interval whose lower bound nearly touches the null, is rated at high risk of publication bias, and is classified as favouring neither arm. Procurement decisions resting on long-term recognition gains should recognise that this specific claim is, at present, under-evidenced rather than refuted.

**For programme designers,** the consistently positive direction of all four descriptive estimates alongside a pooled estimate at the lower end of that range is compatible with — though far from proof of — partial attenuation of recognition gains over time. This is the pattern that refresher or booster training is designed to address, and it would be a reasonable target for direct experimental test.

**For the vignette outcome itself,** recognition of depression in a hypothetical vignette is a proxy for, not a measure of, real-world first-aid behaviour. Even a securely established recognition advantage would leave open whether trainees act differently when encountering a distressed colleague, student or family member. Outcomes capturing actual helping behaviour and recipient-level benefit remain the critical evidence gap.

### 11.4 Implications for research

The clearest need is for **adequately reported trials with long-term follow-up**. Half the extractable effect sizes in this review were unusable for native-scale pooling purely because of reporting deficiencies — missing arm-level counts, unclear denominators, or effect estimates presented without confidence intervals. These are entirely remediable. Trialists should report, for every vignette recognition outcome and every timepoint: the number analysed per arm, the number correctly recognising per arm, and the resulting odds ratio with its confidence interval (cluster-adjusted where relevant). Pre-registration with specified timepoints would additionally reduce the selective-reporting pressures that the Egger result flags. Direct head-to-head comparison of delivery formats, and trials that extend follow-up to twelve and twenty-four months, would address the moderator questions this review was entirely unable to examine.

## 12. Limitations

**12.1 Review-level limitations.** The search is reported at the level of study reports entering extraction rather than as a database-by-database PRISMA flow, and per-database record counts are unavailable; the completeness of retrieval therefore cannot be independently verified. Study-level bibliographic identifiers were not carried through the pipeline, so included studies are referenced only by internal identifiers, limiting readers' ability to audit the included set. Screening and extraction were performed within an automated pipeline, and independent dual screening with inter-rater reliability statistics is not documented. No protocol was prospectively registered.

**12.2 Risk-of-bias limitations.** No formal domain-level risk-of-bias assessment (RoB 2, ROBINS-I) was completed, and no sensitivity analysis by study quality was possible. Blinding of participants to allocation is generally infeasible in training trials, and the vignette outcome is self-reported, so detection bias cannot be excluded. Long-term attrition — characteristically high in ≥6-month training follow-ups — could not be quantified, and the analysis plan's provision for substituting randomised for analysed denominators indicates that denominator ambiguity was expected in at least some reports.

**12.3 Statistical limitations.** The primary synthesis pools **k = 2 studies**, the minimum possible. Consequently: τ² and I² are boundary estimates with no meaningful precision; Cochran's Q is tested on one degree of freedom with negligible power; the REML random-effects model is effectively a fixed-effect model; no moderator or subgroup analysis is possible; influence and leave-one-out diagnostics are uninformative (each omission leaves a single study); and the cumulative plot has only two steps. Publication-bias diagnostics were computed on k = 4, far below the conventional minimum of ten studies for funnel-asymmetry testing, rendering both the Egger p-value and the trim-and-fill null result unreliable; the two procedures additionally disagree, and the adjusted estimate is reported on a different (standardized) metric from the pooled OR.

**12.4 Data-availability limitations.** Two of four extractable effect sizes (Studies 1 and 3) were excluded from pooling for lacking raw inputs, including the study with both the largest sample and the largest effect. One eligible study (Study 4) yielded no usable estimate at all. Whether the planned continuous-to-log-OR conversion, denominator substitution, or cluster-adjustment provisions were invoked for any contributing study cannot be confirmed from the available metadata.

**12.5 Interpretive limitations.** The `favors` field classifies the pooled result as favouring neither arm, which stands in tension with a statistically significant OR above 1. This report presents both facts without resolving the tension, because the available data do not permit resolution. Readers should not infer a confirmed direction of benefit from the numerical sign of the pooled estimate.

**12.6 Generalisability.** With two studies in the synthesis, the populations, settings, delivery formats, control conditions and exact follow-up intervals represented are necessarily narrow and unspecified in the extracted data. Extrapolation to MHFA as delivered in any particular jurisdiction, sector or format is not supported.

## 13. Conclusion

Five study reports met the eligibility criteria for this review of whether MHFA training improves correct recognition of depression in a case vignette at six months or longer; four yielded extractable effect sizes, and only **two** provided the raw data required for pooling on the pre-specified native odds-ratio scale.

The pooled **Odds Ratio (OR)** from those two trials (N = 601) was **1.819 (95% CI 1.067 to 3.099, p = 0.0279)**, estimated under a REML random-effects model with no detectable heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0; Q = 0.654, df = 1, p = 0.4186). Native-scale pooling was confirmed, so this is a genuine odds ratio rather than a standardized fallback. In accordance with the synthesis output's direction field, the result **favours neither arm** — it is characterised as essentially a tie, or as a ratio measure whose favourability depends on which event was counted — and a direction of benefit therefore cannot be asserted from this synthesis.

Descriptively, all four extractable effect sizes were positive with confidence intervals excluding zero (*g* = 0.279 to 1.068), indicating that every analysable trial found a recognition advantage for MHFA trainees at long-term follow-up. This consistency is encouraging but is not a confirmed pooled finding; it is offset by a **high** rated risk of publication bias (Egger's p = 0.007, albeit from only four effect sizes, with trim-and-fill imputing no studies), by the exclusion of the two largest descriptive effects from the pooled analysis, and by the absence of any formal risk-of-bias assessment.

**Bottom line:** the available evidence is too sparse and too incompletely reported to support a confident conclusion that MHFA training produces durable improvements in depression recognition at six months or beyond. The pooled odds ratio of 1.819 is best regarded as provisional, potentially overestimated, and in urgent need of corroboration from larger, pre-registered trials that report arm-level recognition counts and denominators at clearly specified long-term timepoints. The certainty of evidence for this outcome is **very low**.
