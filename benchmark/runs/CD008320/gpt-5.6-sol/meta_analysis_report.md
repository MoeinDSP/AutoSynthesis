# Kappa-Opioid Agonists Versus Placebo for Uremic Pruritus: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Visual Analogue Scale Severity

## Abstract

**Background:** Kappa-opioid receptor agonists, including difelikefalin, nalbuphine, and nalfurafine, have been evaluated for uremic pruritus. This review assessed their class effect versus placebo on pruritus severity measured using a 0–10 cm visual analogue scale (VAS), for which lower scores indicate improvement.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons were parallel-group, placebo-controlled studies enrolling participants with uremic pruritus and reporting post-treatment or consistently poolable change scores for pruritus severity. Equivalent VAS units were intended to be harmonized to 0–10 cm. Kappa-opioid agonists were pooled as one drug class. Native mean differences (MDs) were synthesized using a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were explored using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill, subject to the severe limitations imposed by the small evidence base.

**Results:** Five studies were represented in the pipeline, but only three effect sizes (total N=468) were available, and only two studies (N=294) supplied the raw inputs required for native MD pooling. The pooled mean difference was −7.75277405986832 (95% CI −13.118 to −2.387; p=0.0046259638959165374), favoring kappa-opioid agonist therapy. No observed statistical heterogeneity was detected (Q=0.457, p=0.4991; I²=0.0%; τ²=0.0). Three available standardized study effects were negative: −0.303277 (95% CI −0.556 to −0.050; N=243), −0.465645 (95% CI −1.022 to 0.091; N=51), and −0.560216 (95% CI −0.905 to −0.216; N=174). Egger’s intercept was −0.1728 (p=0.4741), while trim-and-fill estimated two potentially missing studies and produced an adjusted standardized estimate of −0.3033. Overall publication-bias risk was classified as moderate.

**Conclusions:** The pooled analysis statistically favored kappa-opioid agonists over placebo, but confidence in the magnitude is limited. Most importantly, the native MD and its confidence limits appear inconsistent with a correctly harmonized 0–10 cm VAS, necessitating verification of unit conversion and source extraction before clinical interpretation. The very small number of poolable studies, exclusion of one study from native MD synthesis, unavailable study-level bibliographic and design details, and underpowered publication-bias analyses further constrain inference.

## Introduction

Uremic pruritus is a burdensome symptom among people with advanced kidney disease, particularly those receiving dialysis. Kappa-opioid receptor agonism has been investigated as a therapeutic strategy, with agents including difelikefalin, nalbuphine, and nalfurafine. This review addressed the following question: among participants with uremic pruritus, what is the effect of any kappa-opioid agonist, compared with placebo, on pruritus severity measured using a 0–10 cm visual analogue scale (VAS) in parallel-group studies?

All eligible agonists were treated as one therapeutic class. Lower raw VAS scores represent less severe pruritus and therefore a better outcome. The prespecified direction of benefit was such that an effect favoring the active-treatment arm indicates lower pruritus severity with kappa-opioid agonist therapy.

## Meta-Analytic Result

Using a REML random-effects model, the **pooled mean difference** across two studies (N=294) was **−7.75277405986832** (95% CI **−13.118 to −2.387**; p=**0.0046259638959165374**). According to the supplied direction field, this result **favors kappa-opioid agonist therapy (all eligible drugs pooled as one class)** over placebo.

The confidence interval excludes the null, indicating a statistically significant pooled difference under the fitted model. Nevertheless, the magnitude should not be given a direct clinical interpretation in centimeters without validation. A treatment-minus-placebo difference of −7.7528 on a genuine 0–10 cm VAS would be exceptionally large, and the lower confidence limit of −13.118 lies outside the possible difference implied by two bounded 0–10 scales. Although the analysis was identified as native MD pooling, these values strongly suggest that at least some input may have remained on another scale—potentially a 0–100 unit scale—or that a unit conversion or extraction error occurred. The supplied source data are insufficient to determine the cause. Accordingly, the direction and statistical result can be reported, but the claimed 0–10 cm magnitude requires audit against the trial reports.

## Heterogeneity

Observed statistical heterogeneity was absent:

- Cochran’s Q = **0.457**;
- Q-test p = **0.4991**;
- I² = **0.0%**;
- τ² = **0.0**.

These statistics indicate that the two included native MD estimates did not display more variability than expected from sampling error under this model. They do **not** establish clinical or methodological homogeneity. With k=2, Q has very low power, I² is imprecisely estimated, and τ² can readily be estimated as zero even when genuine between-study variation exists. Moreover, different drugs were intended to be pooled as a class, and the absence of study-level intervention and design details prevents assessment of important clinical diversity.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s test yielded an intercept of **−0.1728** with p=**0.4741**, providing no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry. Trim-and-fill estimated **two potentially missing studies** and returned an adjusted standardized estimate of **−0.3033**. The supplied overall publication-bias classification was **moderate risk**.

These analyses should not be overinterpreted. Only three standardized effect-size records were available, far below the number generally considered sufficient for reliable funnel-plot asymmetry testing. Egger’s test is severely underpowered in this setting, and trim-and-fill can be unstable when k is very small. Furthermore, the trim-and-fill estimate is on a standardized scale, whereas the principal synthesis used native MDs; it is therefore not a directly comparable adjusted version of the pooled MD. The appropriate conclusion is that publication bias and small-study effects cannot be excluded, despite the non-significant Egger test.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

The REML random-effects synthesis of two parallel-group placebo comparisons found a statistically significant pooled mean difference favoring the kappa-opioid agonist class. The study-level standardized estimates were all in the same direction, although one had a confidence interval crossing the null. No observed statistical heterogeneity was detected in the two-study native MD analysis.

The apparent consistency should be balanced against three major concerns. First, only two of five pipeline studies contributed to the native-scale synthesis. Second, agents with potentially different pharmacologic and delivery characteristics were intended to be treated as a single class, but there were too few studies to test whether a class effect was a defensible assumption. Third, the reported MD is not plausibly interpretable on a correctly harmonized 0–10 cm VAS without further verification.

### Clinical interpretation

If the scale and extraction were correct, the pooled estimate would indicate markedly lower pruritus severity with kappa-opioid agonist therapy than with placebo. However, because the point estimate and confidence interval conflict with the expected bounds of the target scale, translating this result into a clinical statement such as a 7.75 cm average improvement would be unsafe. One plausible explanation is that source outcomes were analyzed in millimeters or on a 0–100 scale without conversion to centimeters or 0–10 units; if so, the numerical estimate would need division by 10. This is only a hypothesis and must not be treated as a corrected result without checking the source values.

The synthesis also does not establish that every kappa-opioid agonist has the same effect. Pooling as a drug class answers the specified research question but can obscure agent-specific efficacy, dose-response patterns, and differences among populations or treatment durations.

### Strength and certainty of the evidence

The direction of the pooled finding favors treatment and its model-based confidence interval excludes no effect. Nevertheless, certainty is constrained by sparse data, incomplete study reporting, unexplained attrition from five pipeline studies to two native-scale studies, inability to assess risk of bias, possible outcome-scale error, and possible publication bias. No formal GRADE assessment can be completed from the supplied information because study-level risk of bias, consistency across clinically comparable settings, directness, and reporting completeness were not documented.

## Limitations

1. **Sparse quantitative evidence:** Only two studies, totaling 294 participants, were included in the native MD meta-analysis.
2. **Incomplete use of the identified evidence:** Five studies were represented in the pipeline, three yielded effect-size records, and one of those three lacked the inputs required for native MD pooling.
3. **Potential scale-harmonization problem:** The reported pooled MD and lower confidence limit are incompatible with straightforward interpretation on a bounded 0–10 cm VAS.
4. **Unavailable study characteristics:** Drug, dose, route, setting, participant characteristics, follow-up, and outcome timing were not provided.
5. **Unknown endpoint structure:** It was not reported whether the two pooled effects were both post-treatment values, both changes from baseline, or otherwise demonstrably consistent.
6. **No study-level risk-of-bias assessment:** Randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, missing data, selective reporting, and other bias domains could not be evaluated.
7. **Class pooling:** Combining different kappa-opioid agonists assumes sufficient clinical and pharmacologic comparability, which could not be tested.
8. **Uninformative heterogeneity tests:** With k=2, I²=0% and τ²=0 do not rule out meaningful between-study heterogeneity.
9. **Unreliable small-study-effect diagnostics:** Egger’s test and trim-and-fill are not dependable with only three standardized effects.
10. **No subgroup or moderator analysis:** Potential differences by drug, dose, dialysis status, severity, and duration remain unresolved.
11. **Incomplete search documentation:** Search sources, dates, terms, screening procedures, and reasons for exclusion were unavailable, precluding full assessment of review completeness and reproducibility.
12. **No included-study citations:** Bibliographic details for the five pipeline studies were not supplied, so the primary reports cannot be listed or independently verified here.

## Conclusion

In a REML random-effects meta-analysis of two studies (N=294), the pooled mean difference was −7.75277405986832 (95% CI −13.118 to −2.387; p=0.0046259638959165374), favoring kappa-opioid agonist therapy over placebo for uremic pruritus. Observed heterogeneity was zero (I²=0.0%; τ²=0.0), but the evidence base was too small for this to demonstrate true homogeneity. Publication bias could not be excluded and was classified as moderate risk.

The result suggests a beneficial class effect, but its magnitude is not clinically interpretable as a 0–10 cm VAS difference until the original scale, unit conversions, and extracted raw inputs are verified. A definitive update should retrieve all eligible reports, document the search and selection process, validate outcome units, separate incompatible endpoint types or timepoints, assess risk of bias, and examine individual drugs when enough studies are available.
