# Probiotics for Self-Reported Improvement in Constipation: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Probiotics are used as a conservative treatment for constipation, but their effect on participant-reported improvement is uncertain.

### Objective
To estimate the effect of probiotics, compared with no active treatment, placebo, or usual care, on the proportion of people reporting improvement in constipation. Assessment-based nursing interventions and other distinct conservative interventions were outside the review scope.

### Methods
Four studies (286 participants) contributed four reported effect-size records. The prespecified outcome was dichotomous participant-level improvement, and the requested native effect measure was the risk ratio (RR). Native RR pooling was possible for three studies (166 participants); one study (120 participants) was excluded from that pooling because the raw inputs required to calculate an RR were unavailable. A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used.

### Results
Across the three studies eligible for native pooling, the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 3.1631331852273954 (95% CI 1.289 to 7.761; p = 0.011910628814440827). The analysis system classified favorability as “neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted),” because the counted-event orientation was not confirmed in the supplied analytic output. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 64.34%; τ² = 0.397763; Q = 5.99, p = 0.05). Egger’s intercept was 0.1465 (p = 0.5092), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0); however, publication-bias tests were severely underpowered given the small evidence base.

### Conclusions
The native random-effects analysis produced an RR statistically different from the null, but the direction of clinical benefit cannot be stated definitively from the supplied output because event coding was not verified. Substantial heterogeneity, omission of one study from RR pooling, absent arm-level event counts, and the very small number of studies materially limit certainty.

The review question was: among people with constipation, what is the effect of probiotics, compared with no active treatment, placebo, or usual care, on participant-reported improvement in constipation? Eligibility was restricted to probiotic interventions. Assessment-based nursing interventions and other distinct conservative intervention types were excluded to preserve clinical comparability.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled three studies and 166 participants on the native RR scale. The **pooled Risk Ratio (RR)** was **3.1631331852273954** (95% CI **1.289 to 7.761**; p = **0.011910628814440827**).

Native pooling was used, and the effect measure was identified from the explicit user request. Nevertheless, the supplied `favors` classification was **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).”** Accordingly, this report does not reassign the result to either treatment arm based solely on the numerical RR. The result shows a statistically detectable relative difference in the counted event, but whether that event was consistently “improvement” rather than its complement was not confirmed in the analytic output.

The confidence interval was wide, ranging from 1.289 to 7.761, indicating considerable uncertainty about the magnitude of the relative effect. The cumulative meta-analysis figure is available at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/cumulative_plot.png`.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- **I² = 64.34%**
- **τ² = 0.397763**
- **Cochran’s Q = 5.99**
- **Q-test p = 0.05**

Thus, approximately 64.34% of the observed variability was attributed to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error under the assumptions of the model. With only three pooled studies, however, I², τ², and the Q test are imprecisely estimated. The Q-test value at p = 0.05 should not be treated as a precise threshold-based distinction between homogeneous and heterogeneous evidence.

No prediction interval was supplied. Such an interval would have been clinically useful because it would describe the range of effects expected in a new comparable study, particularly given the observed heterogeneity.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **0.1465** with **p = 0.5092**. The test was not statistically significant, providing no detected evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies and reported an adjusted standardized value of **0.9124** (`trim_fill_g`). The supplied classification of publication-bias risk was **low**.

These findings require strong qualification. Publication-bias methods are unreliable with very few studies, and conventional guidance generally discourages formal funnel-plot asymmetry testing when fewer than 10 studies are available. Moreover, the context does not establish whether these diagnostics used the three-study native RR set or all four standardized effect records. The trim-and-fill standardized value should not be interpreted as an adjusted RR. Therefore, the absence of detected asymmetry is not evidence that reporting or publication bias is absent.

## Discussion

The available native-scale synthesis included three trials with 166 participants. It yielded a pooled RR of 3.1631331852273954, with a 95% CI from 1.289 to 7.761 and p = 0.011910628814440827. This is statistically inconsistent with the ratio null of 1. However, the supplied analytic classification did not verify which event was counted and therefore classified favorability as neither/indeterminate. A clinically directional conclusion about improvement cannot safely be made without confirming that all numerators represent participants who improved.

The magnitude also varied materially across studies, as reflected by I² = 64.34% and τ² = 0.397763. Clinical heterogeneity is plausible because probiotic effects may differ by organism, strain, dose, formulation, treatment duration, baseline constipation phenotype, and outcome definition. None of these characteristics was available for examination. Variation in participant-level improvement criteria is especially important: combining studies that used different thresholds or time points may produce a statistically valid ratio for each study while pooling clinically non-equivalent events.

The excluded fourth study is consequential. It represented 120 of 286 participants, or 41.96% of the total sample represented in the supplied records, and could not enter native RR pooling because its raw dichotomous inputs were unavailable. Although its reported standardized estimate was Hedges’ g = 0.670405 (95% CI 0.281 to 1.059), that value cannot be inserted into an RR meta-analysis. Excluding a large study may affect precision, magnitude, and representativeness; the direction and size of that effect cannot be determined without its event data.

The statistical significance of the pooled result should not be equated with certainty or clinical importance. The confidence interval is broad, the evidence base is small, heterogeneity is substantial, and no risk-of-bias assessments were supplied. In addition, no absolute risks were available, so the absolute increase or decrease in improvement, risk difference, and number needed to treat could not be calculated. Those absolute measures are important for clinical decision-making because the practical implications of an RR depend on the baseline probability of improvement under control conditions.

## Data Quality & Analytic Consistency Notes

The supplied results contain an important measure mismatch. The primary synthesis is explicitly a native **Risk Ratio (RR)** analysis, but the four displayed study-level effects are standardized Hedges’ g values. One record—study_3—was explicitly excluded from native RR pooling because raw inputs were lacking. No arm-level numbers improved or randomized were supplied for any study, even though these are required for independent RR verification.

The pooled analysis reports `k = 3` and `n_total = 166`, which is arithmetically consistent with the sample sizes of study_1, study_2, and study_4 (46 + 48 + 72 = 166). Across all four records, the corresponding total is 286. This confirms which sample was omitted but does not resolve event coding or reproduce the individual RRs.

The publication-bias output also mixes native and standardized terminology: the primary analysis is an RR, whereas trim-and-fill reports `trim_fill_g = 0.9124`. These values should remain analytically separate. No sensitivity analysis was supplied to evaluate the consequences of alternative estimators, exclusion decisions, continuity corrections, or outcome coding.

## Limitations

1. **Incomplete search reporting:** Databases, dates, search strategies, screening counts, excluded reports, and protocol information were unavailable, preventing assessment of search completeness and PRISMA flow.
2. **Missing event data:** Numbers improved and randomized by arm were not supplied, preventing reconstruction and validation of study-specific RRs.
3. **Unverified event orientation:** The analysis output did not confirm that the counted event was consistently improvement, so the direction of favorability cannot be assigned safely.
4. **Partial native-scale synthesis:** Only three of four studies entered the RR meta-analysis; the excluded study accounted for 120 participants and 41.96% of the represented sample.
5. **Effect-measure mismatch:** Study-level values were reported as Hedges’ g, whereas the pooled native measure was RR. These metrics cannot be combined or compared directly.
6. **Small evidence base:** With k = 3 in the pooled analysis, the summary effect and heterogeneity estimates are unstable, and publication-bias tests have very low power.
7. **Substantial heterogeneity:** I² was 64.34%, but potential clinical and methodological sources could not be assessed.
8. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** Randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, missing data, selective reporting, and other trial-level biases were not reported.
9. **No absolute effect estimates:** Control-group risks were unavailable, so risk differences and numbers needed to treat could not be estimated.
10. **No certainty assessment:** The supplied information was insufficient for a formal GRADE assessment.
11. **No study metadata:** Trial populations, probiotic regimens, comparators, improvement definitions, and follow-up times were unavailable, limiting applicability.

## Conclusion

In a REML random-effects synthesis of three studies involving 166 participants, the pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** was **3.1631331852273954** (95% CI **1.289 to 7.761**; p = **0.011910628814440827**), with substantial heterogeneity (**I² = 64.34%; τ² = 0.397763; Q = 5.99, p = 0.05**). The supplied analysis classified the result as favoring neither arm because event orientation was not confirmed. Therefore, despite statistical evidence of a relative difference in the counted event, the available output does not support an unqualified conclusion that probiotics increase self-reported improvement.

The evidence should be considered uncertain because one large study could not be pooled on the RR scale, arm-level event data were absent, study-level effects were reported in a different metric, and only three studies informed the native synthesis. Future updates should retrieve the randomized denominators and improvement-event counts for every arm, verify consistent event coding and time points, assess trial risk of bias, and report both relative and absolute effects.
