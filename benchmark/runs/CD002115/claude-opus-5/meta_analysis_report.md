# Probiotics and Self-Reported Improvement in Constipation: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomized Comparisons Against Placebo, Usual Care, or No Active Treatment

## Abstract

**Background.** Constipation is a highly prevalent functional gastrointestinal complaint, and probiotic supplementation is widely marketed and self-administered for its relief. Dichotomous, patient-centred endpoints — the proportion of participants who report that their constipation has improved — are arguably the most clinically meaningful outcomes in this field, yet they are less frequently synthesised than continuous stool-frequency measures.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of probiotic supplementation (any strain, dose, or formulation) compared with no active treatment, placebo, or usual care on the proportion of participants reporting improvement in constipation, pooled as a Risk Ratio (RR).

**Methods.** A systematic search and structured screening pipeline identified randomized comparisons reporting the number of participants reporting improvement (patient-rated relief, global improvement, or "treatment success") out of the total randomized per arm. Four studies entered the review; three contributed extractable dichotomous improvement data and were pooled. Effects were synthesised on the native risk-ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression test and the trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** Across 3 pooled studies (N = 214 participants), the pooled Risk Ratio for self-reported improvement in constipation was **RR = 2.120 (95% CI 1.472 to 3.053; p = 5.43 × 10⁻⁵)**. Native risk-ratio pooling was used (native_pooling_used = true), so this is a confirmed native-scale result rather than a standardized fallback. Between-study heterogeneity was nil: Q = 2.52 (df = 2, p = 0.2836), I² = 0.0%, τ² = 1 × 10⁻⁶. Egger's regression showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = −0.0835, p = 0.149) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), consistent with a low assessed risk of publication bias. The direction field accompanying the pooled estimate is reported as "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)", reflecting that for a ratio of a desirable event the favoured arm cannot be assigned automatically from the sign convention; under the review's pre-specified effect direction (RR > 1 = more participants improved on probiotics), an RR of 2.120 corresponds to approximately twice the proportion of improvement reporters in the probiotic arms.

**Conclusions.** In a small but statistically homogeneous evidence base, probiotic supplementation was associated with a markedly and statistically significantly higher risk ratio for self-reported improvement in constipation relative to placebo, usual care, or no active treatment. The estimate rests on only 3 pooled studies and 214 participants, with an unavoidably wide confidence interval and limited power to detect bias, so it should be treated as promising but provisional.

## 1. Introduction

Chronic constipation affects a substantial minority of adults worldwide and is associated with reduced health-related quality of life, high rates of self-medication, and considerable primary-care and over-the-counter expenditure. Pharmacological laxatives are effective for many patients but are frequently discontinued because of tolerability concerns, loss of perceived efficacy, or reluctance to use long-term medication. This has sustained strong interest in dietary and microbiota-targeted approaches, of which probiotic supplementation is the most widely available.

The biological rationale is plausible: probiotic organisms — commonly *Bifidobacterium* and *Lactobacillus* species, alone or in multi-strain formulations — may influence colonic transit through modulation of luminal pH, short-chain fatty acid production, bile salt metabolism, intestinal motility signalling, and gut–brain axis mediators. Trials in this area, however, have been heterogeneous in strain, dose, delivery vehicle, duration, and above all in outcome definition.

Much of the existing synthesis literature has focused on continuous endpoints such as bowel movements per week or stool consistency scores. These are objective and easily pooled, but they do not necessarily capture what matters most to patients. Regulatory and patient-centred-outcomes guidance increasingly favours **responder analyses** — the proportion of participants who themselves report that their condition has improved. This review therefore deliberately restricts attention to the dichotomous, patient-reported improvement endpoint and pools it as a **Risk Ratio (RR)**, the most directly interpretable measure for a binary benefit outcome.

### 1.1 Research question

> What is the effect of probiotics, compared with no active treatment, placebo, or usual care, on self-reported improvement in constipation (proportion of participants reporting improvement)?

- **Population:** Participants with constipation (functional, chronic, or otherwise self-reported) enrolled in randomized comparisons.
- **Intervention:** Probiotic supplementation — any strain, dose, or formulation.
- **Comparator:** No active treatment, placebo, or usual care.
- **Outcome:** Number of participants in each arm reporting improvement (patient-rated relief, global improvement, or "treatment success") out of the total randomized per arm.
- **Effect measure:** Risk Ratio, pooled under a random-effects model.
- **Pre-specified effect direction:** RR > 1 indicates that a larger proportion of participants receiving probiotics reported improvement, i.e. probiotics favour improvement.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Information sources and search strategy

Records were retrieved and processed through a structured systematic-review pipeline designed to operationalise PRISMA 2020 screening logic. The search targeted randomized or controlled comparisons of probiotic supplementation against placebo, usual care, or no active treatment in participants with constipation, with emphasis on reports containing arm-level counts of participants reporting improvement.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Inclusion.** (i) Randomized or controlled comparison; (ii) probiotic supplementation of any strain, dose, or formulation as the intervention arm; (iii) placebo, usual care, or no active treatment as comparator; (iv) a dichotomous, participant-reported improvement endpoint (global relief, patient-rated improvement, or treatment success) with extractable numerators and denominators or a convertible effect estimate per arm.

**Exclusion.** (i) Narrative or systematic reviews, editorials, and commentaries, which were removed at the document-classification stage; (ii) duplicate reports of the same trial; (iii) studies reporting only continuous endpoints (stool frequency, consistency scores, transit time) with no dichotomous improvement outcome; (iv) single-arm or uncontrolled designs.

### 2.3 Screening outcome

The screening and extraction pipeline yielded **4 included studies**, of which **3 provided effect sizes** that could be placed on the pooled risk-ratio scale for the self-reported improvement outcome. The fourth included study did not contribute a usable dichotomous improvement effect size — it is retained in the qualitative review and in the count of included studies, but it is **not** represented in the quantitative synthesis. All reported pooled statistics below therefore refer to **k = 3**.

Because the pipeline identified a single outcome construct at a single analytic timepoint, no `meta_analysis_groups` partition was produced: there is one meta-analytic group, and no subgroup was flagged as having insufficient data for pooling.

## 3. Included Studies

Three studies contributed to the quantitative synthesis, together randomizing **214 participants**. Sample sizes ranged from 46 to 120, with a median of 48. All three compared probiotic supplementation against an inactive comparator (placebo, usual care, or no active treatment) and reported the proportion of participants who reported improvement in constipation.

| Study label | N (total, both arms) | Contributed to pooled RR | Direction of individual effect |
|---|---|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | 46 | Yes | Favours improvement with probiotics |
| study_2_R2 | 48 | Yes | Favours improvement with probiotics |
| study_3_R3 | 120 | Yes | Favours improvement with probiotics |
| Fourth included study | — | No (no extractable dichotomous improvement data) | Not estimable |
| **Total pooled** | **214** | **k = 3** | — |

The evidence base is small. Two of the three pooled trials enrolled fewer than 50 participants in total, meaning that the pooled estimate is dominated by study_3_R3 (120 participants, 56% of the pooled sample), which also carries the greatest inverse-variance weight and the narrowest individual confidence interval.

Study-level descriptive detail on strain identity, daily colony-forming-unit dose, treatment duration, and participant age distribution was not uniformly recoverable through the extraction pipeline; consequently, the clinical characterisation of this body of evidence is thinner than the statistical characterisation, and the pooled estimate should be read as applying to "probiotics, broadly defined" rather than to any specific formulation.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure and scale

The review question specified the **Risk Ratio (RR)** as the target effect measure, and pooling was performed on the native risk-ratio scale (`measure_label` = "Risk Ratio (RR)"; `computation_scale` = RR; `native_pooling_used` = **true**). The pooled value is therefore a **confirmed native-scale risk ratio**, not a standardized fallback converted from another metric.

One documented measure conflict should be stated explicitly: the effect measure source is recorded as `explicit_user_request`, and the pipeline flagged that **the source paper(s) explicitly report Odds Ratios (OR) rather than Risk Ratios**. The user-specified RR was retained as authoritative. For a common outcome such as self-reported improvement — where event rates in the comparator arm are typically well above 10–20% — an odds ratio is numerically further from the null than the corresponding risk ratio. Readers should keep in mind that the reconciliation of the originally reported OR onto the requested RR scale is an analytic decision of this review, not a property of the primary reports.

### 4.2 Study-level estimates

For plotting and bias diagnostics, study-level effects were additionally expressed on a standardized (log-ratio-equivalent) scale. The study-level values supplied by the pipeline were:

| Study label | Standardized effect | 95% CI | N |
|---|---|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | 1.321 | [0.683, 1.959] | 46 |
| study_2_R2 | 0.832 | [0.240, 1.423] | 48 |
| study_3_R3 | 0.670 | [0.281, 1.059] | 120 |

All three study-level confidence intervals exclude the null, and all three point estimates lie in the same direction — more participants reported improvement in the probiotic arms than in the comparator arms. There is a visible inverse relationship between sample size and effect magnitude (the smallest trial, study_1_R1, shows the largest effect; the largest trial, study_3_R3, the smallest), which is the classical pattern associated with small-study effects and is examined formally in Section 8.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects meta-analysis with REML estimation of between-study variance was fitted to the 3 studies on the native risk-ratio scale.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Risk Ratio (RR)** |
| Native pooling used | Yes (confirmed native-scale result) |
| Studies pooled (k) | 3 |
| Participants (N) | 214 |
| **Pooled Risk Ratio** | **2.120** |
| 95% confidence interval | **1.472 to 3.053** |
| p-value | **5.426 × 10⁻⁵** |
| Model | Random effects (REML) |
| Favours (as reported by the analysis) | "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)" |

The pooled Risk Ratio is **2.120 (95% CI 1.472–3.053, p = 5.43 × 10⁻⁵)**. The confidence interval excludes 1.0 by a comfortable margin, and the p-value is well below conventional thresholds.

**On direction.** The analysis's own `favors` field returns "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)". This is the authoritative direction label and is reported here as such: for a ratio measure, the software does not assign a favoured arm automatically, because whether a ratio above 1 is good or bad depends entirely on whether the counted event is desirable (improvement) or undesirable (an adverse event). It should not be read as meaning the two arms performed identically — the pooled estimate is far from the null and highly statistically significant. Under this review's **pre-specified** effect direction, where the counted event is *improvement* and RR > 1 means probiotics favour improvement, a pooled RR of 2.120 indicates that participants allocated to probiotics were approximately **2.1 times as likely** to report improvement in their constipation as participants allocated to placebo, usual care, or no active treatment. Expressed as a plausible range, the data are compatible with anywhere from a 47% relative increase (RR 1.472) to a roughly three-fold increase (RR 3.053) in the proportion of improvement reporters.

The forest plot for this analysis is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. A cumulative meta-analysis, ordered to show how the pooled estimate evolves as successive studies are added, is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; the cumulative trajectory stabilises once the largest trial enters, which is the expected behaviour when the largest study is also the most precise and the most conservative.

## 6. Heterogeneity

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 2.52 (df = 2) | Observed dispersion |
| Q p-value | 0.2836 | No significant heterogeneity |
| I² | **0.0%** | No observed variance attributable to between-study differences |
| τ² | 1 × 10⁻⁶ | Effectively zero between-study variance |

Heterogeneity was **absent by every available criterion**. Cochran's Q of 2.52 on 2 degrees of freedom is almost exactly what would be expected under the null hypothesis of a common underlying effect (expected Q = df = 2), and the associated p-value of 0.2836 provides no evidence against homogeneity. I² is 0.0%, and the REML estimate of τ² is 1 × 10⁻⁶ — numerically indistinguishable from zero. In consequence, the random-effects and fixed-effect estimates would be essentially identical here, and the confidence interval is not inflated by any between-study variance component.

Two important caveats temper this reassuring picture. First, **Q and I² are severely underpowered with k = 3**; a non-significant Q test in three studies is weak evidence of homogeneity rather than strong evidence against heterogeneity, and the confidence interval around I² would be extremely wide if estimated. Second, statistical homogeneity is not clinical homogeneity: three trials can produce concordant numerical effects while differing substantially in strain, dose, duration, and improvement definition. The apparent consistency should therefore be understood as "no detectable inconsistency in a very small sample" rather than as demonstrated equivalence of effect across formulations.

A graphical summary of the heterogeneity diagnostics is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 7. Moderators

No moderator or subgroup analysis was conducted, and none could be defensibly conducted. With k = 3 studies and τ² estimated at effectively zero, there is no between-study variance for a moderator to explain, and any meta-regression would have at most one residual degree of freedom — far below the commonly cited minimum of roughly ten studies per covariate. Formal tests of probiotic strain, dose, treatment duration, comparator type (placebo versus usual care versus no treatment), or improvement-definition stringency as effect modifiers were therefore not attempted, and any such analysis would be uninterpretable rather than merely imprecise.

The only quasi-moderator observation available is descriptive and informal: study-level effect magnitude decreased monotonically with increasing sample size across the three trials (1.321 at N = 46; 0.832 at N = 48; 0.670 at N = 120 on the standardized scale). This pattern is visualised in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`). It is consistent with either genuine small-study effects, chance in a three-study sample, or greater methodological rigour in the larger trial — these explanations cannot be distinguished with the available data, and the formal asymmetry tests in Section 8 did not reach significance.

## 8. Publication Bias

| Diagnostic | Value | Reading |
|---|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | −0.0835 | Near zero |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.149** | Not statistically significant |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **0** | No studies imputed |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (standardized scale) | 0.8763 | Unchanged from observed |
| Assessed risk | **Low** | — |

**Interpretation.** Egger's regression test showed no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.149), and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), meaning the pooled estimate is unchanged after adjustment. Together these indicate a low risk of publication (small-study) bias, so the summary effect is unlikely to be materially inflated by selective reporting of positive results.

**Caveat.** Absence of detectable asymmetry is not proof of no bias — these tests have limited statistical power, particularly with fewer than approximately ten studies or substantial between-study heterogeneity, and asymmetry can also arise from genuine small-study effects rather than suppressed findings. With only three studies, Egger's test is essentially a regression through three points and has almost no capacity to detect asymmetry; the non-significant p-value of 0.149 should be read as uninformative rather than as exoneration. The negative Egger intercept (−0.0835) does not indicate the usual small-study inflation pattern, but it is estimated with correspondingly enormous uncertainty. The descriptive size–effect gradient noted in Section 7 remains a residual concern that the formal tests are simply not powered to adjudicate. The "low risk" classification should accordingly be qualified as "no bias detected, by tests with minimal power to detect it".

## 9. Discussion

Across three randomized comparisons enrolling 214 participants in total, probiotic supplementation was associated with a pooled Risk Ratio of **2.120 (95% CI 1.472–3.053, p = 5.43 × 10⁻⁵)** for self-reported improvement in constipation relative to placebo, usual care, or no active treatment. Under the review's pre-specified effect direction — in which RR > 1 denotes more participants reporting improvement on probiotics — this corresponds to roughly a doubling of the proportion of participants who report that their constipation has got better. The result was statistically homogeneous (I² = 0.0%; Q = 2.52, p = 0.2836; τ² = 1 × 10⁻⁶), all three contributing studies pointed in the same direction, and all three individual confidence intervals excluded the null.

Three features of this result deserve emphasis.

**(1) The outcome is patient-centred.** Unlike stool-frequency endpoints, the pooled measure here is whether participants themselves reported improvement. This is directly interpretable for shared decision-making and maps naturally onto the clinical question "will I notice a difference?". It is, however, also the endpoint most vulnerable to expectation effects. Constipation responder outcomes are known to show high placebo response rates, and in unblinded designs (usual care or no-treatment comparators are, by construction, unblinded) a substantial portion of a measured improvement differential can be attributable to knowledge of allocation rather than to the organism being supplemented. Whether the included comparators were placebo-controlled and double-blind, or open usual-care arms, is the single most important determinant of how much of the observed RR of 2.120 can be attributed to the probiotic itself — and the available extraction does not permit a clean separation of those comparator types.

**(2) The magnitude is large relative to prior synthesis literature.** Published meta-analyses of probiotics for chronic constipation have generally reported modest improvements in stool frequency (on the order of one additional bowel movement per week) and more variable results on global symptom endpoints. A pooled risk ratio above 2 for self-reported improvement sits at the optimistic end of that range. Two technical considerations plausibly contribute. First, the source reports expressed their results as **odds ratios**, and the present synthesis reports the user-requested **risk ratio**; for common outcomes the OR exceeds the RR, and any residual carry-over of the odds-based scale would inflate rather than deflate the pooled figure. Second, the smallest trials in the set produced the largest effects, and with only three studies the pooled estimate cannot be shielded from that pattern by weighting alone.

**(3) Statistical homogeneity should not be over-read.** I² of exactly 0.0% in three studies is a common and largely uninformative finding. It tells us that these three results are mutually compatible with a single underlying effect; it does not tell us that probiotic effects on constipation are consistent across strains, doses, populations, or improvement definitions. Clinical heterogeneity in this literature is well documented, and a three-study sample is too small to surface it.

Taken together, the evidence assembled here is **directionally consistent, statistically significant, and clinically plausible, but quantitatively fragile**. A reasonable summary for a clinician would be: probiotics appear more likely than inactive comparators to produce patient-reported improvement in constipation, the best available estimate is around a two-fold relative increase in improvement reporting, and the true effect could plausibly be anywhere from modest (RR ≈ 1.5) to substantial (RR ≈ 3.0). Given the generally benign safety profile of commercial probiotics in immunocompetent adults, the benefit–risk balance is favourable even under the more conservative end of that interval — but the confidence attached to the point estimate itself should remain low until replicated in larger, uniformly blinded trials with pre-specified responder definitions.

## 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-handling and validation issues were flagged during the pipeline run and are reported here without softening:

- **Effect measure conflict (OR vs. RR).** The source paper(s) explicitly report **Odds Ratios**, whereas the review question explicitly requested **Risk Ratios**. The pipeline recorded this conflict (`effect_measure_conflict`) and retained the user-specified RR as authoritative (`effect_measure_source` = explicit_user_request). The pooled figure is reported on the RR scale with native pooling confirmed, but readers should be aware that the primary reports' own stated metric was the odds ratio, which for a common outcome lies further from the null than the corresponding risk ratio.
- **Included studies exceed pooled studies (4 vs. 3).** Four studies met inclusion criteria, but only three yielded an extractable dichotomous self-reported-improvement effect size. The fourth is counted in the review but contributes nothing to the pooled estimate; all pooled statistics refer to k = 3 and N = 214.
- **Reviews and duplicate reports.** Narrative and systematic reviews, editorials, and duplicate reports of the same trial were removed at the document-classification stage so that no trial contributes more than once to the pooled estimate.
- **Single outcome group.** The pipeline produced one meta-analytic group only; no subgroup was flagged as `insufficient_data`, and no separate outcome or timepoint required independent pooling.
- **Direction label.** The `favors` field for the pooled estimate returns "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)" — a property of ratio measures of desirable events, not a statement that the arms performed equivalently. The pre-specified effect direction (RR > 1 = probiotics favour improvement) is what supplies the clinical reading.
- **Limited covariate capture.** Strain, dose, formulation, treatment duration, and comparator subtype were not consistently extractable across studies, precluding any clinical characterisation beyond "probiotics, broadly defined".

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base.** Only 3 studies and 214 participants were pooled. With k = 3, the random-effects variance estimate, the heterogeneity statistics, and the bias diagnostics are all operating far below the sample sizes at which they behave reliably.
2. **Underpowered bias and heterogeneity diagnostics.** Egger's test (p = 0.149) and trim-and-fill (k₀ = 0) have negligible power at k = 3; "low risk of publication bias" here means "none detected by an insensitive test", not "none present". Likewise, I² = 0.0% and Q p = 0.2836 do not establish homogeneity.
3. **Scale reconciliation.** The primary reports used odds ratios; the synthesis reports a risk ratio by explicit request. The conversion decision is analytic, not empirical, and could bias the pooled magnitude upward relative to a true risk-ratio synthesis.
4. **Unverified blinding and comparator mix.** The comparator category spans placebo, usual care, and no active treatment. Unblinded comparators inflate self-reported improvement differentials, and the proportion of the pooled estimate contributed by blinded versus unblinded designs could not be determined.
5. **Outcome definition heterogeneity.** "Self-reported improvement" aggregates patient-rated relief, global improvement, and "treatment success". These thresholds are not interchangeable, and a lenient definition yields higher event rates — and often larger ratios — than a stringent one.
6. **No formal risk-of-bias assessment.** Domain-level risk-of-bias appraisal (randomization, allocation concealment, blinding, attrition, selective reporting) was not available from the extraction pipeline, so the internal validity of the contributing trials could not be graded, and no sensitivity analysis restricted to low-risk studies was possible.
7. **No moderator analysis possible.** Strain, dose, duration, and population could not be tested as effect modifiers (Section 7), so the pooled RR cannot be translated into guidance about which probiotic to use.
8. **Dominance of one trial.** study_3_R3 contributes 56% of the pooled participants and carries the largest weight; the pooled estimate is substantially a reflection of that single trial, and its own effect (0.670 standardized) is the most conservative of the three.
9. **Attrition and intention-to-treat handling.** The outcome was specified as improvement reporters out of the total randomized per arm, but whether contributing trials analysed on a strict intention-to-treat basis or imputed non-completers as non-responders could not be verified.
10. **Short-term endpoints and no safety synthesis.** Durability of benefit beyond the trial treatment periods was not assessable, and adverse events were outside the scope of this synthesis, so the report characterises benefit only.

---

## 12. Conclusion

In a meta-analysis of 3 randomized comparisons enrolling 214 participants, probiotic supplementation produced a pooled **Risk Ratio of 2.120 (95% CI 1.472 to 3.053; p = 5.43 × 10⁻⁵)** for self-reported improvement in constipation versus placebo, usual care, or no active treatment, pooled natively on the risk-ratio scale with a REML random-effects model. The analysis's own direction field records "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)", reflecting the ratio-measure convention; interpreted against the review's pre-specified direction, in which RR > 1 means probiotics favour improvement, the estimate indicates that roughly twice as many participants reported improvement on probiotics as on inactive comparators. Heterogeneity was undetectable (I² = 0.0%; Q = 2.52, p = 0.2836; τ² = 1 × 10⁻⁶) and no publication-bias asymmetry was detected (Egger p = 0.149; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted estimate unchanged at 0.8763 on the standardized scale).

The signal is consistent and statistically strong, but the evidence base is thin, the primary reports' native metric was the odds ratio rather than the requested risk ratio, the comparator and blinding mix is unverified, and the bias diagnostics are too underpowered to be reassuring. The appropriate conclusion is therefore a qualified positive one: **probiotics are probably beneficial for patient-reported improvement in constipation, with a plausible effect somewhere between a 47% and a three-fold relative increase in the proportion of responders, but the point estimate of 2.120 should be regarded as provisional pending larger, uniformly blinded trials with pre-specified responder definitions and complete reporting of strain and dose.**
