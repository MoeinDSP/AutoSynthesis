# Extended- versus Standard-Duration Anticoagulation and Major Bleeding in Acutely Ill Medical Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Extended-duration anticoagulation may prevent venous thromboembolism after acute medical illness but may increase major bleeding. This review evaluated major bleeding within 45 days or fewer using each trial’s primary overall analysis.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons evaluated extended-duration versus standard-duration anticoagulation for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis in acutely ill medical patients. The outcome was major bleeding (event versus no event) during follow-up of 45 days or fewer. Outcomes beyond 45 days, COVID-19-only and same-extended-agent sensitivity analyses, prespecified subgroup analyses, symptomatic venous thromboembolism, and all-cause mortality were excluded. Risk ratios (RRs) were pooled using a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood model. Distinct source-measure groups were not merged when insufficient for pooling.

**Results:** Seven studies were represented in the pipeline, but only five eligible effect-size records were supplied. Three records (21,831 participants) contributed to the pooled RR analysis. The pooled RR was 1.9885935294017867 (95% CI 1.093–3.619; p=0.024435981374573392). Heterogeneity was moderate (I²=56.83%; τ²=0.157155; Q=4.751, p=0.093). The pipeline’s favorability field classified the result as neither arm favored/tie or undetermined; under the prespecified clinical convention, however, an RR above 1 corresponds to more major bleeding with extended-duration prophylaxis. One additional record originating from a hazard-ratio report (n=11,962) and one originating from a risk-difference report (n=5,963) were each insufficient for separate pooling. Egger’s intercept was −0.0752 (p=0.5511), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k0=0); these analyses have very low power with only five effect-size records.

**Conclusions:** The three-study random-effects analysis found a statistically significant RR above 1 for major bleeding with extended-duration anticoagulation, but the automated favorability designation was undetermined and heterogeneity was moderate. Interpretation is constrained by missing trial identities, arm-level event counts, incomplete representation of the seven studies, source-measure conflicts, and the small number of studies.

## Introduction

Acutely ill medical patients are at increased risk of venous thromboembolism during hospitalization and after discharge. Extending pharmacological prophylaxis beyond the standard inpatient or short-duration course may reduce thromboembolic events, but the benefit must be balanced against major bleeding. Because major bleeding is uncommon yet clinically consequential, synthesis across randomized trials can improve precision while also revealing differences among treatment regimens and trial populations.

This review addressed the following question: among acutely ill medical patients receiving venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, what is the effect of extended-duration anticoagulation versus standard-duration anticoagulation on major bleeding during short-term follow-up of 45 days or fewer, using only each trial’s primary overall analysis?

The treatment was extended-duration anticoagulation for venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, and the comparator was standard-duration prophylaxis. For the requested RR, values below 1 favor extended-duration anticoagulation and values above 1 indicate greater bleeding harm with extended-duration anticoagulation. Separately, the supplied analysis included an automated `favors` field, which was retained exactly as provided when describing arm favorability.

## Search/Screening

### Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies enrolled acutely ill medical patients receiving venous thromboembolism prophylaxis and compared extended-duration with standard-duration anticoagulation. The eligible outcome was major bleeding, analyzed as event versus no event, occurring within 45 days or fewer and reported in the trial’s primary overall analysis.

The following were excluded:

- outcomes measured beyond 45 days;
- COVID-19-only sensitivity analyses;
- same-extended-agent sensitivity analyses;
- subgroup analyses by sex, age, cancer history, D-dimer status, or infection status;
- symptomatic venous thromboembolism; and
- all-cause mortality.

The intended data elements were the number of participants with major bleeding and the total randomized in each treatment arm. The supplied results did not include searchable databases, search dates, full search strings, deduplication counts, title/abstract screening counts, full-text exclusions, trial names, or arm-level event counts. Consequently, a conventional PRISMA flow diagram and independent reconstruction of trial eligibility were not possible from the available material.

### Study flow

The pipeline summary listed seven studies and five effect-size records. Thus, two of the seven studies did not contribute an eligible effect-size record in the supplied output, but study-specific reasons were not reported. Of the five effect-size records, three were combined in the RR meta-analysis; one record originated from a source reporting a hazard ratio (HR), and one originated from a source reporting a risk difference (RD). The latter two formed separate one-study groups and could not be pooled.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

### Primary RR group

Three studies comprising 21,831 participants were pooled. The REML random-effects **pooled Risk Ratio (RR)** was **1.9885935294017867** (95% CI **1.093–3.619**; p=**0.024435981374573392**).

The supplied automated favorability field classified this result as **“neither (tie or undetermined)”** (the overall record similarly stated “neither [essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted]”). Accordingly, no favored arm is assigned from that automated field. Under the review’s prespecified clinical interpretation of major-bleeding events, however, an RR greater than 1 denotes a higher major-bleeding risk under extended-duration anticoagulation. The estimate is therefore statistically incompatible with an RR of 1 at the two-sided 0.05 level and lies on the side corresponding to greater bleeding harm with extended treatment, while the pipeline’s formal arm-favorability designation remains undetermined.

## Publication Bias

The supplied publication-bias analysis reported:

- **Egger’s intercept:** −0.0752
- **Egger’s test p value:** 0.5511
- **Trim-and-fill imputed studies:** k0=0
- **Trim-and-fill adjusted effect on the pipeline’s bias-analysis scale:** 0.4125
- **Pipeline risk classification:** low

Egger’s test was not statistically significant, and trim-and-fill did not identify missing studies. These outputs provide no detected evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Nevertheless, five effect-size records—and only three in the pooled RR group—are far below the study count generally needed for adequately powered funnel-plot asymmetry testing. A nonsignificant Egger test therefore should not be interpreted as proof that reporting bias is absent. Moreover, the trim-and-fill value of 0.4125 was not identified as a native RR estimate and should not be compared directly with the pooled RR of 1.9885935294017867.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

In the primary quantitative synthesis, three studies involving 21,831 participants produced a pooled RR of 1.9885935294017867 (95% CI 1.093–3.619). The result was statistically significant (p=0.024435981374573392) and was accompanied by moderate heterogeneity (I²=56.83%). Under the prespecified event-based interpretation, the magnitude and confidence interval indicate that extended-duration prophylaxis was associated with a higher risk of major bleeding than standard-duration prophylaxis over follow-up of 45 days or fewer. At the same time, the supplied automated direction field did not assign either arm as favored, so this report preserves that designation rather than replacing it with an inferred arm-favorability label.

The confidence interval is wide, ranging from a relatively small increase to more than a threefold risk ratio. This imprecision, together with moderate inconsistency and only three pooled studies, limits confidence in the exact magnitude of the association. The result is most appropriately interpreted as evidence that extended prophylaxis may materially increase major-bleeding risk, rather than as a precise estimate of the increase across all acutely ill medical populations and regimens.

### Clinical interpretation

Major bleeding is a safety outcome, and even a low absolute event rate may be important when prophylaxis is administered broadly. Relative effects alone do not establish the absolute clinical burden. Because arm-specific event counts and denominators were unavailable, absolute risks, risk differences, and numbers needed to harm could not be calculated. The safety result should ultimately be weighed against trial-specific efficacy in preventing venous thromboembolism, but efficacy outcomes were outside this review’s scope.

Differences among anticoagulants, prophylaxis durations, eligibility criteria, baseline bleeding risk, and outcome definitions may account for some of the observed heterogeneity. Such explanations remain speculative because the corresponding study characteristics and moderator analyses were unavailable.

### Strengths

The analysis addressed a focused safety question, restricted follow-up to 45 days or fewer, and used only primary overall trial analyses. It avoided mixing prespecified subgroup and sensitivity analyses with overall trial estimates. It also kept records originating from HR and RD reports separate from the multi-study RR group rather than forcing all five entries into one pooled result. A random-effects REML model was reasonable given the observed between-study inconsistency.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Several validation issues materially affect interpretation:

1. The pipeline listed seven studies but only five effect-size records; reasons that two studies did not contribute were not supplied.
2. Trial identities, citations, treatment regimens, follow-up times, major-bleeding definitions, and arm-level bleeding counts and randomized denominators were absent. Eligibility and effect calculations therefore could not be independently verified.
3. One record came from a paper explicitly reporting an HR and one from a paper explicitly reporting an RD, whereas the user-requested measure was RR. The pipeline retained the requested RR label but flagged both conflicts. These records were each kept in separate, unpooled groups.
4. The supplied per-study numerical field was not fully concordant with the native-measure metadata or the reported pooled RR. Accordingly, the record-level entries are reproduced as supplied but are not asserted to be independently validated RRs.
5. The automated favorability field labeled the pooled result as neither arm favored/tie or undetermined, despite the prespecified clinical rule that RR>1 corresponds to greater major-bleeding harm with extended treatment. Both pieces of information have been stated explicitly without silently overriding the supplied direction field.
6. Publication-bias outputs appear to use a pipeline bias-analysis scale that is not confirmed as the native RR scale. The trim-and-fill adjusted value should therefore not be interpreted as an adjusted pooled RR.
7. No information was provided about duplicate reports, review articles, overlapping trial populations, or decisions used to prevent double-counting.

## Limitations

First, the evidence base available for pooling was small: only three studies entered the primary meta-analysis. This limits precision, heterogeneity assessment, and the reliability of publication-bias tests.

Second, no arm-level event counts or randomized totals were available. The requested extraction could not be displayed, continuity-correction choices could not be assessed, and pooled estimates could not be independently reproduced from 2×2 tables.

Third, two additional eligible records had source-measure conflicts and could not be pooled within their own groups because each group contained only one study. The synthesis therefore did not quantitatively incorporate all five available effect-size records into a single estimate.

Fourth, the absence of trial characteristics precluded assessment of clinical heterogeneity, risk of bias by domain, anticoagulant-specific effects, outcome-definition variation, and applicability to particular patient populations. No certainty-of-evidence assessment, such as GRADE, was supplied.

Fifth, search methods and screening counts were unavailable. Completeness of study identification and adherence to every PRISMA search-reporting item cannot be confirmed.

Finally, the direction metadata were internally difficult to reconcile: the event-based interpretation of RR>1 indicates greater bleeding with extended treatment, whereas the automated `favors` field was undetermined. This discrepancy warrants resolution against the original trial event coding before clinical decisions are based on the pooled estimate.

## Conclusion

Among acutely ill medical patients receiving venous thromboembolism prophylaxis, the three-study REML random-effects analysis found a pooled **Risk Ratio (RR) of 1.9885935294017867** (95% CI **1.093–3.619**; p=**0.024435981374573392**) for major bleeding within 45 days or fewer. Heterogeneity was moderate (I²=56.83%; τ²=0.157155). Under the prespecified event-based interpretation, the estimate lies in the direction of greater major-bleeding harm with extended-duration anticoagulation, although the supplied automated favorability field classified the favored arm as undetermined. One HR-origin record and one RD-origin record were each insufficient for separate pooling. The finding should be interpreted cautiously until trial-level event counts, study identities, source measures, and direction coding are verified.
