# Short-Term Effects of Child-Feeding Interventions on Children's Vegetable Intake Compared with No-Intervention Control: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Vegetable intake in childhood is consistently below recommended levels in most high- and middle-income settings. Child-feeding interventions — repeated taste exposure, parent feeding-practice training, rewards and modelling, and nutrition education targeting the feeding environment — are widely promoted as modifiable levers on early vegetable acceptance, but estimates of their short-term magnitude vary widely across trials.

**Objective.** To quantify the short-term effect (<12 months after intervention end) of child-feeding interventions versus a no-intervention or usual-care control on children's vegetable intake, measured by any quantitative index (grams consumed, servings/day, portions, intake frequency, weighed plate waste, 24-hour recall, food frequency questionnaire, parent- or child-reported consumption, or acceptance/consumption at a test meal).

**Methods.** A systematic search and structured screening pipeline identified 15 studies meeting inclusion criteria, of which 9 independent comparisons supplied data sufficient for effect-size computation (N = 791 participants). Study-level standardized mean differences were pooled using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. The pre-specified effect measure was the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD), and pooling was performed natively on that scale.

**Results.** The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was 0.957 (95% CI 0.099 to 1.814; p = 0.0288; k = 9; N = 791), favouring the child-feeding intervention arm. Between-study heterogeneity was extreme: Q = 95.308 (df = 8, p < 0.001), I² = 96.8%, τ² = 1.635 (τ ≈ 1.28). Individual effects ranged from −0.340 (95% CI −0.891 to 0.212) to 3.950 (95% CI 2.979 to 4.921); five of nine comparisons were individually statistically significant and all five favoured the intervention. The approximate 95% prediction interval spanned roughly −2.16 to 4.07, indicating that the direction of effect in a future comparable trial cannot be confidently predicted. Egger's regression indicated marked funnel asymmetry (intercept = −1.67, p < 0.001), and overall small-study-effect risk was rated high; trim-and-fill imputed 2 studies and returned an adjusted estimate of 1.268, which did not attenuate the pooled value.

**Conclusions.** Child-feeding interventions are associated with a statistically significant short-term increase in children's vegetable intake relative to no-intervention control, but the pooled estimate is imprecise, rests on only nine small comparisons, and sits within a body of evidence showing near-total between-study inconsistency (I² = 96.8%) and high risk of small-study bias. The pooled SMD of 0.957 should be read as an upper-bound, hypothesis-generating figure rather than a stable estimate of expected benefit; the seven comparisons excluding the two extreme outliers have an unweighted mean SMD of approximately 0.36, which is likely a more realistic indication of typical short-term benefit. Larger, pre-registered trials using objectively weighed intake outcomes are needed.

## 1. Introduction

Insufficient vegetable consumption in childhood is one of the most durable and widely documented dietary shortfalls in contemporary public health nutrition. Vegetable intake tracks moderately from early childhood into adolescence and adulthood, so the preschool and early school years are widely regarded as a strategic window for intervention. A heterogeneous family of behavioural strategies — collectively referred to here as **child-feeding interventions** — has been developed to act on this window. These include:

- **Repeated taste exposure**, in which a target vegetable is offered on multiple occasions to attenuate neophobia and build familiarity-driven liking;
- **Parent feeding-practice training**, which targets the proximal feeding environment (pressure to eat, restriction, responsive feeding, availability and accessibility);
- **Rewards and modelling**, including non-food tangible rewards, verbal praise, peer modelling and adult modelling at mealtimes; and
- **Nutrition education targeting feeding**, in which caregivers or children receive structured curricular content aimed specifically at vegetable provision and consumption.

Although the mechanistic rationale for each of these approaches is well established in experimental feeding research, their aggregate effect on *actual consumption* — as opposed to liking, willingness to taste, or caregiver knowledge — remains contested. Trials differ substantially in the age of participants, the intensity and duration of exposure, the setting (home, childcare, school, laboratory), and, critically, in how vegetable intake is measured. Weighed plate waste at a single laboratory test meal and parent-reported servings per day on a food frequency questionnaire are both admissible operationalisations of the outcome, yet they differ markedly in measurement error, reactivity and susceptibility to social-desirability bias.

This review addresses a deliberately narrow and policy-relevant question: **what is the short-term effect (<12 months after intervention end) of child-feeding interventions, relative to a no-intervention or usual-care control, on children's vegetable intake?** Restricting comparators to no-intervention/usual care isolates the total effect of being offered a feeding intervention rather than the incremental effect of one active component over another; restricting follow-up to under 12 months targets the window in which effects are most plausibly detectable and most frequently reported.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Information sources and search strategy

A structured systematic search and screening pipeline was executed to identify randomised and quasi-randomised controlled evaluations of child-feeding interventions reporting a quantitative vegetable-intake outcome against a no-intervention or usual-care comparator. Records were screened in two stages — title/abstract followed by full text — against the eligibility criteria set out below, with each surviving record subjected to structured data extraction covering design, participants, intervention components, comparator, outcome instrument, timing of assessment relative to intervention end, and the arm-level statistics required for effect-size computation (means, standard deviations or convertible precision statistics, and group sample sizes).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Children (infancy through school age) in community, childcare, school, clinic or laboratory settings.

**Intervention.** Any child-feeding intervention, including repeated exposure, parent feeding-practice training, rewards/modelling, or nutrition education explicitly targeting feeding.

**Comparator.** No-intervention control or usual care. Active-comparator-only trials (one feeding strategy against another, with no inactive arm) were not eligible for this synthesis.

**Outcome.** Children's vegetable intake quantified by any of: grams consumed, servings/day, portions, frequency of intake, weighed plate waste, 24-hour recall, food frequency questionnaire, parent- or child-reported vegetable consumption, or vegetable acceptance/consumption at a test meal.

**Timing.** Outcome assessment occurring within 12 months of intervention end (short-term follow-up). Where a study reported multiple post-intervention timepoints within this window, the measurement closest to the primary endpoint was used so that each study contributed a single, statistically independent effect size.

**Design.** Controlled evaluations with a concurrent inactive comparator.

### 2.3 Screening yield

The pipeline identified **15 studies** meeting the substantive inclusion criteria. Of these, **9 studies (60%) provided arm-level data sufficient to compute a standardized effect size** and constitute the quantitative synthesis set (N = 791 participants). The remaining six included studies could not be carried into the pooled analysis because the reported statistics were not convertible to an effect size and variance on the SMD scale — typically because dispersion statistics were omitted, results were reported only as within-group change significance, or outcomes were presented graphically or categorically without accompanying numerical detail. These studies are retained in the narrative description of the evidence base but are, by necessity, absent from all pooled estimates, and their omission is treated as a material limitation in Section 9.

No separate `meta_analysis_groups` structure was present in the analysis output: all nine contributing comparisons addressed the same outcome construct (vegetable intake) at the same timepoint class (short-term, <12 months post-intervention) and were therefore pooled as a single stratum.

**Sample sizes.** Total analysed N = 791. Per-comparison sample sizes ranged from 35 to 180, with a median of 58. Six of the nine comparisons enrolled fewer than 100 participants, and three enrolled fewer than 50. This is a small-trial literature: the two largest comparisons (study_2_R2, n = 180; study_13_R13, n = 139) together account for 40.3% of all participants, while the four smallest (n = 35, 47, 56, 56) account for 24.5%.

Effects of this size (2.5 to 4 standard deviations) imply near-complete separation of the intervention and control distributions and are more characteristic of tightly controlled single-session laboratory test-meal paradigms, or of outcome scales with very small within-group variance, than of free-living dietary change. Both are small studies (n = 56 and n = 47). Their influence on the pooled result is addressed throughout Sections 5 to 8.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure and computation

The pre-specified and analysed effect measure was the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, computed as Hedges' g (i.e. with the small-sample bias correction applied), with the computation scale and the pooling scale both set to SMD. The effect measure was specified by explicit request, no conflicting measure was detected in the extracted data, and **native pooling on the SMD scale was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`) — the pooled statistic reported below is therefore a confirmed native-scale Standardized Mean Difference, not a standardized fallback approximation.

Because the primary studies measured vegetable intake on incommensurable scales (grams, servings per day, portions, intake frequency, plate-waste weight, recall- and questionnaire-derived consumption), standardization is not merely a convenience here but a necessity; no common natural unit exists across the nine comparisons.

### 4.2 Direction convention

A **positive** SMD indicates that children receiving the feeding intervention consumed **more** vegetables than no-intervention controls at short-term follow-up. All reported signs follow the standard treatment-minus-control convention.

### 4.3 Dispersion of study-level effects

Study-level effects span 4.29 SMD units, from −0.340 to 3.950. Excluding the two extreme positive outliers, the remaining seven comparisons fall in a comparatively tight band from −0.340 to 1.034, with an unweighted arithmetic mean of 0.357. The distribution of observed effects is plotted in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`, which shows a strongly right-skewed, bimodal-appearing spread consistent with a dense cluster of small-to-moderate effects plus a detached high-magnitude tail.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

### 5.1 Primary pooled estimate

A random-effects model with REML estimation of the between-study variance component yielded:

> **Pooled Standardized Mean Difference = 0.957 (95% CI 0.099 to 1.814), p = 0.0288, k = 9, N = 791.**

Per the analysis output's `favors` field, this result **favours the child-feeding intervention** arm — that is, children receiving repeated exposure, parent feeding-practice training, rewards/modelling or feeding-targeted nutrition education consumed more vegetables at short-term follow-up than children in no-intervention or usual-care control conditions.

Three features of this estimate warrant immediate emphasis:

1. **Magnitude.** Taken at face value, an SMD of 0.957 is a large effect by conventional benchmarks — approaching a full standard deviation of vegetable intake. In a field where interventions on free-living dietary behaviour commonly produce effects in the 0.1–0.3 range, this is an implausibly large population-level claim and almost certainly reflects the composition of the included sample rather than a realistic expectation of benefit.
2. **Precision.** The confidence interval is extremely wide (span = 1.715 SMD units) and its lower bound (0.099) sits close to the null. The result is statistically significant, but only marginally so (p = 0.0288), and the interval is compatible with anything from a trivially small to a very large benefit.
3. **Model dependence.** With τ² = 1.635 dominating the sampling variances of the individual studies, the random-effects weights are close to being equalised across comparisons. In practical terms, the two outlier studies (n = 56 and n = 47) are weighted comparably to the largest study (n = 180), which is precisely the mechanism by which the pooled value is pulled upward to 0.957.

The forest plot, showing individual effects, confidence intervals, study weights and the pooled diamond, is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`.

### 5.2 Prediction interval

The 95% prediction interval — the range within which the true effect of a future comparable trial would be expected to fall — is approximately **−2.16 to 4.07**. This interval comfortably includes zero and includes substantial effects in *both* directions. The practical interpretation is stark: on this evidence base, one cannot predict with confidence even the *direction* of effect of a new child-feeding intervention trial, let alone its magnitude. The contrast between a statistically significant pooled mean and a prediction interval spanning the null is the single most important analytic finding of this review.

### 5.3 Cumulative and sensitivity perspectives

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) illustrates how the pooled estimate evolves as comparisons are added, and clarifies the extent to which the running estimate is destabilised at the points where the two high-magnitude studies enter the synthesis.

A formal leave-one-out analysis was not reported in the analysis output, but the structure of the data permits a transparent statement about influence: the seven comparisons remaining after removal of study_8_R8 and study_10_R10 have an unweighted mean of 0.357 and a range of −0.340 to 1.034. A random-effects pooled estimate confined to those seven comparisons would therefore be expected to fall well below the headline figure of 0.957 and to carry far less heterogeneity. Readers should treat **0.957 as an outlier-inflated upper bound and approximately 0.36 as the more defensible indication of typical short-term benefit** in this literature, pending a formally re-estimated sensitivity model.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was extreme and is the defining characteristic of this evidence base:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 95.308 (df = 8) |
| p-value for Q | < 0.001 |
| I² | 96.8% |
| τ² | 1.635 |
| τ (SD of true effects) | ≈ 1.279 |
| 95% prediction interval | ≈ [−2.16, 4.07] |

An I² of **96.8%** indicates that essentially all of the observed variation in effect estimates is attributable to genuine differences in underlying effects rather than to sampling error. Q is more than eleven times its degrees of freedom. τ = 1.279 means that the standard deviation of true effects across studies is larger than the pooled mean effect itself — the between-study spread dwarfs the average signal.

Heterogeneity of this order has direct consequences for interpretation. First, the "average" effect becomes a weak summary of a distribution whose members do not share a common value; reporting a single number risks implying a consistency that the data flatly contradict. Second, under REML the large τ² flattens the weighting scheme, transferring influence from large to small studies — which, in a literature where small studies report the largest effects (Section 8), systematically inflates the pooled mean. Third, standard bias-correction tools, including trim-and-fill, are known to behave unreliably when τ² is large, which bears directly on the publication-bias findings below.

Plausible sources of this heterogeneity, given the inclusion criteria, include: (i) **outcome instrument** — weighed intake at a controlled test meal versus parent-reported FFQ servings produce effects on very different error scales; (ii) **intervention modality and intensity** — a multi-week repeated-exposure protocol with direct food provision differs fundamentally from a caregiver education module; (iii) **setting and control over consumption** — laboratory and childcare settings permit direct measurement and exposure control, home settings do not; (iv) **participant age**, which determines both neophobia levels and the ceiling for behaviour change; and (v) **timing within the <12-month window**, since exposure-induced acceptance effects are known to decay. A graphical summary of heterogeneity diagnostics is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 7. Moderators

With **k = 9** contributing comparisons, formal moderator analysis is not statistically defensible. Conventional guidance suggests roughly ten studies per covariate for meta-regression; a single-covariate model here would be badly underpowered, and any multi-covariate model would be uninterpretable. No moderator model reached the threshold for inclusion in the analysis output, and none is reported as a confirmed finding.

Two descriptive observations are nonetheless available and are offered strictly as hypothesis-generating:

**Sample size as an informal moderator.** Effect magnitude is inversely associated with sample size across the nine comparisons. The four comparisons with n ≥ 124 (study_2_R2, study_12_R12, study_13_R13, and at n = 96 study_6_R6) report effects of 0.421, 0.222, 0.282 and 0.140 respectively — a tight cluster of small positive effects. The two largest effects (3.950 and 2.577) come from the comparisons with n = 56 and n = 47. This gradient is exactly what the publication-bias diagnostics in Section 8 formalise, and it admits at least three non-exclusive explanations: selective reporting of small striking results, methodologically weaker measurement in small studies, or genuinely different (more tightly controlled, higher-dose) designs among small trials.

**Plausible but untested effect modifiers.** Outcome instrument type (objectively weighed intake versus self/proxy report), intervention modality (direct repeated exposure versus caregiver-directed education), and setting (laboratory/childcare versus home) are the most likely drivers of heterogeneity on theoretical grounds. The analysis output contains no coded moderator variables permitting these to be tested, so they remain conjectures. A bubble plot exploring the relationship between study precision and effect magnitude is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`.

**Statement of insufficiency.** To be explicit: this review does **not** report moderator effects, because there are too few studies to estimate them. Any apparent subgroup pattern described above is descriptive only and must not be read as evidence that one intervention type outperforms another.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | −1.67 |
| Egger's test p-value | < 0.001 |
| Trim-and-fill: studies imputed (k₀) | 2 |
| Trim-and-fill: adjusted estimate | 1.268 |
| Overall small-study-effect risk rating | **High** |

**Egger's test** is highly significant (p < 0.001), establishing substantial funnel-plot asymmetry. Smaller studies in this synthesis report systematically larger effects than larger ones — a pattern directly visible in the raw data (Section 7) and consistent with publication bias or other small-study effects.

**Trim-and-fill** imputed only two hypothetical missing studies and returned an adjusted estimate of **1.268**, which is *higher* than the unadjusted pooled estimate of 0.957 rather than lower. This is an atypical and uninformative result and should not be read as evidence that the pooled effect survives bias adjustment. Trim-and-fill is well documented to perform poorly — under-imputing, mis-identifying the side of the funnel requiring augmentation, and producing unstable adjusted estimates — when between-study heterogeneity is large, and with I² = 96.8% and τ² = 1.635 this analysis sits far outside the conditions under which the estimator is reliable. The divergence between a strongly significant Egger's test and a non-attenuating trim-and-fill adjustment is best understood as a signal that the asymmetry is driven by the *magnitude* of the small-study effect in a handful of studies rather than by a large number of plausibly "missing" null trials.

**Overall assessment.** The "high" risk rating is appropriate and should be carried into every reading of the primary result. The pooled SMD of 0.957 should be presented as **probably inflated**. Publication bias is the most likely explanation for the observed asymmetry but is not the only one: genuine clinical and methodological heterogeneity, poorer measurement and weaker design control in small studies, and true differences in intervention dose across trial sizes can all generate the same funnel pattern. Appropriate next steps, which the present dataset does not support, would include restricting synthesis to larger and low-risk-of-bias studies, applying selection models such as PET-PEESE, and formal outlier-exclusion sensitivity analyses.

A final caution: with k = 9, the power of Egger's test and the reliability of trim-and-fill are both limited. The significant asymmetry test here is informative because the gradient is steep, but the absence of such a signal in a nine-study synthesis would not have been reassuring either.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Principal findings

Across nine controlled comparisons and 791 children, child-feeding interventions produced a statistically significant short-term increase in vegetable intake relative to no-intervention control (pooled SMD = 0.957, 95% CI 0.099 to 1.814, p = 0.0288), favouring the intervention arm. Eight of nine comparisons were positively signed, five significantly so, and none favoured the control condition significantly. On the question of *whether* child-feeding interventions can move short-term vegetable consumption, the direction of evidence is consistent.

On the question of *how much*, the evidence is not usable in its current form. Heterogeneity is near-total (I² = 96.8%, τ² = 1.635), the prediction interval spans from a large negative to a very large positive effect, and small-study bias risk is high with a highly significant Egger's test. The pooled magnitude is substantially driven by two small trials reporting effects (g = 3.950 and g = 2.577) that are difficult to reconcile with any plausible model of free-living dietary change; the remaining seven comparisons average approximately 0.36, a figure far more consistent with the broader behavioural-nutrition literature.

### 9.2 Interpretation in context

The pattern observed here — directionally consistent benefit, wildly inconsistent magnitude, inverse relation between study size and effect — is characteristic of an immature intervention literature composed largely of small, single-site, proof-of-concept trials using heterogeneous and partly self-reported outcome measures. It is informative that the four best-powered comparisons (n = 96 to 180) converge on small positive effects of 0.140 to 0.421. If one treats those four as the least bias-prone subset, the implied short-term benefit is modest but real: on the order of a fifth to two-fifths of a standard deviation of vegetable intake. For a behaviour as resistant to change as children's vegetable consumption, an effect of that size is still of public-health interest, particularly if achievable at low cost and at scale.

Mechanistically, the finding is consistent with the well-replicated experimental literature on repeated exposure and familiarity-driven liking, and with evidence that caregiver feeding practices shape availability and the affective context of eating. What the present synthesis cannot establish is which of these mechanisms accounts for the observed benefit, because k = 9 precludes component-level moderator analysis.

### 9.3 Implications for practice

Practitioners and programme commissioners can reasonably conclude that offering a structured child-feeding intervention is more likely than not to increase children's vegetable intake in the short term, and is unlikely to reduce it. They should **not** plan on the basis of an effect approaching one standard deviation. Planning assumptions anchored on an SMD in the region of 0.2 to 0.4 are better supported by the larger trials in this synthesis and are more robust to the bias signals documented in Section 8.

### 9.4 Implications for research

Three priorities follow directly. First, **larger trials**: with a median n of 58, this literature is dominated by studies incapable of estimating modest effects with usable precision. Second, **objective outcome measurement**: weighed intake or validated plate-waste protocols should be preferred to parent-report instruments, and reporting should state the instrument, the timing relative to intervention end, and the full arm-level descriptive statistics. Third, **pre-registration and complete reporting**: six of the fifteen included studies could not contribute to the pooled analysis because essential statistics were unavailable — a 40% data-loss rate that is itself a potential source of bias and is entirely preventable.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No formal `validation_flags` structure was returned by the analysis pipeline, so no automated data-quality exceptions (review/duplicate exclusions, unclassifiable outcomes, or companion-report deduplication) are on record for this synthesis. Three transparency items nonetheless require plain statement:

1. **Partial data availability.** Fifteen studies met the inclusion criteria; only nine contributed effect sizes. Six included studies (40%) were absent from all pooled estimates because their reported statistics could not be converted to an SMD and variance. The pooled result therefore describes a majority but not the totality of the eligible evidence, and the direction of effect in the six non-contributing studies is unknown to this analysis.
2. **Effect measure provenance.** The effect measure was the Standardized Mean Difference, set by explicit user specification, with no detected conflict against the extracted data. Native pooling on the SMD scale was confirmed (`native_pooling_used = true`); the reported pooled value is a genuine native-scale SMD and not a standardized fallback.
3. **Single analysis stratum.** No `meta_analysis_groups` were present: all nine comparisons shared one outcome construct and one timepoint class and were pooled together. No results have been merged across distinct outcomes or timepoints.
4. **Risk-of-bias assessment.** No study-level risk-of-bias ratings were available in the analysis output. The influence of trial quality on the pooled estimate therefore could not be examined, and the high small-study-effect rating could not be decomposed into reporting-bias versus methodological-quality components.

## 11. Limitations

- **Very small number of comparisons (k = 9).** This precludes moderator analysis and meta-regression entirely, limits the power of asymmetry tests, and makes both τ² and the pooled estimate sensitive to individual studies.
- **Extreme heterogeneity (I² = 96.8%, τ² = 1.635).** The pooled mean is a weak summary of a highly dispersed distribution of true effects; the prediction interval (≈ −2.16 to 4.07) includes the null and large effects in both directions.
- **High risk of small-study effects.** Egger's intercept = −1.67, p < 0.001. The pooled estimate should be regarded as probably inflated. Trim-and-fill (k₀ = 2, adjusted estimate 1.268) failed to attenuate the estimate and is unreliable at this level of heterogeneity; it provides no reassurance.
- **Outlier dominance.** Two small studies (g = 3.950, n = 56; g = 2.577, n = 47) exert disproportionate influence under the flattened random-effects weighting induced by the large τ². A formal leave-one-out and outlier-exclusion sensitivity analysis was not available in the analysis output; the unweighted mean of the remaining seven comparisons (0.357) is offered as an informal substitute, not as a re-estimated model.
- **40% of included studies contributed no data.** Six of fifteen eligible studies lacked extractable statistics, introducing potential availability bias of unknown direction.
- **Outcome measurement heterogeneity.** Pooling weighed test-meal intake alongside parent-reported FFQ servings and 24-hour recalls combines measures with very different reliability and reactivity profiles. Standardization permits arithmetic aggregation but does not make these measures conceptually equivalent.
- **No risk-of-bias stratification.** Allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessment, attrition and selective reporting could not be assessed or used to stratify the synthesis.
- **Imprecise pooled interval.** The 95% CI (0.099 to 1.814) has a lower bound adjacent to the null; the result is statistically significant at p = 0.0288 but is not robust to modest perturbation.
- **Unexamined timing effects within the <12-month window.** Effects measured days after intervention end and effects measured eleven months later were pooled together, despite strong theoretical grounds for expecting decay.

## 12. Conclusion

Child-feeding interventions — repeated exposure, parent feeding-practice training, rewards and modelling, and feeding-targeted nutrition education — are associated with a statistically significant short-term increase in children's vegetable intake relative to no-intervention or usual-care control (pooled Standardized Mean Difference 0.957, 95% CI 0.099 to 1.814, p = 0.0288, k = 9, N = 791, favouring the intervention arm). The consistency of direction across eight of nine comparisons, including all five statistically significant ones, supports the conclusion that such interventions do something useful in the short term.

The magnitude of that benefit, however, cannot be established from the present evidence. Between-study heterogeneity is close to maximal (I² = 96.8%, τ² = 1.635, Q = 95.308, p < 0.001), the prediction interval spans the null in both directions, and funnel asymmetry is marked and statistically unambiguous (Egger's p < 0.001; high risk rating), with a trim-and-fill adjustment that is uninformative at this level of heterogeneity. The headline estimate is materially driven by two small trials reporting effects of 2.6 and 4.0 standard deviations; excluding these, the remaining seven comparisons have an unweighted mean of approximately 0.36, and the four best-powered comparisons cluster between 0.140 and 0.421.

The defensible summary is therefore: **child-feeding interventions probably produce a small-to-moderate short-term increase in children's vegetable intake, plausibly in the region of 0.2 to 0.4 standardized units, and the pooled figure of 0.957 should be treated as an inflated upper bound rather than an expected effect.** Confirming this will require adequately powered, pre-registered trials with objectively measured intake, complete statistical reporting, and pre-specified follow-up timepoints within and beyond the 12-month window.
