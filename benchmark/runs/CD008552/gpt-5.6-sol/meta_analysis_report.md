# Child-Feeding Interventions and Short-Term Vegetable Intake in Children: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Increasing vegetable intake during childhood is an important dietary objective. This review evaluated whether child-feeding interventions increase children’s short-term vegetable intake relative to no intervention.

### Methods
Eligible comparisons evaluated a child-feeding intervention against a no-intervention control in children and quantitatively assessed vegetable intake after the intervention but less than 12 months later. Measures could include amount, frequency, servings, grams, or a validated intake score. When multiple eligible assessments were available, one was selected per comparison, prioritizing the endpoint closest to intervention completion and then the most comprehensive measure. Six independent effect sizes from 520 participants were synthesized using a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The prespecified pooled statistic was the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD), with positive values indicating greater vegetable intake under the intervention.

### Results
The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was 1.097981187802555 (95% CI −0.239 to 2.435; p=0.10760038349207113; k=6; total N=520), favoring the child-feeding intervention according to the supplied direction classification, but the confidence interval included the null. Heterogeneity was extreme (Q=91.656, df=5, p=0.0; I²=97.9%; τ²=2.698659). Individual estimates ranged from −0.339518 to 3.950422. Egger’s intercept was −2.1834 (p=0.0001), indicating marked funnel-plot asymmetry and possible small-study effects. Trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀=0) and produced an adjusted estimate of 1.098; publication-bias risk was nevertheless classified as high.

### Conclusions
The point estimate favored child-feeding interventions, but the pooled effect was not statistically significant and was highly inconsistent across studies. Extreme heterogeneity, few analyzable comparisons, and strong evidence of small-study effects substantially limit confidence in the average estimate. The evidence does not establish a reliable, generalizable short-term benefit, although some interventions produced large positive effects.

## Search/Screening

The review question was defined using the following eligibility framework:

- **Population:** children;
- **Intervention:** a child-feeding intervention;
- **Comparator:** no-intervention control;
- **Outcome:** any quantitative measure of children’s vegetable intake, including amount, frequency, servings, grams, or a validated intake score;
- **Timing:** assessed after the intervention and less than 12 months later.

Assessments conducted at 12 months or later were ineligible for this outcome. To preserve statistical independence and avoid double-counting participants, only one eligible short-term assessment was to be used per comparison. Selection prioritized the endpoint closest to the end of the intervention; where timing was equivalent, the most comprehensive vegetable-intake measure was preferred.

The supplied pipeline summary contained 15 studies and six effect sizes. Thus, six comparisons contributed quantitative data to this meta-analysis. The context did not provide database names, search dates, search strings, registry searches, deduplication counts, title/abstract screening counts, full-text exclusion reasons, or a PRISMA flow diagram. It also did not specify why nine of the 15 pipeline studies did not contribute to this particular synthesis. Those studies may have lacked an eligible short-term vegetable-intake estimate or sufficient quantitative data, but this cannot be determined from the supplied results and should not be assumed.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) yielded a **pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) of 1.097981187802555** (95% CI −0.239 to 2.435; p=0.10760038349207113; k=6; total N=520). The supplied direction classification indicates that this result favors the **child-feeding intervention**.

Although the point estimate was positive and numerically large, the 95% confidence interval included zero and extended from a small effect in the opposite direction to a very large positive effect. The pooled result therefore did not reach conventional statistical significance. It should not be interpreted as establishing that child-feeding interventions increase short-term vegetable intake. Equally, the wide interval means that potentially important benefits cannot be excluded.

The estimate represents an average across highly heterogeneous interventions or study conditions, rather than a single effect expected to apply uniformly. Native SMD pooling was used, and the effect measure was explicitly specified; therefore, this is a confirmed standardized-scale result rather than a fallback arising from an unconfirmed effect measure.

A cumulative meta-analysis plot is available at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/cumulative_plot.png`. Because the ordering variable and sequential estimates were not supplied in the numerical context, the cumulative plot should be treated as descriptive and should not be used to infer chronological stabilization without checking its underlying ordering.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study inconsistency was extreme:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 91.656 with 5 degrees of freedom;
- **Q-test p value:** 0.0, as reported by the analysis pipeline;
- **I²:** 97.9%;
- **τ²:** 2.698659.

An I² of 97.9% indicates that nearly all observed dispersion beyond sampling error was attributed to between-study heterogeneity under the model. The τ² estimate of 2.698659 also indicates substantial variance in true effects on the standardized scale. The heterogeneity statistics are summarized graphically at `outputs/HShuwD5_xJc0KHeDZGpcYg/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

The study-level pattern suggests that the extreme heterogeneity is driven in large part by study_8_R8 and study_10_R10, whose estimates were far larger than those of the other four comparisons. However, no prespecified influence analysis, leave-one-out analysis, or outlier-adjusted synthesis was provided. Those studies should therefore not be removed post hoc merely because their results differ. Their divergence instead underscores the need to investigate differences in intervention intensity, participant characteristics, outcome measurement, study conduct, and risk of bias.

Given this degree of heterogeneity, the pooled SMD is of limited standalone interpretability. The average may combine intervention effects generated under substantially different conditions, and the effect in a new setting could differ materially from the pooled estimate. A prediction interval was not supplied and was not reconstructed.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−2.1834** with **p=0.0001**. This highly significant result indicates funnel-plot asymmetry and is consistent with small-study effects or publication bias. It does not, by itself, prove selective publication: asymmetry can also arise from genuine effect modification by study size, methodological differences, outcome-selection processes, or extreme heterogeneity.

The trim-and-fill analysis estimated **k₀=0** missing studies and returned an adjusted estimate of **1.098**, effectively unchanged from the unadjusted pooled estimate. This finding does not negate the Egger result. Trim-and-fill can perform poorly in the presence of severe heterogeneity and with few studies. Based on the supplied analysis, the overall publication-bias risk was classified as **high**.

These diagnostics require particular caution because only six comparisons were available. Conventional guidance generally regards funnel-plot asymmetry tests as unreliable when fewer than approximately 10 studies are included. Here, the very small evidence base and extreme heterogeneity undermine the stability and specificity of both Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill. Nevertheless, the marked asymmetry signal, the concentration of very large effects in smaller studies, and the pipeline’s high-risk classification together warrant serious concern.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis examined whether child-feeding interventions improve vegetable intake in children over a follow-up period of less than 12 months. Across six comparisons and 520 participants, the REML random-effects model yielded a pooled SMD of 1.097981187802555, favoring child-feeding intervention according to the supplied classification. However, the 95% CI ranged from −0.239 to 2.435 and the p value was 0.10760038349207113. The synthesis therefore does not provide statistically conclusive evidence of an average short-term benefit.

The principal finding is not simply the magnitude of the pooled point estimate, but the profound inconsistency among studies. Four effects clustered between −0.339518 and 0.282126, whereas two effects were 2.576669 and 3.950422. This yielded I²=97.9% and τ²=2.698659. Such dispersion suggests that intervention effectiveness may depend strongly on intervention design, population, context, measurement, or study quality. It also means that the pooled average should not be interpreted as the effect expected from every child-feeding intervention.

The two very large effects merit careful verification. Standardized effects can become unusually large when group mean differences are substantial, when within-study variability is small, or when measurement and analytic choices differ across studies. Without the original means, standard deviations, instruments, protocols, and risk-of-bias assessments, it is not possible to distinguish genuine high-impact interventions from scale-related, methodological, or reporting artifacts.

The evidence also showed possible small-study effects. Egger’s p value of 0.0001 was highly significant, although the direction and meaning of asymmetry are complicated by the small number of comparisons and severe heterogeneity. Trim-and-fill did not impute missing studies, but this does not resolve the concern. The combination of a wide pooled confidence interval, extreme heterogeneity, and high publication-bias risk substantially lowers confidence in the apparent average benefit.

From a clinical and policy perspective, these results do not justify assuming that child-feeding interventions will consistently produce a large increase in short-term vegetable intake. They do indicate that some intervention implementations may achieve large effects, making identification of their active components a priority. Future trials should use adequately powered samples, prospectively registered protocols, concealed allocation where relevant, blinded or objective outcome assessment where feasible, standardized and validated intake measures, and complete reporting at prespecified time points. Trials should also report intervention fidelity and contextual features so that heterogeneity can be investigated rather than merely quantified.

## Limitations

1. **Few analyzable comparisons:** Only six effect sizes were pooled. This limits precision, makes random-effects variance estimation less stable, and weakens publication-bias and moderator analyses.
2. **Extreme heterogeneity:** I² was 97.9% and τ² was 2.698659. The pooled mean may not represent any particular intervention or setting well.
3. **Incomplete review-process information:** Search sources, dates, search strategies, screening counts, exclusion reasons, and the relationship between the 15 pipeline studies and six pooled comparisons were unavailable. Search completeness and selection reproducibility therefore cannot be evaluated.
4. **No study-characteristic detail:** Participant, intervention, comparator, setting, outcome-instrument, and follow-up details were not supplied, preventing assessment of clinical comparability and sources of heterogeneity.
5. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** The internal validity of the contributing studies could not be evaluated.
6. **Possible reporting and small-study effects:** Egger’s test indicated pronounced asymmetry (intercept −2.1834; p=0.0001), and publication-bias risk was classified as high. Both Egger’s test and trim-and-fill are unstable with only six studies and substantial heterogeneity.
7. **Reliance on standardized effects:** SMDs permit combination across different measures but are less directly interpretable than servings, frequency, or grams and may be influenced by differences in within-study variability.
8. **No sensitivity analyses:** Influence diagnostics, leave-one-out analyses, alternative τ² estimators, Hartung–Knapp adjustment, and analyses excluding extreme estimates were not supplied.
9. **Short-term scope:** The synthesis deliberately excluded follow-up at 12 months or later. It cannot address persistence of intervention effects.
10. **Outcome specificity:** The results concern vegetable intake only and do not establish effects on total diet quality, nutritional status, growth, or health outcomes.

## Conclusion

Among children, the pooled Standardized Mean Difference for child-feeding interventions versus no intervention was 1.097981187802555 (95% CI −0.239 to 2.435; p=0.10760038349207113; six comparisons; N=520), favoring the child-feeding intervention according to the supplied direction classification. However, the confidence interval included no effect, heterogeneity was extreme (I²=97.9%; τ²=2.698659), and the risk of publication bias was high. The available evidence therefore does not establish a reliable or generalizable increase in short-term vegetable intake. Better-reported, adequately powered trials and prospectively specified subgroup analyses are needed to determine which child-feeding approaches work, for whom, and under what conditions.
