# Effects of Vocabulary Interventions on Immediate Post-Intervention Distal Expressive Second-Language Vocabulary in Children up to Six Years of Age: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Young children learning the societal or school language as a second language (L2) frequently enter formal schooling with smaller L2 vocabularies than their monolingual peers. Shared/dialogic book reading, explicit word teaching and dual-language instruction are widely promoted to narrow this gap. Whether such interventions produce *generalised* gains — that is, improvements on broad, standardised expressive vocabulary tests that are not built from the taught word set — remains uncertain, because most trials emphasise proximal, taught-word outcomes.

**Objective.** To estimate the immediate post-intervention effect of vocabulary interventions versus standard care, usual classroom instruction or no intervention on *distal* (general/untaught-word) expressive L2 vocabulary in children up to six years of age.

**Methods.** We systematically identified controlled trials of vocabulary interventions for L2-learning children aged ≤6 years. One outcome per trial was extracted: the broadest standardised distal expressive L2 vocabulary measure at the first post-intervention assessment. Receptive measures, proximal/taught-word tests, combined receptive+expressive totals, L1 outcomes, delayed follow-ups and non-vocabulary outcomes were excluded. Post-test means, SDs and per-arm n were extracted (SD derived as SE × √n where variability was reported as SE/SEM; model-adjusted means used and flagged where these were the only estimates available). Cluster-randomised trials were handled using cluster-adjusted estimates where reported, otherwise by dividing sample sizes by the design effect 1 + (mean cluster size − 1) × ICC. Multi-arm trials contributed pooled eligible intervention arms with the control arm used once. Hedges' g was computed per trial and pooled using a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Seven studies met the review's substantive inclusion criteria; five contributed extractable distal expressive post-test data and were meta-analysed (k = 5; N = 687 children). Individual Hedges' g estimates ranged from −0.228 to 0.398. The pooled **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** was **0.101 (95% CI −0.064 to 0.266; p = 0.2302)**, a small and statistically non-significant difference; the direction of this estimate **favours the vocabulary intervention** arm. Heterogeneity was low: Q = 4.328 (df = 4), p = 0.3635; I² = 12.24%; τ² = 0.004543. Egger's regression showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept = 0.1821, p = 0.7361) and trim-and-fill imputed one study (k₀ = 1), yielding a near-identical adjusted estimate of 0.1127; overall publication-bias risk was nonetheless rated **moderate** given the small number of trials.

**Conclusions.** Early-childhood vocabulary interventions delivered to L2 learners produced, at most, a small and statistically unreliable advantage on distal, untaught-word expressive L2 vocabulary immediately post-intervention. The confidence interval excludes moderate or large benefits but is also compatible with a small negative effect, so a trivial-to-small generalised benefit cannot be distinguished from no benefit. Low statistical heterogeneity suggests this pattern is reasonably consistent across the available trials, but with only five pooled effect sizes the evidence base is thin and the finding should be read as "generalisation to broad expressive measures is not yet demonstrated" rather than as evidence that these interventions are ineffective on the outcomes they directly target.

## 1. Introduction

Children who begin formal education while still acquiring the societal or school language — the second language (L2) — typically do so with substantially smaller L2 vocabularies than their monolingual peers, simply because their total language exposure is distributed across two or more languages. Because expressive vocabulary at school entry is among the most robust predictors of later reading comprehension and academic achievement, early intervention to accelerate L2 vocabulary growth has become a prominent policy and practice goal in multilingual education systems.

Three intervention families dominate the early-childhood L2 vocabulary literature:

1. **Shared and dialogic book reading** — adult–child or small-group reading in which the adult uses open questions, expansions, recasts and repeated readings to elicit and extend child talk around target words.
2. **Explicit word teaching** — direct, often scripted instruction on a defined word set, typically combining definitions, pictorial and gestural support, multiple exposures and spaced review.
3. **Dual-language (bilingual) instruction** — deliberate structuring of instructional time across L1 and L2, on the theory that consolidating conceptual and lexical knowledge in L1 supports L2 lexical acquisition.

A persistent methodological problem in evaluating these approaches is **outcome proximity**. Most trials assess the specific words taught (a *proximal* measure), on which effects are routinely large — unsurprisingly, since the test is constructed from the curriculum. The clinically and educationally important question is whether gains *generalise*: whether children's broad, standardised expressive L2 vocabulary improves on instruments that were not built from the taught words (*distal* measures). Reviews that pool proximal and distal outcomes together, or that combine receptive and expressive modalities, obscure this distinction and can substantially overstate generalised benefit.

Expressive vocabulary is of particular interest because it is the more demanding modality: a child may recognise a word (receptive knowledge) long before they can retrieve and produce it. Expressive distal measures therefore represent a conservative, high-bar test of whether an intervention has genuinely expanded the child's productive L2 lexicon rather than merely trained a word list.

This meta-analysis addresses a single, deliberately narrow question: **in children up to six years of age learning an L2, what is the effect of vocabulary interventions compared with standard care, usual instruction or no intervention on immediate post-intervention expressive L2 vocabulary measured with distal, general/untaught-word instruments?** By restricting to one outcome modality (expressive), one proximity class (distal), one language (the L2), and one timepoint (first post-intervention assessment), we aim to produce an estimate that is interpretable without the dilution introduced by heterogeneous outcome aggregation.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Children up to six years of age (preschool, kindergarten and early primary) who were learning the societal/school language as a second or additional language. Studies of monolingual children, of children above six years, or of learners of a foreign language with no societal exposure were not eligible for this review's focal estimate.

**Intervention.** Any structured vocabulary intervention, including shared or dialogic book reading, explicit/direct word teaching, and dual-language or bilingual instructional programmes, delivered by teachers, researchers, assistants or parents.

**Comparator.** Standard care, usual classroom instruction, business-as-usual curriculum, waitlist or no intervention. Active comparators consisting of an alternative vocabulary intervention were not treated as eligible controls for the focal contrast.

**Outcome (single outcome per trial).** Expressive vocabulary in the second (societal/school) language, assessed with a **distal** measure — a standardised or otherwise general expressive vocabulary test (e.g. picture naming, definitional vocabulary) *not* constructed from the words taught — at the **first post-intervention assessment**. Where a trial reported more than one eligible instrument, the standardised and broadest distal expressive test was selected.

**Explicit outcome exclusions.** Receptive vocabulary measures; proximal/taught-word tests; combined receptive + expressive composite totals; first-language (L1) outcomes; delayed follow-up assessments; and all non-vocabulary outcomes (e.g. phonological awareness, narrative, comprehension, school readiness).

**Design.** Controlled trials with a concurrent comparison group, including individually randomised, cluster-randomised and quasi-experimental controlled designs, reporting post-test means with a measure of variability and per-arm sample sizes.

### 2.2 Screening and selection

Records were screened in two stages — title/abstract, then full text — against the criteria above. Screening focused sequentially on (i) population age and L2-learner status, (ii) intervention type, (iii) presence of an eligible standard-care/no-intervention comparator, and (iv) availability of an eligible **distal expressive L2** outcome at the immediate post-test with extractable numerical data.

The dominant reason for exclusion at full text was outcome mismatch rather than population or intervention mismatch: many otherwise eligible trials reported only receptive vocabulary (most commonly a picture-vocabulary receptive test), only researcher-designed taught-word probes, or only combined receptive-plus-expressive composites. Such trials were excluded in line with the pre-specified outcome definition, since including them would have conflated proximity and modality.

### 2.3 Yield

**Seven studies** satisfied the substantive population, intervention and comparator criteria and were retained as the included study set. Of these, **five** provided an eligible distal expressive L2 vocabulary outcome at the immediate post-test with sufficient numerical detail (post-test mean, variability and n per arm) to compute an effect size, and these five constitute the quantitative synthesis (k = 5). The remaining two included studies could not be incorporated into the pooled estimate because the required distal expressive post-test statistics were not recoverable from the reports; they are described narratively and are counted in the included-studies total but not in k.

The quantitative synthesis therefore comprises **5 effect sizes from 5 trials, encompassing 687 children**.

## 3. Included Studies

### 3.1 Characteristics

The five trials contributing to the pooled estimate span the three intervention families of interest and are uniformly set in early-childhood or kindergarten contexts where the intervention language is the societal/school language. Sample sizes analysed for the focal outcome ranged widely, from 20 to 239 children (median 115), a spread of more than an order of magnitude. Two trials were comparatively large (n = 232 and n = 239) and together contribute the majority of the analytic information; one trial was very small (n = 20) and contributes a correspondingly wide confidence interval.

All five trials compared a vocabulary intervention with standard care, usual classroom instruction or no intervention, and all contributed exactly one effect size, consistent with the one-measure-per-trial rule. Where a trial reported several eligible distal expressive instruments, the broadest standardised test was selected; this choice was applied consistently and recorded during extraction.

### 3.2 Data extraction conventions

The following pre-specified conventions were applied:

- **Variability.** Where variability was reported as a standard error or SEM rather than an SD, the SD was reconstructed as SE × √n.
- **Adjusted means.** Where only model-based/covariate-adjusted means were reported (e.g. from ANCOVA or multilevel models), these were used and the fact noted at extraction; adjusted means are generally preferable for baseline-imbalanced designs but are not perfectly interchangeable with raw post-test means.
- **Cluster-randomised trials.** Cluster-adjusted effect estimates were used when reported by the authors. Where they were not, the effective sample size was obtained by dividing the per-arm n by the design effect 1 + (mean cluster size − 1) × ICC, using the trial's own reported ICC where available and an assumed ICC of 0.15 otherwise, with the source of the ICC recorded for each trial.
- **Multi-arm trials.** Eligible intervention arms were combined (means and SDs pooled, sample sizes summed), or, where arms were substantively distinct, the single arm matching the review question was selected and the choice stated. In either case the shared control arm was used only once, preventing within-study double-counting of control participants.

### 3.3 Studies included but not pooled

Two of the seven included studies could not contribute to the quantitative synthesis. In both cases the barrier was reporting completeness for the focal outcome rather than design quality: the distal expressive post-test was either described without the accompanying dispersion statistics or reported only in a form (e.g. change scores without SDs, or significance statements without estimates) from which an unbiased standardised mean difference could not be reconstructed under the pre-specified rules. No imputation of missing SDs from other trials was performed, in order to avoid introducing artificial precision. This is a form of selective-reporting-driven attrition from the synthesis and is revisited in the Limitations.

## 4. Effect Sizes

Effect sizes were computed as **Hedges' g** (the standardised mean difference with the small-sample bias correction applied), with positive values indicating that the intervention group achieved *higher* distal expressive L2 vocabulary scores than the control group — i.e. a result favouring the vocabulary intervention.

|---|---:|---:|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | 232 | −0.039 | [−0.298, 0.219] | Slightly favours control (CI spans null) |
| study_2_R2 | 20 | −0.228 | [−1.112, 0.656] | Slightly favours control (CI spans null) |
| study_3_R3 | 115 | 0.398 | [0.029, 0.768] | Favours intervention (CI excludes null) |
| study_6_R6 | 239 | 0.139 | [−0.115, 0.393] | Slightly favours intervention (CI spans null) |
| study_7_R7 | 81 | 0.036 | [−0.400, 0.472] | Essentially null |
| **Total** | **687** | — | — | — |

Four observations follow directly from Table 1:

1. **The spread of point estimates is modest.** The full range is 0.626 standard deviation units (−0.228 to 0.398), which is narrow for an educational intervention literature, and three of the five estimates lie between −0.04 and +0.14 — i.e. essentially at zero.
2. **Only one trial reached conventional significance in isolation** (study_3_R3, g = 0.398, 95% CI [0.029, 0.768]); its lower bound sits just above zero, so even this result is compatible with a near-trivial effect.
3. **The two negative estimates are small and imprecise.** The more negative of the two (study_2_R2, g = −0.228) comes from the smallest sample (n = 20) and has an interval spanning more than 1.7 g units; it carries very little weight.
4. **Precision is concentrated in the two largest trials** (study_1_R1 and study_6_R6, together n = 471, 69% of the analysed sample), whose estimates are −0.039 and 0.139 — bracketing zero.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A single meta-analytic group was identified: all five pooled effect sizes share the same outcome construct (distal expressive L2 vocabulary) and the same timepoint (first/immediate post-intervention assessment). No separate outcome or timepoint subgroups required independent pooling, so one overall estimate is reported.

Effects were pooled under a **random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**, which is appropriate given the expectation of between-study variation in populations, intervention intensity and instruments.

**Table 2. Random-effects meta-analysis — immediate post-intervention distal expressive L2 vocabulary.**

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** |
| Pooled effect | **0.101** |
| 95% confidence interval | **[−0.064, 0.266]** |
| p-value | **0.2302** |
| k (effect sizes pooled) | 5 |
| N (participants) | 687 |
| Model / estimator | Random effects, REML |
| Q (df = 4) | 4.328 (p = 0.3635) |
| I² | 12.24% |
| τ² | 0.004543 |
| Favours | Vocabulary intervention (shared/dialogic book reading, explicit word teaching, or dual-language instruction) for young L2 learners |

The effect measure for this synthesis is the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, which was the measure explicitly requested for the review question and which was used natively in the pooling step (native pooling confirmed). The pooled value is therefore a confirmed native-scale SMD, not a converted or fallback statistic. Because all contributing trials reported continuous post-test scores on non-commensurate instruments, standardisation is the only defensible common metric; the study-level inputs are small-sample-corrected (Hedges' g) standardised mean differences.

### 5.1 Interpretation of the pooled estimate

The pooled SMD of **0.101** corresponds, by conventional benchmarks, to a **small-to-negligible** difference — roughly one tenth of a within-group standard deviation. Expressed differently, it implies that the average intervention child scored at approximately the 54th percentile of the control distribution (vs. the 50th by definition), and the overlap between the two distributions is about 96%. The associated p-value of **0.2302** indicates that a difference of this size or larger would not be unusual under the null hypothesis of no effect, so the result does **not** meet conventional criteria for statistical significance.

The direction of the pooled estimate **favours the vocabulary intervention** arm. It is important to state the two implications of this precisely and separately: (i) the *point estimate* leans toward benefit from vocabulary intervention; (ii) the *interval* [−0.064, 0.266] is fully compatible with no effect and with a small effect in either direction. The interval's upper bound (0.266) rules out moderate or large generalised benefits with reasonable confidence; its lower bound (−0.064) is close enough to zero that a meaningful *harm* is also implausible. The honest summary is therefore: **no demonstrated generalised benefit, with moderate-to-large benefits effectively excluded and any true effect most plausibly lying between negligible and small.**

An approximate 95% prediction interval, incorporating τ² = 0.004543 and the small number of studies, spans roughly −0.24 to 0.44, indicating that the effect in a new, comparable trial would most likely also fall in the trivial-to-small range, though with a possibility of a modest positive result.

### 5.2 Cumulative and sensitivity perspectives

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows the pooled estimate stabilising as the larger trials enter the synthesis, with no sustained drift away from the null. Because the two largest trials (n = 232 and n = 239) carry the greatest weight and both produce near-null estimates, the pooled result is structurally robust: no single trial's removal would be expected to move the pooled estimate across a threshold of educational importance. The one trial with a nominally significant positive effect (study_3_R3, g = 0.398, n = 115) is the principal source of the positive sign of the pooled estimate; with that trial removed, the remaining estimates (−0.039, −0.228, 0.139, 0.036) would pool to a value very close to zero. This dependence of the (already non-significant) positive direction on a single moderate-sized trial should temper any optimistic reading.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **low** on every index:

- **Cochran's Q = 4.328 on 4 degrees of freedom, p = 0.3635.** The observed dispersion of effect sizes is almost exactly what would be expected from sampling error alone (E[Q] = df = 4 under homogeneity); the test provides no evidence against a common underlying effect.
- **I² = 12.24%.** Only about one eighth of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to true between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. By the usual interpretive bands this sits in the "might not be important" range.
- **τ² = 0.004543**, implying a between-study standard deviation of τ ≈ 0.067 SMD units — a very narrow dispersion of true effects around the pooled mean of 0.101.

Heterogeneity diagnostics are summarised visually in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

Two interpretive cautions apply. First, **Q and I² are poorly powered when k = 5**; a non-significant Q with four degrees of freedom is weak evidence of homogeneity rather than confirmation of it, and I² estimates are themselves imprecise in small syntheses. Second, the low heterogeneity is in part a *consequence of the narrow outcome definition*: by restricting to a single modality (expressive), a single proximity class (distal), a single language (L2) and a single timepoint (immediate post-test), a major source of cross-study variation present in broader reviews was eliminated by design. The homogeneity observed here should thus be read as internal consistency of a tightly specified contrast, not as evidence that vocabulary interventions behave uniformly across all outcomes.

The practical consequence of low τ² is that the fixed- and random-effects estimates would be very close, and that the pooled estimate functions as a reasonably stable summary of the set rather than an average over qualitatively divergent findings.

## 7. Moderators

No formal moderator (meta-regression or subgroup) analysis was conducted, and none would be defensible with **k = 5**. Standard guidance recommends a minimum of approximately ten studies per covariate for meta-regression; with five effect sizes and a τ² of only 0.004543, there is essentially no between-study variance available for any moderator to explain, and any apparent association would be driven by one or two trials.

The moderators that would be of greatest substantive interest, and that should be targeted by future syntheses once the evidence base grows, are:

- **Intervention family** — shared/dialogic book reading vs. explicit word teaching vs. dual-language instruction. These differ in theorised mechanism: dialogic reading targets productive language use directly (and might plausibly transfer best to *expressive* distal measures), whereas explicit word teaching targets a defined lexical set (and might plausibly show the largest proximal–distal gap).
- **Dose and duration** — total contact hours, session frequency and programme length.
- **Implementer** — researcher, trained teacher, classroom assistant or parent, which bears on fidelity and on scalability.
- **Language of instruction** — L2-only versus structured L1 support.
- **Baseline L2 proficiency** and socioeconomic composition of the sample.
- **Instrument** — which standardised expressive test was used, and its sensitivity/floor characteristics in L2 learners.
- **Design features** — individual versus cluster randomisation, and whether cluster adjustment used a reported or assumed ICC.

The exploratory relationship between study precision/size and observed effect is displayed in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`). This plot is descriptive only; with five points no inferential claim about any moderator is warranted. It is worth noting descriptively, however, that the two largest trials yielded the smallest absolute effects and the single significant positive effect came from a mid-sized trial — a pattern consistent either with chance or with mild small-study effects, and distinguishable from neither at this k.

## 8. Publication Bias

Two complementary diagnostics were applied.

**Egger's regression test.** The intercept was **0.1821** with **p = 0.7361**, providing no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry. The intercept's magnitude is small and its p-value far from conventional thresholds.

**Trim-and-fill.** The procedure imputed **k₀ = 1** potentially missing study, and the bias-adjusted pooled estimate was **0.1127** — marginally *larger* than, and substantively indistinguishable from, the observed estimate of 0.101. Adjustment for small-study effects therefore does not change any conclusion.

**Overall risk rating: moderate.**

As the automated appraisal notes, Egger's regression shows no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.7361), and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed only one potentially missing study (k₀ = 1), suggesting that any adjustment to the pooled effect would be negligible — there is no direct statistical evidence of small-study effects or publication bias in this body of evidence. The "moderate" rating, despite these reassuring test results, reflects considerations the tests themselves cannot capture: above all, the **small number of included studies** (Egger's test is substantially underpowered with fewer than approximately ten studies, so a null result carries little information), heterogeneity that can mask or mimic asymmetry, and qualitative concerns such as limited grey-literature coverage and the absence of trial registrations in much of the early-childhood language-intervention literature.

A further, review-specific consideration raises concern beyond the numeric diagnostics: this synthesis is restricted to **distal, untaught-word expressive** outcomes, which are precisely the outcomes most vulnerable to *selective within-study reporting*. Trials in this field commonly report proximal taught-word gains prominently and distal expressive results incompletely or not at all. Indeed, two of the seven included studies were lost from the pooled analysis for exactly this reason (insufficient distal expressive reporting). If distal expressive outcomes are reported more fully when they are favourable, the available pooled estimate of 0.101 would be an *over*-estimate of the true generalised effect — a bias that funnel-based methods, which key on study size rather than outcome selection, cannot detect.

**Bottom line:** publication bias is unlikely to materially alter the pooled estimate based on the available diagnostics, but the findings should still be interpreted with caution, since absence of detected bias is not strong evidence of absence of bias — particularly with k = 5 and with documented incomplete reporting of the focal outcome.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Principal finding

Across five controlled trials and 687 children up to six years of age learning a second language, vocabulary interventions produced a pooled Standardized Mean Difference of **0.101 (95% CI −0.064 to 0.266; p = 0.2302)** on **distal, untaught-word expressive L2 vocabulary** at the immediate post-intervention assessment. The point estimate favours the vocabulary intervention, but the result is small, non-significant, and compatible with no effect.

### 9.2 Reconciling this with the wider literature

This result sits in apparent tension with the frequently reported conclusion that early vocabulary interventions "work", and the tension is almost entirely explained by **outcome proximity and modality**.

- **Proximity.** Trials of explicit word teaching and dialogic reading reliably produce large effects on the specific words taught. Those effects are real and educationally meaningful for the taught set, but they are not evidence of generalisation. The present review deliberately excluded taught-word probes, and the effect shrinks accordingly.
- **Modality.** Receptive gains typically exceed expressive gains, because recognition precedes production. By restricting to expressive outcomes, this review applies the stricter test — and interventions of the typical dose (often a few weeks to a few months, in small-group sessions) may simply be too brief to shift the breadth of a child's *productive* lexicon enough to register on a standardised instrument.
- **Instrument sensitivity.** Standardised expressive vocabulary tests are normed, broad-coverage instruments sampling thousands of lexical items. Teaching 20–40 target words — even extremely effectively — cannot move such a score by much, because the taught items constitute a negligible fraction of the item pool. A near-null distal effect is, to some degree, an arithmetic expectation rather than evidence of pedagogical failure. For L2 learners specifically, these instruments may also exhibit floor effects, further compressing detectable differences.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore **not** that shared reading, explicit word teaching or dual-language instruction are ineffective. It is that, on current evidence, their benefits have **not been shown to generalise** to broad productive L2 vocabulary measured immediately after a typical intervention period.

### 9.3 Consistency of the evidence

A notable feature of this synthesis is its internal consistency: I² = 12.24%, τ² = 0.004543, and Q = 4.328 (p = 0.3635). Three of the five trials returned estimates within ±0.14 of zero, and the two largest trials — contributing 69% of participants — returned −0.039 and 0.139. The near-null result is not an average of strongly conflicting findings; it is a reasonably uniform signal across trials differing in intervention type and setting. That consistency strengthens confidence in the *direction and magnitude* of the conclusion even as the small k limits confidence in its *precision*.

### 9.4 Implications for practice

For practitioners and commissioners, three implications follow:

1. **Expect targeted, not global, gains in the short term.** Interventions should be selected and evaluated against the vocabulary they actually teach, with distal generalisation treated as an additional, harder-to-achieve goal rather than an assumed by-product.
2. **Dose and duration likely matter.** If the mechanism for distal gain is cumulative lexical growth and increased productive language use, brief programmes are unlikely to register on standardised expressive measures. Sustained, curriculum-embedded approaches are more plausible candidates for generalised effects than short add-on blocks.
3. **Do not read this as a reason to withdraw provision.** The confidence interval excludes meaningful harm, the interventions are generally low-cost and low-risk, and their established proximal and receptive benefits are not addressed by this estimate.

### 9.5 Implications for research

Future trials should: (i) pre-register and fully report **both** proximal and distal outcomes, in **both** receptive and expressive modalities, so that generalisation can be quantified rather than inferred; (ii) report post-test means, SDs and per-arm n in full, including for null distal results; (iii) in cluster-randomised designs, report the ICC and mean cluster size so that design effects need not be assumed; (iv) include **delayed follow-ups**, since generalisation may emerge only after taught vocabulary has been consolidated and reused; and (v) be powered for small effects — detecting an SMD of 0.10 with 80% power requires roughly 1,570 children per arm, far beyond any trial in this set, which implies that the field should plan either substantially larger trials or prospective pooled analyses.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were raised for this synthesis. The following data-handling facts are nonetheless recorded for transparency, as each bears on how the pooled estimate should be read:

- **Seven studies were included; five contributed effect sizes.** Two included studies could not be pooled because the distal expressive post-test statistics required for a standardised mean difference were not recoverable from the reports. No SDs were imputed from other trials. The pooled estimate therefore rests on five of seven eligible trials, and the two omitted trials represent an unquantified risk of outcome-reporting bias.
- **Derived variability.** Where variability was reported as SE or SEM, SDs were reconstructed as SE × √n. This is exact only if the reported SE is the standard error of the arm mean; departures from that assumption introduce small errors in the weights.
- **Adjusted means.** Where only covariate-adjusted/model-based means were available, these were used and flagged. Adjusted and unadjusted means are not strictly interchangeable, and pairing adjusted means with unadjusted SDs can modestly misstate precision.
- **Cluster adjustment.** Cluster-randomised trials were handled with author-reported cluster-adjusted estimates where available; otherwise sample sizes were divided by the design effect 1 + (mean cluster size − 1) × ICC, using a reported ICC where given and an assumed ICC of 0.15 otherwise. Which ICC source applied was recorded per trial. Reliance on an assumed ICC makes the corresponding weights approximate.
- **Multi-arm trials.** Eligible intervention arms were combined (means/SDs pooled, n summed) or a single matching arm was selected with the choice stated; the shared control arm was used only once in each case, so no control participants are double-counted.
- **One outcome per trial.** Each trial contributes exactly one effect size, so the five effect sizes are statistically independent and no multiplicity correction for within-study dependence was required.
- **Effect measure.** The Standardized Mean Difference was the explicitly requested measure and was used natively in pooling (native pooling confirmed); the reported pooled value is a confirmed native-scale SMD, not a standardized fallback conversion.
- **Outcome exclusions applied as specified.** Receptive measures, proximal/taught-word tests, combined receptive+expressive totals, L1 outcomes, delayed follow-ups and non-vocabulary outcomes were excluded throughout; the many trials reporting only such outcomes were not pooled.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small number of pooled studies (k = 5).** This is the dominant limitation. It constrains the precision of the pooled estimate, renders heterogeneity statistics unstable, makes Egger's test substantially underpowered, and precludes any moderator analysis.
2. **Low statistical power for a small expected effect.** With N = 687 total, the synthesis has limited power to detect an SMD of ~0.10. The non-significant result should not be equated with a demonstrated null.
3. **Incomplete outcome reporting and resulting attrition from the synthesis.** Two of seven included studies were lost for lack of extractable distal expressive data, and many screened trials were excluded for reporting only receptive or taught-word outcomes. If distal expressive results are reported more completely when favourable, the pooled estimate is biased upward.
4. **Instrument insensitivity and possible floor effects.** Broad standardised expressive tests may be intrinsically insensitive to short vocabulary interventions and may show floor effects in young L2 learners with limited L2 proficiency, both of which compress observable differences toward zero.
5. **Assumed ICC for some cluster-randomised trials.** Where no ICC was reported, a value of 0.15 was assumed. Mis-specification would alter effective sample sizes and hence weights, although with low τ² the pooled estimate is relatively insensitive to moderate weight changes.
6. **Mixing of adjusted and unadjusted means.** Use of model-based adjusted means in some trials alongside raw post-test means in others introduces minor non-comparability.
7. **Aggregation across heterogeneous interventions.** Shared/dialogic book reading, explicit word teaching and dual-language instruction differ substantially in mechanism; pooling them answers a broad question ("do vocabulary interventions generalise?") at the cost of masking family-specific effects that could not be separated at this k.
8. **Immediate post-test only.** By design, delayed outcomes were excluded. Generalised effects that emerge or consolidate later are invisible to this analysis.
9. **No formal risk-of-bias synthesis is reported here.** Study-level methodological quality (allocation concealment, blinding of outcome assessors, attrition, baseline comparability) was not incorporated as a quantitative moderator or sensitivity analysis, so the pooled estimate is unweighted by internal validity.
10. **Unassessed language-pair and context variation.** L1–L2 typological distance, community language status and L2 exposure outside school differ across trials and could not be examined.

## 12. Conclusion

In children up to six years of age who are learning a second language, vocabulary interventions — shared/dialogic book reading, explicit word teaching and dual-language instruction — were associated with a pooled **Standardized Mean Difference of 0.101 (95% CI −0.064 to 0.266; p = 0.2302; k = 5; N = 687)** on **distal, untaught-word expressive second-language vocabulary** at the immediate post-intervention assessment. The direction of this estimate **favours the vocabulary intervention**, but the effect is small, statistically non-significant, and compatible with no difference. Heterogeneity was low (I² = 12.24%; τ² = 0.004543; Q = 4.328, df = 4, p = 0.3635), indicating a reasonably consistent near-null pattern across trials. Publication-bias diagnostics were reassuring (Egger's p = 0.7361; trim-and-fill k₀ = 1, adjusted estimate 0.1127) but underpowered, and overall bias risk was rated moderate.

The defensible conclusion is that **generalisation of early vocabulary intervention effects to broad, productive second-language vocabulary has not been demonstrated**, while moderate or large generalised benefits can be reasonably excluded. This is a statement about the *distal expressive* outcome at the *immediate* post-test and must not be read as evidence that these widely used interventions lack value: their effects on taught vocabulary and on receptive knowledge lie outside this review's scope. Progress will require trials that are larger, longer, fully reported across both proximity classes and both modalities, and that follow children beyond the end of the intervention period.
