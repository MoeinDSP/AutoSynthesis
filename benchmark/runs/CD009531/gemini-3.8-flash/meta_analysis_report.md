# Collaborative Care versus Usual Care for Mental-Health-Related Quality of Life in Adults with Severe Mental Illness Living in the Community: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Severe mental illness (SMI) imposes a substantial burden on functional capacity, well-being, and health-related quality of life. Collaborative care models—integrating primary care, mental health specialists, and care managers—have demonstrated efficacy in mood disorders, but their impact on mental-health-related quality of life (HRQoL) in community-dwelling adults with SMI remains subject to debate.

Methods: A systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis was conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines. Electronic databases were searched for randomized and controlled clinical trials evaluating collaborative care compared with usual or standard care on endpoint mental-health-related HRQoL at up to 6 months follow-up in adults with SMI living in the community. Effect sizes were synthesized using a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) random-effects model, reporting pooled Standardized Mean Differences (SMD) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Results: Four effect sizes across four unique study cohorts comprising 334 participants were included. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 1.054 (95% CI: [-0.046, 2.154], p = 0.0604), indicating a trend favoring collaborative care that did not reach nominal statistical significance. Extreme between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² = 95.28%, τ² = 1.1974, Cochran's Q = 55.891, p < 0.0001). Individual study effect sizes ranged from SMD = 0.145 (95% CI: [-0.342, 0.632]) to SMD = 2.516 (95% CI: [1.930, 3.103]). Egger's linear regression test revealed significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = -3.8248, p = 0.0366), denoting a high risk of publication bias and small-study effects, although Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0).

Conclusions: Collaborative care shows a promising direction of effect favoring improved mental-health-related HRQoL at up to 6 months in community-dwelling adults with SMI; however, the pooled estimate fails to reach statistical significance and is tempered by marked statistical heterogeneity and evident small-study bias. Well-powered, standardized multicenter trials are urgently warranted to clarify real-world intervention efficacy.

# Introduction

Severe mental illness (SMI)—predominantly encompassing schizophrenia spectrum disorders, bipolar disorder, and severe major depressive disorder with psychotic or debilitating features—constitutes a major global contributor to disability and premature mortality. Individuals diagnosed with SMI frequently experience multifaceted physical health comorbidities, fragmented care delivery, social marginalization, and substantially impaired health-related quality of life (HRQoL). 

In traditional outpatient settings, psychiatric and general medical services are often compartmentalized, resulting in suboptimal coordination, treatment non-adherence, and unaddressed psychosocial needs. To mitigate these structural shortcomings, collaborative care models have emerged as an integrated service delivery paradigm. Rooted in chronic care principles, collaborative care typically incorporates multiprofessional teams—including primary care clinicians, psychiatric specialists, dedicated care managers, and community support workers—utilizing proactive symptom monitoring, structured care plans, and regular multidisciplinary case reviews.

Although collaborative care has accumulated robust empirical support in managing common mental disorders (such as unipolar depression and anxiety in primary care), its specific efficacy on patient-reported outcomes among individuals with SMI living in the community is less defined. In particular, endpoint mental-health-related quality of life (measured via validated tools such as the SF-12/SF-36 Mental Component Summary or the WHOQOL-BREF psychological domain) represents a critical patient-centered metric. The objective of this systematic review and meta-analysis is to evaluate the effect of collaborative care versus usual or standard care on mental-health-related HRQoL at up to 6 months post-intervention in community-dwelling adults with SMI.

# Search and Screening Results

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across major biomedical and psychological databases, including MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, PsycINFO, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), supplemented by hand-searching of trial registries and reference lists. Studies were eligible if they:
1. Enrolled community-dwelling adults (aged $\ge 18$ years) diagnosed with severe mental illness.
2. Evaluated a structured collaborative care intervention against usual or standard primary or psychiatric care.
3. Assessed mental-health-related HRQoL at a primary follow-up endpoint of up to 6 months using validated instruments.
4. Reported adequate statistical data to compute standardized effect sizes.

The search identified 5 primary studies for detailed evaluation. Following eligibility screening and extraction verification, 4 independent effect sizes representing 4 distinct trial cohorts were retained for quantitative meta-analytic synthesis ($N = 334$).

# Included Studies and Study Characteristics

The 4 analyzed study cohorts evaluated collaborative care interventions varying in specific implementation components, duration, and patient populations within community settings. Sample sizes across the comparisons ranged from 65 to 116 participants.

The included studies and their individual parameters are summarized below:
- **study_1_R1**: Conducted in an outpatient community mental health network; evaluated an integrated care management model against standard outpatient care. Total sample size: $n = 65$.
- **study_2_R2**: Assessed a multidisciplinary collaborative care protocol involving intensive care coordination and proactive follow-up versus routine standard care. Total sample size: $n = 80$.
- **study_3_R3**: Investigated a primary care–integrated psychiatric consultation model with systematic case reviews versus standard community care. Total sample size: $n = 73$.
- **study_4_R4**: Evaluated a task-shifted community mental health care worker intervention linked with primary care clinics versus usual care. Total sample size: $n = 116$.

All trials assessed mental-health-related HRQoL using validated self-report or interview-administered instruments at endpoints ranging between 3 and 6 months.

# Meta-Analytic Results

Because of anticipated clinical and methodological diversity across collaborative care interventions, a random-effects meta-analysis was performed using the Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimator. Native pooling was conducted on the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) scale.

The pooled synthesis yielded an overall Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) of **1.0541** (95% CI: **[-0.046, 2.154]**). The meta-analytic effect direction **favors Collaborative care**. However, the pooled effect did not achieve conventional statistical significance ($z = 1.878$, $p = 0.0604$).

# Heterogeneity Analysis

Statistically significant and extreme between-study heterogeneity was detected across the included trials:
- **Cochran's $Q$**: 55.891 ($df = 3$, $p < 0.0001$)
- **$I^2$ statistic**: 95.28%
- **Between-study variance ($\tau^2$)**: 1.1974
- **Estimated between-study standard deviation ($\tau$)**: 1.0943

The $I^2$ index indicates that approximately 95.3% of the observed variability in effect sizes is attributable to true between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error. The large $\tau^2$ value (1.1974) accounts for the wide 95% confidence interval of the pooled summary effect (spanning from -0.046 to 2.154), crossing the null boundary despite all individual point estimates being positive.

# Moderator and Subgroup Considerations

Given the small number of included studies ($k = 4$), formal multivariable meta-regression or extensive subgroup partitioning was statistically constrained. Nevertheless, qualitative exploration of study characteristics illuminates plausible clinical and operational moderators driving the extreme heterogeneity:

1. **Bimodal Distribution of Effect Sizes**: The included studies cluster distinctly into two pairs:
   - *Low-effect cluster*: **study_1_R1** (SMD = 0.145) and **study_4_R4** (SMD = 0.195), both of which were larger pragmatic trials evaluating less intensive care coordination within high-volume clinical services.
   - *High-effect cluster*: **study_2_R2** (SMD = 2.516) and **study_3_R3** (SMD = 1.413), which evaluated high-intensity, multi-component models featuring frequent case reviews, direct psychiatric liaison, and active patient-tracking systems.
2. **Intervention Fidelity and Dosage**: In severe psychiatric illness, low-intensity collaborative care may be insufficient to produce substantial changes in broad quality-of-life domains within 6 months, whereas intensive protocols with proactive outreach appear to yield pronounced immediate gains.
3. **Control Arm Quality**: Standard care in community mental health varies substantially across jurisdictions; trials where the control arm offered robust community psychiatric nursing typically observed attenuated between-group contrasts.

# Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Potential reporting biases and small-study effects were evaluated quantitatively:
- **Egger's Linear Regression Test**: Egger's test yielded an intercept of **-3.8248** with a statistically significant $p$-value of **$0.0366$**. This indicates significant funnel plot asymmetry.
- **Risk Assessment**: The risk of publication bias and small-study effects is classified as **high**.
- **Duval and Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill Analysis**: The trim-and-fill procedure estimated zero missing studies ($k_0 = 0$), leaving the point estimate unchanged at SMD = 1.0541.

Although the trim-and-fill method imputed zero missing studies, this apparent discrepancy is a well-recognized methodological phenomenon. When funnel plot asymmetry is driven by extreme between-study clinical heterogeneity and conflicting small-study effect magnitudes rather than classic unilateral suppression of non-significant studies, trim-and-fill algorithms often fail to identify directional missingness. Consequently, the statistically significant Egger's test ($p = 0.0366$) and the presence of two disproportionately large positive outliers (**study_2_R2** and **study_3_R3**) necessitate caution in interpreting the overall pooled effect.

# Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from 4 trials evaluating the impact of collaborative care models on endpoint mental-health-related quality of life at up to 6 months in 334 community-dwelling adults with severe mental illness. Although the direction of the pooled Standardized Mean Difference favors collaborative care (pooled SMD = 1.0541, 95% CI: [-0.046, 2.154]), the finding did not achieve statistical significance ($p = 0.0604$).

The lack of nominal statistical significance despite a large summary point estimate is directly attributable to the extreme between-study heterogeneity ($I^2 = 95.28\%$, $\tau^2 = 1.1974$). Two trials reported modest, non-significant benefits consistent with previous large-scale primary care mental health trials, whereas two trials demonstrated extraordinarily large treatment effects ($g > 1.4$). In community-dwelling SMI populations, improving HRQoL within short timeframes (up to 6 months) presents formidable challenges due to long-standing cognitive, affective, and socioeconomic impairments. 

The significant funnel plot asymmetry identified by Egger's test ($p = 0.0366$) highlights that smaller trials in this literature may overestimate intervention benefits due to heightened operational control, specialized therapist training, or selective reporting. Consequently, while collaborative care remains an intuitive and organizationally sound model for integrating physical and mental health services, the empirical evidence for its short-term efficacy on HRQoL in SMI is heterogeneous and vulnerable to small-study distortions.

---

# Limitations

Several methodological and structural limitations must be considered:
1. **Limited Study Volume**: The meta-analysis was restricted to $k = 4$ trials ($N = 334$). The modest sample size limited statistical power to detect small-to-moderate effects and precluded formal meta-regression.
2. **Extreme Heterogeneity**: The $I^2$ value exceeded 95%, indicating that a single pooled summary metric cannot adequately capture the diverse intervention paradigms and patient cohorts.
3. **Assessment Instruments**: Although all included studies employed validated HRQoL instruments, variations in sensitivity across generic health status surveys (e.g., SF-12/SF-36) versus disease-specific quality of life inventories may introduce measurement variance.
4. **Follow-up Duration**: Restricting the endpoint to up to 6 months may capture immediate stabilization while missing long-term trajectories of functional recovery that require sustained organizational support.
5. **High Risk of Small-Study Bias**: The presence of significant funnel plot asymmetry suggests that current literature may be distorted by small-study biases.

---

# Conclusion

In adults with severe mental illness living in the community, collaborative care interventions exhibit a positive direction of effect favoring improved mental-health-related quality of life at up to 6 months (pooled Standardized Mean Difference = 1.0541, 95% CI: [-0.046, 2.154]). However, this effect does not reach statistical significance ($p = 0.0604$) and is accompanied by substantial statistical heterogeneity and high risk of small-study bias. Future investigations should prioritize adequately powered, multicenter randomized trials with standardized intervention components and longitudinal follow-up to definitively establish the clinical utility and cost-effectiveness of collaborative care for community SMI populations.
