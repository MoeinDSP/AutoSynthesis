# Collaborative Care and Mental-Health-Domain Health-Related Quality of Life in Adults with Severe Mental Illness: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomised Trials at ≤6-Month Follow-Up

## Abstract

**Background.** Collaborative care — structured, multi-professional, measurement-guided management delivered in community and outpatient settings — is well established for depression, but its value for adults with severe mental illness (SMI; bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, other psychotic disorders) is less certain. Patient-reported mental-health-domain health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is a person-centred endpoint that is rarely synthesised separately from symptom or functioning scales.

**Objective.** To determine whether collaborative care improves mental-health-domain HRQoL (SF-12/SF-36 Mental Component Summary [MCS] or WHOQOL-BREF psychological domain) compared with usual care in adults with SMI, at the follow-up closest to and no later than 6 months.

**Methods.** We systematically identified randomised controlled trials comparing collaborative care with usual care in adults with SMI treated in community or outpatient settings. One eligible score per trial was extracted as a post-intervention (endpoint) mean and SD per arm, not as change from baseline: SF-12/SF-36 MCS or the WHOQOL-BREF psychological domain. Physical component/domain scores, total or overall quality-of-life scores, symptom scales, functioning scales and satisfaction scales were ineligible, as were follow-ups at ≥12 months. Randomised group sizes were used where analysed Ns were not reported, and standard errors or 95% confidence intervals were converted to SDs using the group n. Effect sizes were computed as Hedges' g and pooled on the standardized mean difference (SMD) scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ²; small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Five studies entered the pipeline and four contributed an analysable mental-health-domain HRQoL effect size at ≤6 months (k = 4; N = 346 participants). Individual Hedges' g values were 0.129 (95% CI −0.358 to 0.616; n = 65), 2.523 (1.975 to 3.071; n = 92), 1.413 (0.900 to 1.927; n = 73) and 0.195 (−0.170 to 0.560; n = 116). The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 1.055 (95% CI −0.055 to 2.166; p = 0.0625), a direction favouring collaborative care but not statistically significant at the conventional 5% threshold. Heterogeneity was extreme (Q = 61.291, df = 3, p < 0.001; I² = 95.51%; τ² = 1.223), with a clearly bimodal distribution of trial-level effects (two near-null, two very large). Egger's test showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = −4.0245, p = 0.1072) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted SMD unchanged at 1.0553), supporting a low risk of publication (small-study) bias, albeit with limited power at k = 4. With only four effect sizes, no moderator analysis or meta-regression was statistically justifiable.

**Conclusions.** Across four small randomised trials, collaborative care was associated with a large but statistically non-significant improvement in mental-health-domain HRQoL relative to usual care at ≤6 months. The pooled estimate is unstable: it is driven by two trials with implausibly large effects, the confidence interval crosses the null, and between-study inconsistency is near the maximum observable (I² = 95.51%). The evidence is therefore insufficient to support or refute a benefit of collaborative care on mental-health HRQoL in SMI, and adequately powered trials reporting MCS or WHOQOL-BREF psychological-domain endpoints are required.

## 1. Introduction

Severe mental illness (SMI) — here comprising bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder and other psychotic disorders — is associated with substantial and persistent impairment in subjective wellbeing, social participation and self-rated health. Most people with SMI are now managed predominantly in community and outpatient settings, where care is frequently fragmented across primary care, specialist mental health services, and social and physical health providers. Fragmentation has been repeatedly implicated in undertreatment, poor continuity, and the well-documented mortality gap experienced by this population.

Collaborative care is a complex service-delivery intervention developed to address precisely this fragmentation. Its canonical components include a defined multi-professional team (typically a care manager working with a primary care clinician and a supervising specialist), a structured management plan, scheduled proactive follow-up with systematic outcome measurement, and enhanced inter-professional communication. The evidence base for collaborative care in common mental disorders — particularly unipolar depression and anxiety in primary care — is extensive and broadly positive. Whether these gains extend to people with SMI is less clear. SMI populations differ in the chronicity and relapsing course of illness, the centrality of antipsychotic and mood-stabiliser pharmacotherapy, the degree of cognitive and negative symptomatology, and the frequent coexistence of substance use and cardiometabolic comorbidity. These differences plausibly alter both the mechanism and the magnitude of any collaborative-care effect.

Most syntheses in this field have prioritised clinician-rated symptom severity, service-use metrics, or composite functioning scores. Patient-reported health-related quality of life (HRQoL) is a distinct and arguably more person-centred construct, and within HRQoL the mental-health domain is conceptually closest to the target of psychosocial service redesign. Pooling mental-health-domain scores together with physical-domain or total quality-of-life scores obscures effects, because collaborative-care programmes for SMI often incorporate physical-health components with their own, separate pathway to benefit. For this reason the present review deliberately restricted synthesis to a single mental-health-domain HRQoL score per trial.

### 1.1 Objective

To estimate the effect of collaborative care, compared with usual care, on mental-health-domain HRQoL (SF-12 or SF-36 Mental Component Summary [MCS], or the WHOQOL-BREF psychological domain) in adults with SMI treated in community or outpatient settings, measured at the follow-up closest to and no later than 6 months.

A positive Hedges' g / positive SMD denotes better mental-health-related quality of life (a higher MCS or WHOQOL-BREF psychological-domain score) under collaborative care than under usual care.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

This review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. The review question, eligibility criteria, outcome definition, timepoint rule and effect-measure specification were fixed in advance of synthesis.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults (≥18 years) with a diagnosis of severe mental illness — bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, or other psychotic disorders — receiving care in community or outpatient settings. Inpatient-only cohorts were ineligible.

**Intervention.** Collaborative care, defined as a structured, multi-professional, proactively followed-up care model, however locally branded (e.g. care management, integrated care, collaborative chronic care model).

**Comparator.** Usual care, including treatment as usual, enhanced usual care, and waiting-list-style continuation of routine services.

**Outcome.** One mental-health-domain HRQoL score per trial:
- SF-12 Mental Component Summary (MCS), or
- SF-36 Mental Component Summary (MCS), or
- WHOQOL-BREF psychological domain.

Explicitly **ineligible**: physical component summary or physical-domain scores; overall, global or total quality-of-life scores; symptom severity scales; functioning scales; and satisfaction scales.

**Timepoint.** The follow-up closest to 6 months and no later than 6 months. Follow-ups at 12 months or beyond were not used, even where reported.

**Design.** Randomised controlled trials, including cluster-randomised designs.

### 2.3 Data extraction conventions

Three extraction rules were applied uniformly and are material to the interpretation of the results:

1. **Endpoint, not change.** Effect sizes were computed from post-intervention (endpoint) means and SDs per arm, rather than from change-from-baseline scores. This is the more conservative and more widely reportable formulation, but it does not adjust for baseline imbalance.
2. **Denominators.** Where analysed Ns were not reported, randomised group sizes were used.
3. **Dispersion conversion.** Where standard errors or 95% confidence intervals were reported instead of SDs, these were converted to SDs using the relevant group n.

One eligible score was taken per trial, so no trial contributes more than one effect size and there is no within-study dependency in the pooled estimate.

### 2.4 Effect size computation and synthesis

For each trial the standardized mean difference was computed and bias-corrected to Hedges' g. Trial-level effects were pooled in a random-effects model with between-study variance estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The pooling scale was the standardized mean difference (SMD); the effect measure was set by explicit specification of the review question rather than inferred, and no effect-measure conflict was detected. **Native pooling on the specified measure was used (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the pooled value reported below is a confirmed SMD-scale result and not a standardized fallback.** The statistic is therefore correctly named the *pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)*.

### 2.5 Heterogeneity and bias assessment

Heterogeneity was assessed by Cochran's Q with its p-value, the I² statistic, and the REML estimate of τ². Small-study effects and potential publication bias were examined with Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. Moderator analyses were planned contingent on a sufficient number of effect sizes.

## 3. Search and Screening

The review pipeline identified and processed records through title/abstract screening and full-text assessment against the criteria in Section 2.2. **Five studies** were retained as meeting the population, intervention, comparator and design criteria. Of these, **four** supplied data permitting computation of an eligible mental-health-domain HRQoL effect size at the ≤6-month timepoint, and these four constitute the analytic set (k = 4).

The discrepancy between the five retained studies and the four pooled effect sizes is stated plainly: one retained study did not yield an extractable endpoint mean and SD (or convertible SE/CI) for an eligible mental-health-domain instrument within the ≤6-month window, and therefore could not be quantitatively synthesised. Its exclusion from pooling is a data-availability exclusion, not a judgement about its quality or relevance, and it means that roughly one fifth of the otherwise eligible trial base is absent from the numerical result.

The dominant reasons for exclusion at full text were, in order of frequency: outcomes limited to physical-component or total quality-of-life scores; outcomes limited to symptom, functioning or satisfaction scales; absence of any mental-health HRQoL assessment at or before 6 months (with the earliest reported follow-up at 12 months or later); non-randomised design; and populations not meeting the SMI definition (e.g. depression-only or anxiety-only primary-care cohorts, which form the bulk of the broader collaborative-care literature).

## 4. Included Studies

Four randomised comparisons of collaborative care versus usual care contributed to the quantitative synthesis, together enrolling **346 participants** across both arms. All were conducted in community or outpatient settings in adults with SMI, and all reported a single eligible mental-health-domain HRQoL endpoint (SF-12/SF-36 MCS or WHOQOL-BREF psychological domain) at the follow-up closest to and no later than 6 months.

| study_2_R2 | 92 | 2.523 | 1.975 to 3.071 | Favours collaborative care | Yes |

Two features of this evidence base are immediately consequential. First, every trial is small: the largest contributes 116 participants and the smallest 65, and the total of 346 is well below the sample size that a single adequately powered trial of a moderate HRQoL effect would require. Second, the point estimates do not form a continuum — they cluster into a near-null pair (g = 0.129, g = 0.195) and a very large pair (g = 1.413, g = 2.523) with no intermediate values. This bimodality, visible in the effect distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`), is the single most important descriptive fact about this dataset and conditions everything that follows.

## 5. Effect Sizes

All four effects are positive, i.e. all point in the direction of better mental-health-domain HRQoL under collaborative care than under usual care. Their magnitudes, however, span almost the entire plausible range for a psychosocial intervention.

- **study_1_R1** (n = 65): g = 0.129, 95% CI −0.358 to 0.616. A trivial effect by conventional benchmarks, with a confidence interval comfortably including zero and compatible with both a small harm and a moderate benefit. This is the least precise estimate in the set.
- **study_2_R2** (n = 92): g = 2.523, 95% CI 1.975 to 3.071. An effect of this size — more than two and a half pooled standard deviations — is far outside the range typically observed for service-delivery interventions on patient-reported HRQoL, and the lower confidence bound (1.975) still exceeds any conventional "large" threshold. This trial is the principal source of both the magnitude and the instability of the pooled estimate.
- **study_3_R3** (n = 73): g = 1.413, 95% CI 0.900 to 1.927. A very large and statistically significant effect, again well above the range usual for this intervention class.
- **study_4_R4** (n = 116): g = 0.195, 95% CI −0.170 to 0.560. A small, non-significant effect from the largest trial in the set.

Notably, the two trials with the most precise estimates in absolute terms (study_4_R4, n = 116; study_1_R1, n = 65) are the two reporting near-null effects, while the very large effects come from trials of intermediate size. The relationship between precision and effect magnitude is therefore not a clean monotone small-study gradient, which is consistent with the formal bias tests reported in Section 8. ## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was identified; the included studies do not span more than one distinct outcome/timepoint combination, so one pooled result is reported.

**Primary result.** Random-effects (REML) synthesis of k = 4 trials and N = 346 participants yielded a **pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) of 1.055 (95% CI −0.055 to 2.166; p = 0.0625)**. The result **favours Collaborative care**.

Interpretation requires care on three counts.

1. **The direction is favourable but the result is not statistically significant.** The 95% confidence interval includes zero, and p = 0.0625 lies above the conventional 0.05 threshold. Under standard frequentist conventions this is a null result: the data are compatible with no effect of collaborative care on mental-health-domain HRQoL at ≤6 months.
2. **The interval is extraordinarily wide.** It spans from a marginal disadvantage (SMD = −0.055) to an effect of more than two standard deviations (SMD = 2.166). The width reflects both the small number of trials and the enormous between-study variance (τ² = 1.223). In practical terms, this meta-analysis has not localised the effect of collaborative care to any clinically informative range.
3. **The point estimate is not a trustworthy summary of a common effect.** With I² = 95.51% and a bimodal distribution of trial results, the pooled SMD of 1.055 is an arithmetic compromise between two near-null effects and two very large ones — a value that no included trial actually observed. The random-effects mean is the correct estimand for an average effect across a distribution of true effects, but when that distribution is as dispersed as it is here, the mean carries little practical meaning.

An approximate 95% prediction interval, derived from τ² = 1.223 and the standard error of the pooled estimate (≈0.567) on 2 degrees of freedom, extends from roughly −4.3 to +6.4. This interval is so wide as to be uninformative, and should be read as a formal statement that the present data cannot predict the effect in a new setting.

The forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) displays the individual trial estimates, their confidence intervals, their weights and the pooled diamond. The cumulative meta-analysis plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows how the running pooled estimate shifts as studies are added; with only four trials, each addition moves the summary substantially, which is itself a direct visual indicator of the estimate's instability.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was extreme and is the defining characteristic of this synthesis.

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 61.291 |
| Degrees of freedom | 3 |
| p-value for Q | < 0.001 |
| I² | 95.51% |
| τ² (REML) | 1.223 |
| τ (REML) | ≈1.106 |
| k | 4 |

Q = 61.291 on 3 degrees of freedom is roughly twenty times its expected value under homogeneity, and the associated p-value is below 0.001. I² = 95.51% indicates that approximately 95.5% of the observed variability in effect estimates reflects genuine differences in underlying effects rather than sampling error — essentially the ceiling of the I² scale, and far above the ≥75% threshold conventionally labelled "considerable".

The between-study standard deviation of true effects, τ ≈ 1.106, is of the same order as the pooled estimate itself (1.055). Put differently, the dispersion of true effects across settings is as large as the average effect, which is incompatible with the assumption that these four trials are estimating a similar quantity.

Plausible sources of this heterogeneity, none of which can be tested with four studies, include:
- **Instrument non-equivalence.** The protocol permitted both the SF-12/SF-36 MCS and the WHOQOL-BREF psychological domain. These instruments are not interchangeable: the MCS is norm-based with a population mean of 50 and SD of 10, whereas the WHOQOL-BREF psychological domain is transformed differently and has a different distributional spread and ceiling behaviour. Standardisation mitigates but does not eliminate this problem, particularly when within-trial SDs are small.
- **Intervention intensity and fidelity.** "Collaborative care" covers a wide range of staffing ratios, contact frequencies, supervision arrangements and degrees of measurement-based care.
- **Comparator intensity.** "Usual care" ranges from genuinely minimal routine follow-up to well-resourced specialist community mental health teams; the more active the comparator, the smaller the achievable contrast.
- **Diagnostic composition.** Trials weighted towards bipolar disorder and those weighted towards schizophrenia-spectrum disorders may differ substantially in responsiveness of self-rated mental-health HRQoL.
- **Small-sample artefacts.** With arm sizes in the tens, an underestimated or erroneously converted SD — for example, from an SE-to-SD conversion using a randomised rather than analysed n — inflates g dramatically. The two outlying effects (g = 2.523 and g = 1.413) are of a magnitude that should prompt scrutiny of the reported dispersion statistics in the source reports.

## 8. Moderators

**No moderator analysis was performed, and none could be.** With k = 4 effect sizes, a meta-regression can accommodate at most one covariate while retaining 2 residual degrees of freedom, and any such model would be grossly overfitted and uninterpretable. Standard guidance recommends at least ten studies per covariate; this dataset has four studies in total. Subgroup analyses by instrument (MCS vs WHOQOL-BREF), diagnostic mix, intervention intensity, comparator intensity, country or risk-of-bias level were therefore all precluded.

The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) is presented descriptively only. It illustrates the relationship between study size and effect magnitude but supports no inferential claim; the apparent pattern in which the largest trial (n = 116) reports one of the smallest effects is hypothesis-generating at best and is formally untested.

The bimodal split visible in the effect distribution plot strongly suggests that one or more unmeasured study-level factors separates the two near-null trials from the two very large ones. Identifying that factor — whether it is instrument, fidelity, comparator strength, or a data-extraction artefact — should be a priority for any future update of this review, and will require substantially more trials than are currently available.

## 9. Publication Bias

Two complementary assessments were conducted.

**Egger's regression test.** The test for funnel-plot asymmetry gave an intercept of **−4.0245** with **p = 0.1072**. The result is not statistically significant at the 5% level, providing no detectable evidence of small-study effects. The negative sign of the intercept is worth noting: taken at face value it would indicate asymmetry in the direction of *smaller* effects among *less* precise studies — the opposite of the classic publication-bias signature — which is consistent with the observation that the two near-null effects came from the least precise and the most precise trials respectively rather than clustering by precision.

**Trim-and-fill.** The Duval–Tweedie procedure imputed **no** missing studies (**k₀ = 0**). The adjusted estimate is therefore identical to the unadjusted one, **SMD = 1.0553**, indicating that the pooled result is unchanged after adjustment for hypothetical suppressed studies.

**Overall judgement: low risk of publication (small-study) bias.** As summarised in the pipeline interpretation: Egger's regression showed no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.1072), and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the pooled estimate is unchanged after adjustment; these convergent results support an overall judgement of low risk of publication bias, suggesting the meta-analytic estimate is unlikely to be materially inflated by selective reporting.

**Important caveat.** Both tests have very limited statistical power when fewer than ten studies are pooled or when heterogeneity is high — and this synthesis violates both conditions simultaneously (k = 4, I² = 95.51%). Egger's test in particular is known to be both underpowered and prone to false positives in the presence of substantial τ². The non-significant result should therefore be read as an *absence of detectable asymmetry*, not as positive evidence that no publication bias exists. Because this review covers a literature in which small single-centre trials predominate, selective publication of favourable results remains a live possibility that the present data simply cannot exclude. A funnel plot with four points is not meaningfully interpretable by eye and is not relied upon here.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis set out to answer a narrowly specified question: does collaborative care, compared with usual care, improve mental-health-domain HRQoL in adults with SMI managed in community or outpatient settings, at the follow-up closest to and no later than 6 months? Across four randomised trials and 346 participants, the pooled Standardized Mean Difference was 1.055 (95% CI −0.055 to 2.166; p = 0.0625), favouring collaborative care but not reaching statistical significance.

The honest summary of this result is that it is **directionally encouraging and evidentially weak**. Three observations support that reading.

First, **consistency of sign without consistency of size.** All four trials produced positive effects, meaning that in every included comparison the collaborative-care arm reported better mental-health HRQoL at ≤6 months than the usual-care arm. A uniform direction across four independent trials is not nothing. But the magnitudes range from 0.129 to 2.523 — a twenty-fold spread — and two of the four confidence intervals include zero. Sign consistency in a set of four small trials is weak evidence, especially when the intervention is unblinded and the outcome is self-reported.

Second, **the pooled effect is dominated by two implausibly large estimates.** Effects of g = 2.523 and g = 1.413 are far larger than anything observed in the much better-powered collaborative-care literature for depression, where pooled standardized effects on quality-of-life outcomes are typically in the 0.1–0.3 range. Several explanations compete. Genuinely profound benefit in a highly selected population is one. More prosaic explanations — unusually small within-arm SDs in small samples, SE-to-SD conversion using randomised rather than analysed denominators, baseline imbalance that an endpoint-only analysis cannot adjust for, or outcome-assessment unblinding in an intervention that cannot be masked from participants — are at least as plausible and, in our judgement, more likely. Because effect sizes of this magnitude exert disproportionate leverage on a four-study random-effects mean, the pooled value of 1.055 should not be quoted as an estimate of the expected benefit of collaborative care.

Third, **heterogeneity precludes a single summary.** With I² = 95.51% and τ ≈ 1.106, the four trials are not plausibly estimating a common effect, and the approximate prediction interval (roughly −4.3 to +6.4) is uninformative. The appropriate conclusion from a synthesis with this heterogeneity profile is not "collaborative care produces a large benefit" but "the effect of collaborative care on mental-health HRQoL in SMI varies enormously across trials for reasons we cannot identify from four studies."

**Relation to the wider literature.** The broader collaborative-care evidence base, built largely in depression and anxiety populations in primary care, consistently shows small-to-moderate benefits on symptoms and smaller benefits on quality of life. The present SMI-specific synthesis neither confirms nor contradicts that pattern: its point estimate is far larger, but its confidence interval is so wide that it encompasses the small effects seen in the depression literature as well as effects several times larger. The restriction of this review to mental-health-domain HRQoL — excluding physical component scores, total QoL, symptoms, functioning and satisfaction — makes it less directly comparable with reviews that pool across those domains, but also more interpretable, since domain-specific effects are not diluted by physical-health outcomes that operate through an entirely different causal pathway.

**Clinical implications.** Clinicians and commissioners should not treat this result as establishing that collaborative care improves mental-health quality of life in SMI. Equally, they should not treat the non-significant p-value as evidence of no effect: with 346 participants across four trials, this synthesis had little power to detect the small-to-moderate effects that are a priori most plausible. The decision to implement collaborative care for SMI should therefore continue to rest on the wider evidence base — including effects on service use, continuity, physical-health monitoring and symptom outcomes — rather than on mental-health HRQoL evidence, which remains inconclusive at the 6-month horizon.

**Research implications.** What this field needs is not more small trials but (i) adequately powered randomised trials reporting a prespecified mental-health-domain HRQoL endpoint at a short-term (≈6-month) timepoint, (ii) consistent use of a single instrument, preferably the SF-12/SF-36 MCS with full reporting of arm-level means, SDs and analysed Ns, (iii) explicit reporting of intervention fidelity and comparator intensity so that these can be tested as moderators, and (iv) availability of individual participant data, which would permit baseline-adjusted analyses that the endpoint-only extraction used here cannot support.

## 11. Data Quality & Validation Notes

No formal `validation_flags` were returned by the analysis pipeline for this review. The following data-handling issues are nonetheless material and are stated plainly for transparency:

- **One eligible study could not be pooled.** Five studies were retained after screening but only four supplied an extractable mental-health-domain HRQoL effect size at ≤6 months, so the quantitative synthesis rests on 4 of 5 (80%) of the otherwise eligible trial base. The missing trial's results are unknown to the pooled estimate and could move it in either direction.
- **Effect measure.** The analysis measure (SMD) was set by explicit specification of the review question (`effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`), with no detected conflict. Native pooling on this measure was used, so the reported value is a confirmed Standardized Mean Difference and not a standardized fallback.
- **Single meta-analytic group.** All four pooled effects belong to one outcome/timepoint group; no group was flagged as having insufficient data, and no results required separate pooling by effect measure.
- **Denominator and dispersion conventions.** Randomised group sizes were used where analysed Ns were unavailable, and SEs or 95% CIs were converted to SDs using the group n. Both conventions can bias standardized effects in small samples — randomised Ns inflate precision where attrition occurred, and conversion errors propagate directly into g. Given that two trials produced effects above g = 1.4 in samples under 100, these conventions warrant particular caution here.
- **Endpoint rather than change scores.** By protocol, post-intervention means and SDs were used rather than change from baseline. This precludes any adjustment for baseline imbalance, which is a non-trivial risk in trials with arm sizes in the tens.
- **One score per trial.** No trial contributed more than one effect size, so there is no unit-of-analysis error from multiple correlated outcomes and no double-counting of trial reports in the pooled estimate.
- **Timepoint discipline.** Follow-ups at 12 months or longer were not used even where available, so this synthesis speaks only to short-term (≤6-month) effects and says nothing about maintenance or late emergence of benefit.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very few studies (k = 4).** Four effect sizes is below the threshold at which random-effects between-study variance can be estimated with reasonable accuracy; τ² = 1.223 is itself imprecise, and the resulting confidence interval is correspondingly fragile.
2. **Very small total sample (N = 346).** The synthesis is underpowered for the small-to-moderate effects most plausible for this intervention class, so the non-significant p = 0.0625 cannot be read as evidence of absence of effect.
3. **Extreme heterogeneity (I² = 95.51%).** Between-study inconsistency is near the ceiling of the scale, and the bimodal distribution of effects indicates qualitatively different trial results rather than a dispersed continuum. The pooled mean does not describe any observed trial.
4. **Two highly influential outlying estimates.** g = 2.523 and g = 1.413 dominate the pooled result. Formal influence and leave-one-out diagnostics were not reported here, but inspection of the cumulative plot makes clear that removal of either would substantially reduce the summary estimate; a synthesis whose headline figure depends on one or two small trials is not robust.
5. **Instrument heterogeneity.** Pooling SF-12/SF-36 MCS with the WHOQOL-BREF psychological domain assumes these measure a sufficiently similar construct on a standardizable scale. They do not behave identically, and with k = 4 the assumption cannot be tested.
6. **Endpoint-only extraction.** No adjustment for baseline differences was possible.
7. **Unblinded intervention, self-reported outcome.** Collaborative care cannot be masked from participants or providers; mental-health HRQoL is self-reported. Performance and response bias may inflate effects, plausibly more so in smaller, single-centre trials.
8. **Publication-bias tests underpowered.** Egger's test (p = 0.1072) and trim-and-fill (k₀ = 0) both indicate low risk, but both have minimal power at k = 4 with I² > 95%. Absence of detectable asymmetry is not evidence of absence of bias.
9. **No moderator analysis.** Instrument, diagnosis mix, fidelity, comparator intensity and setting could not be examined, so the sources of heterogeneity remain entirely unexplained.
10. **No formal risk-of-bias or certainty rating is reported.** Trial-level risk-of-bias domains and a GRADE assessment were not available in the analysis output. On the features that are observable — few small unblinded trials, self-reported outcomes, extreme unexplained inconsistency and a confidence interval crossing the null — the certainty of this evidence would be expected to rate as very low.
11. **One eligible trial unpooled** (see Section 11), introducing potential selective-availability bias into the analytic set.

---

## 13. Conclusion

In adults with severe mental illness treated in community or outpatient settings, four randomised trials (N = 346) comparing collaborative care with usual care yielded a pooled Standardized Mean Difference of **1.055 (95% CI −0.055 to 2.166; p = 0.0625)** for mental-health-domain health-related quality of life at the follow-up closest to and no later than 6 months. The direction of effect **favours collaborative care**, and all four trials produced positive point estimates. However, the result does not reach statistical significance, the confidence interval includes the null and spans a clinically uninterpretable range, and between-study heterogeneity is at the ceiling of the measurable scale (I² = 95.51%; τ² = 1.223; Q = 61.291, df = 3, p < 0.001), driven by a bimodal split between two near-null and two implausibly large trial effects. Tests for small-study effects were reassuring (Egger's p = 0.1072; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted SMD unchanged at 1.0553) but are severely underpowered at this number of studies.

The evidence is therefore **insufficient to establish, and insufficient to exclude, a benefit of collaborative care on mental-health-domain quality of life in severe mental illness at six months.** The pooled figure of 1.055 should not be cited as an expected effect size. Adequately powered randomised trials, using a single well-specified mental-health HRQoL instrument, reporting arm-level means, SDs and analysed Ns at a prespecified short-term timepoint, and documenting intervention fidelity and comparator intensity, are required before this question can be answered.
