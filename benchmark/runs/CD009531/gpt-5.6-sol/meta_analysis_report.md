# Collaborative Care Versus Usual Care for Mental-Health-Related Quality of Life in Adults With Severe Mental Illness: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Collaborative care may improve health-related quality of life among adults with severe mental illness treated in community or outpatient settings. This review evaluated its effect on the mental-health domain of health-related quality of life at the follow-up closest to, but no later than, 6 months.

**Methods:** Eligible studies compared collaborative care with usual care in adults with bipolar disorder, schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder, or another psychotic disorder. Eligible outcomes were the SF-12 or SF-36 Mental Component Summary or the WHOQOL-BREF psychological domain. One endpoint per trial was to be selected, using 6-month data or the nearest earlier follow-up. Post-intervention continuous outcomes were synthesized as Standardized Mean Differences (SMDs), with positive values favoring collaborative care. A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used.

**Results:** Five studies were represented in the supplied pipeline summary, but only four contributed effect sizes (total N=339). Study-level SMDs ranged from 0.129158 to 2.516215. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was 1.049738713365148 (95% CI −0.055 to 2.154; p=0.06246868365601119), favoring collaborative care, but the confidence interval included no effect. Heterogeneity was considerable (Q=56.735, reported p=0.0; I²=95.39%; τ²=1.207559). Egger’s intercept was −3.8632 (p=0.0219), indicating funnel-plot asymmetry, whereas trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k0=0) and left the pooled estimate at 1.0497. Publication-bias risk was classified as high.

**Conclusions:** The point estimate favored collaborative care, but the pooled result was not statistically significant at the conventional 0.05 threshold and varied markedly across studies. Extreme heterogeneity, evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry, the small number of studies, and incomplete study-level reporting substantially limit confidence in the magnitude and generalizability of the estimated benefit.

- **Outcome:** Mental-health-domain health-related quality of life, measured with the SF-12 or SF-36 Mental Component Summary (MCS) or the WHOQOL-BREF psychological domain.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) combined four studies with 339 participants. The **pooled Standardized Mean Difference** was **1.049738713365148** (95% CI **−0.055 to 2.154**; p=**0.06246868365601119**). According to the prespecified direction and the supplied `favors` classification, the point estimate favored collaborative care. However, the 95% confidence interval crossed zero, and the result did not meet the conventional two-sided threshold of p<0.05.

The estimate represents a standardized difference because eligible studies could use different mental-health quality-of-life instruments. Although the point estimate is large in standardized units, its clinical meaning cannot be established directly without instrument-specific thresholds or minimally important differences. Moreover, the pooled magnitude should not be interpreted as a stable common effect because between-study heterogeneity was extreme.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was considerable:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 56.735
- **Reported Q-test p value:** 0.0
- **I²:** 95.39%
- **τ²:** 1.207559

The Q statistic indicates that variability among study estimates greatly exceeded that expected from sampling error alone. An I² of 95.39% means that nearly all observed variation was attributed to between-study heterogeneity under the model. The τ² estimate likewise indicates substantial dispersion of underlying effects on the standardized scale.

The data show a conspicuous separation between two relatively small estimates (`study_1_R1` and `study_4_R4`) and two much larger estimates (`study_2_R2` and `study_3_R3`). This pattern makes the pooled value sensitive to the assumptions of the random-effects model and limits its interpretation as a typical effect across settings.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−3.8632** with **p=0.0219**, indicating statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry in the supplied analysis. The publication-bias assessment accordingly classified risk as **high**. Such asymmetry may reflect publication bias or small-study effects, but it can also arise from true heterogeneity, outcome or design differences, selective reporting, or chance.

Trim-and-fill estimated **k0=0** missing studies and therefore did not alter the pooled estimate; its adjusted estimate was **1.0497**. The absence of imputed studies does not negate the Egger result or establish the absence of reporting bias. Both methods are particularly unreliable with only four studies, and the extreme heterogeneity further weakens funnel-plot-based inference. Publication-bias findings should thus be regarded as concerning but not definitive.

## Discussion

This synthesis examined mental-health-domain health-related quality of life at no later than 6 months among adults with severe mental illness receiving collaborative care rather than usual care. Across four studies and 339 participants, the pooled Standardized Mean Difference was 1.049738713365148 and favored collaborative care. Nevertheless, the 95% confidence interval ranged from −0.055 to 2.154 and the p value was 0.06246868365601119, so the available data did not exclude no average effect at the conventional 5% significance level.

The central feature of the evidence is not merely imprecision but pronounced inconsistency. Study-level SMDs ranged from 0.129158 to 2.516215, and I² reached 95.39%. The two largest effects were much greater than the remaining estimates. Such dispersion suggests that collaborative care may have different effects across populations, interventions, settings, measurement instruments, or study methods. It may also indicate biases in one or more small studies. Because the necessary study descriptors and risk-of-bias information were unavailable, these explanations could not be tested.

The positive point estimate is compatible with a potentially meaningful benefit, but its magnitude should be interpreted conservatively. Standardization facilitates pooling across eligible instruments but expresses results in study-specific standard-deviation units, which can differ because of variation in populations and measurement variability. Without instrument-specific mean differences or minimally important difference thresholds, the direct clinical significance of the pooled SMD is uncertain.

The evidence concerning dissemination bias adds further uncertainty. Egger’s test detected asymmetry, whereas trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies. These results are not necessarily contradictory because the procedures test different assumptions and both perform poorly when very few studies are available. Extreme heterogeneity can itself produce apparent asymmetry. The prespecified high publication-bias risk is therefore appropriate, but the exact source of asymmetry cannot be determined.

Overall, the evidence suggests a possible short-term advantage of collaborative care for mental-health-related quality of life, but it does not provide a precise or consistent estimate. Future trials should prespecify validated quality-of-life outcomes, report complete group-level endpoint data at standardized time points, describe intervention content and fidelity, and ensure prospective registration and complete publication of results. An updated meta-analysis should examine instrument, diagnosis, intervention intensity, and risk of bias as moderators once a larger evidence base is available.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations.

First, only four studies were pooled. Random-effects estimates, τ², I², Egger’s regression, and trim-and-fill are all unstable with such a small evidence base. The confidence interval around the pooled estimate was correspondingly broad.

Second, heterogeneity was extreme (I²=95.39%; τ²=1.207559). A single pooled estimate inadequately represents effects that appear to differ substantially across studies, and there were insufficient data to explain this variability through subgroup analysis or meta-regression.

Third, the supplied dataset lacked full study characteristics and raw outcome summaries. Instrument selection, endpoint timing, sample-size definitions, variance conversions, and extraction accuracy could not be checked. The reason one of five studies did not contribute to the quantitative synthesis was also unavailable.

Fourth, no trial-level risk-of-bias assessment was provided. The certainty of evidence could therefore not be formally rated, and the possible influence of randomization methods, deviations from intended interventions, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, or selective reporting remains unknown.

Fifth, publication-bias analyses suggested funnel-plot asymmetry, but they have very low reliability at k=4 and are confounded by the observed heterogeneity. The high-risk classification warrants caution but cannot identify the mechanism responsible.

Finally, the review concerns only short-term outcomes assessed at or before 6 months. It does not establish whether any benefit persists over longer follow-up or generalizes beyond the populations, services, and collaborative-care models represented in the included trials.

## Conclusion

Among adults with severe mental illness treated in community or outpatient settings, collaborative care had a pooled Standardized Mean Difference of **1.049738713365148** in mental-health-related quality of life compared with usual care at the follow-up closest to, but no later than, 6 months (95% CI **−0.055 to 2.154**; p=**0.06246868365601119**; four studies; N=339). The point estimate favored collaborative care, but the confidence interval included no effect. Very high heterogeneity (I²=95.39%) and a high assessed risk of publication bias prevent a confident conclusion about the magnitude or consistency of benefit. The findings support the possibility of improved short-term mental-health-related quality of life, but they remain inconclusive and require confirmation in larger, transparently reported trials.
