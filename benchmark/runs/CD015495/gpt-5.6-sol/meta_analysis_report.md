# Immune Checkpoint Inhibitors Added to Platinum-Based Chemotherapy in Adults Aged 65 Years or Older With Cancer: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis of Overall Survival

## Abstract

**Background:** The effect of adding an immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICI) to platinum-based chemotherapy on overall survival among adults aged ≥65 years with cancer is clinically important but may differ from that in broader trial populations.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of ICI plus platinum-based chemotherapy, compared with the same or otherwise comparable platinum-based chemotherapy alone (with or without placebo), on overall survival in trials reporting an unrestricted age ≥65 subgroup.

**Methods:** Eligible effects were trial-specific overall-survival hazard ratios (HRs) for the unrestricted age ≥65 subgroup. Narrower, upper-bounded age categories (for example, 65–74 years) were not acceptable substitutes. The supplied pipeline contained 8 studies, of which 4 contributed effect estimates (total subgroup sample, n = 1,041). Hazard ratios were pooled on their native scale using a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were examined using Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill, while recognizing the limited reliability of these procedures with only four estimates.

**Results:** The pooled Hazard Ratio (HR) was 0.81979630533738 (95% CI 0.699–0.962; p = 0.014793108096996885; k = 4; n = 1,041). The supplied directional field classified the result as **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)”**; this classification is retained rather than independently reassigning favorability. There was no detected heterogeneity (Q = 0.348, df = 3, p = 0.9508; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). Egger’s intercept was −0.2634 (p = 0.6793), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0); the supplied assessment rated publication-bias risk as low.

**Conclusions:** The native-scale REML analysis produced a statistically significant pooled HR below 1, with no detected between-study heterogeneity. However, the supplied result classified favorability as neither/indeterminate, and only 4 of 8 pipeline studies contributed usable estimates. Sparse-study publication-bias diagnostics, absent trial-level bibliographic and risk-of-bias information, and incomplete screening details materially limit certainty.

## Introduction

Older adults account for a substantial proportion of people with cancer, yet they are often underrepresented in randomized trials and may differ from younger participants in comorbidity, functional reserve, treatment tolerance, competing mortality, and immunologic status. Immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) combined with platinum-based chemotherapy have become important treatment options in several malignancies. Whether the survival effects observed in overall trial populations extend to adults aged 65 years and older therefore requires direct evaluation using trial-reported older-adult subgroup estimates.

This review addressed the following question: among people with cancer aged ≥65 years, what is the effect of adding an ICI to platinum-based chemotherapy, compared with the same or otherwise comparable platinum-based chemotherapy alone—with or without placebo—on overall survival at any reported follow-up time?

The target effect estimate was each trial’s own overall-survival hazard ratio (HR) for the **unrestricted age ≥65 subgroup**. An age category with an upper limit, such as 65–74 years, was not considered an acceptable substitute. The intended extraction hierarchy was the prespecified or principal survival model and the most mature available overall-survival analysis, while avoiding duplicate inclusion of the same participants.

\[

## Discussion

### Principal findings

In four studies contributing 1,041 participants, the native-scale random-effects analysis yielded a pooled **Hazard Ratio (HR) of 0.81979630533738 (95% CI 0.699–0.962; p = 0.014793108096996885)**. Statistical heterogeneity was not detected (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0; Q = 0.348, p = 0.9508). The supplied analytic classification described favorability as neither/indeterminate, and this report preserves that designation rather than inferring direction solely from the numerical value.

The use of the unrestricted age ≥65 subgroup is an important methodological strength of the research question. It avoids substituting a narrower 65–74-year category, which would exclude the oldest participants and could produce a different estimate. Use of trial-specific time-to-event analyses also preserves censoring information better than converting survival outcomes to binary mortality at a fixed time.

### Clinical and methodological interpretation

The pooled confidence interval was relatively narrow and excluded 1, while estimated between-study variance was zero. Nevertheless, the evidence base remains small. An age threshold of 65 years encompasses a heterogeneous population, and a trial subgroup meeting that threshold does not necessarily represent frail adults, those with multimorbidity, or adults aged 80 years and older. Without study-level characteristics, it is not possible to assess generalizability to these clinically important groups.

Subgroup estimates may also be less precise than overall trial estimates and may be vulnerable to selective reporting. Although age is generally measured before randomization, randomization is not necessarily preserved within every subgroup in a way that guarantees balance, particularly when subgroup sample sizes are modest. The report therefore supports interpretation of the pooled subgroup association in conjunction with full-trial evidence, biological plausibility, toxicity, quality of life, and geriatric assessment—not in isolation.

The absence of detected heterogeneity does not resolve whether effects are consistent across malignancies or regimens. Pooling may be clinically reasonable only if the treatment contrast and proportional-hazards estimand are sufficiently comparable. The supplied data do not allow that assumption to be examined.

### Certainty of the evidence

A formal GRADE assessment could not be completed because study-level risk of bias, directness, precision of individual estimates, consistency across clinical settings, and reporting-bias information were incomplete. The statistically significant pooled result and lack of detected heterogeneity are favorable statistical features, but confidence is reduced by the small number of contributing studies, absent study-level HRs, unknown reasons for noncontribution by four pipeline studies, missing trial characteristics, and unavailable risk-of-bias assessments.

## Limitations

This meta-analysis has several important limitations:

1. **Incomplete search documentation:** Databases, dates, search strings, language restrictions, grey-literature methods, reviewer procedures, and protocol registration were unavailable.
2. **Incomplete screening accounting:** Four of eight pipeline studies did not enter the quantitative synthesis, but specific exclusion or non-extraction reasons were not reported.
3. **Small evidence base:** Only four estimates were pooled, limiting precision in heterogeneity, moderator, and publication-bias assessments.
4. **Missing trial-level native effects:** Individual HRs and their uncertainty estimates were unavailable, precluding independent calculation of log(HR), standard errors, weights, and influence diagnostics.
5. **Scale discordance in supplied summaries:** Auxiliary study-level standardized effects were provided alongside a native HR meta-analysis. Although the primary analysis was identified as native pooling, the relationship between these entries cannot be audited from the available data.
6. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** Internal validity and selective reporting could not be systematically evaluated.
7. **Unknown clinical diversity:** Cancer types, ICI agents, chemotherapy backbones, stages, and follow-up durations were not reported.
8. **Subgroup evidence:** Age-specific trial estimates can be underpowered and selectively reported, and participants aged ≥65 years in trials may not represent the broader older population encountered in clinical practice.
9. **Proportional-hazards assumptions:** No information was supplied regarding nonproportional hazards or the validity of HR-based summaries in individual trials.
10. **No toxicity or patient-centered outcomes:** The analysis addresses overall survival only and does not establish the net clinical benefit after considering adverse events, functional outcomes, symptoms, or quality of life.
11. **Direction-classification uncertainty:** The supplied favorability field was indeterminate, limiting an unequivocal treatment-direction statement despite the numerical HR.

## Conclusion

Among adults with cancer aged ≥65 years, four studies totaling 1,041 participants contributed to a native-scale REML meta-analysis of overall survival. The pooled **Hazard Ratio (HR) was 0.81979630533738 (95% CI 0.699–0.962; p = 0.014793108096996885)**, with no detected statistical heterogeneity (**I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0; Q = 0.348, p = 0.9508**). The supplied directional field classified this finding as neither/indeterminate, and that classification was retained.

Small-study-effect diagnostics did not identify asymmetry (Egger’s p = 0.6793; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0), but their reliability is poor with only four studies. The result should be interpreted cautiously because half of the pipeline studies did not contribute to pooling, trial-level HRs and study characteristics were unavailable, and neither risk of bias nor clinical heterogeneity could be adequately assessed. More transparent reporting of unrestricted age ≥65 subgroup outcomes—and evidence specifically representing the oldest and frailest adults—is needed.
