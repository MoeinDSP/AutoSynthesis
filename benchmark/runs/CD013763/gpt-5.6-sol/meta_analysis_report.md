# Ultrasound-Guided Erector Spinae Plane Block and 24-Hour Postoperative Opioid Consumption in Adults Undergoing Surgery: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

## Background
Ultrasound-guided erector spinae plane block (ESPB) is intended to reduce postoperative opioid requirements, but its effect relative to general anaesthesia and usual analgesia without any block or injection requires quantitative assessment.

## Objective
To evaluate the effect of ESPB plus general anaesthesia and usual analgesia, compared with general anaesthesia and usual analgesia alone, on cumulative postoperative opioid consumption from the end of surgery through 24 hours in adults undergoing surgery.

## Methods
The supplied evidence set contained 19 studies, of which 16 contributed an effect size and 900 participants to the meta-analysis. The prespecified outcome was cumulative 24-hour postoperative opioid consumption expressed in milligrams of oral morphine equivalents (OME). A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used. The requested raw effect was the mean difference (MD), calculated as ESPB minus control; negative values represent lower opioid consumption with ESPB. Statistical heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were assessed using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

## Results
Across 16 studies, the pooled mean difference was −52.70852283582824 mg OME (95% CI −115.190 to 9.773; p=0.09825103998046936). The supplied direction field identifies the point estimate as favoring ESPB, but the confidence interval included no difference and the result was not statistically significant at the 0.05 level. Heterogeneity was extreme (Q=779.031, p reported as 0.0; I²=99.99%; τ²=16157.698089). Egger’s intercept was 2.0666 with p reported as 0.0, indicating marked funnel-plot asymmetry and a high risk of small-study effects or publication bias. Trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀=0) and yielded a standardized estimate of −2.0458, creating inconsistency between the two publication-bias diagnostics.

## Conclusions
The pooled point estimate favored ESPB and was compatible with a potentially substantial reduction in 24-hour opioid consumption, but it was also compatible with little or no benefit because the confidence interval crossed zero. Extreme heterogeneity, funnel-plot asymmetry, incomplete reporting of extraction and conversion details, and an apparent mismatch between the supplied study-level standardized effects and the native-scale pooled MD substantially limit confidence in the numerical estimate. The evidence does not establish a precise or consistently transferable opioid-sparing effect.

# Introduction

Postoperative opioid exposure is associated with nausea, vomiting, sedation, respiratory depression, ileus, delayed mobilization, and the possibility of persistent opioid use. Regional anaesthetic techniques may reduce these harms by decreasing perioperative opioid requirements. The ultrasound-guided erector spinae plane block (ESPB) is a fascial-plane block in which local anaesthetic is deposited adjacent to the erector spinae muscle and transverse process. It has been studied across diverse surgical procedures, but variation in operative setting, block technique, local-anaesthetic regimen, and background multimodal analgesia may produce differing treatment effects.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among adults undergoing surgery, what is the effect of ultrasound-guided ESPB plus general anaesthesia and usual analgesia, compared with general anaesthesia and usual analgesia alone without sham, saline, or any other injection, on cumulative postoperative opioid consumption during the first 24 hours after surgery?

The prespecified outcome was cumulative opioid consumption from the end of surgery through 24 hours, incorporating recovery-room and ward use from patient-controlled analgesia and rescue doses. The intended common unit was milligrams of oral morphine equivalents (OME). Intraoperative opioid administration and postoperative totals reported only at other time points were outside the outcome definition. Pain scores, time to first analgesic request, postoperative nausea or vomiting, block-related complications, chronic pain, and length of stay were not outcomes of this synthesis.

# Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood included **16 studies and 900 participants**. The **pooled Mean Difference (MD)** was:

**−52.70852283582824 mg OME (95% CI −115.190 to 9.773; p=0.09825103998046936).**

The supplied `favors` field identifies the point estimate as favoring **ultrasound-guided ESPB plus general anaesthesia and usual analgesia**. Because the 95% confidence interval crossed 0 mg OME and the p value exceeded 0.05, the pooled analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference. The confidence interval was broad: it included a potentially large opioid reduction with ESPB as well as a small increase in opioid consumption.

# Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was extreme:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 779.031
- **Q-test p value:** reported as 0.0
- **I²:** 99.99%
- **τ²:** 16157.698089

The Q test strongly rejects a common-effect assumption, while I² indicates that virtually all observed variation beyond sampling error was attributed to between-study heterogeneity. The very large τ² on the MD scale further indicates wide dispersion in underlying effects. Under these conditions, the pooled MD should be regarded as an average across highly inconsistent results rather than as a treatment effect expected to apply uniformly across settings.

Potential clinical sources include type and extent of surgery, block location and laterality, local-anaesthetic drug and dose, timing of block placement, background multimodal analgesia, opioid rescue protocols, and baseline opioid risk. Potential methodological sources include study quality, differential blinding, measurement and conversion of opioid consumption, and estimation of means or SDs from nonparametric summaries. None could be examined using the supplied information.

# Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **2.0666**, with a p value reported as **0.0** (interpretable as below the software’s displayed precision and described in the supplied interpretation as p<0.0001). This result indicates substantial funnel-plot asymmetry and was classified as a **high** risk of small-study effects or publication bias.

Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀=0** missing studies and returned a supplied standardized trim-and-fill estimate of **−2.0458**. Because this is a standardized estimate, it is not directly comparable with the pooled MD in mg OME. The absence of imputed studies conflicts with the strongly significant Egger test. This discrepancy does not eliminate concern: Egger’s test can detect asymmetry caused by publication bias, selective reporting, genuine effect modification associated with study size, or methodological differences, and trim-and-fill can perform poorly when heterogeneity is extreme.

Accordingly, the publication-bias findings should be interpreted as inconsistent but concerning. The highly significant Egger test, extreme heterogeneity, and apparently larger effects in some small studies reduce confidence in the pooled result.

# Discussion

## Principal findings

In 16 studies involving 900 participants, the random-effects pooled Mean Difference was −52.70852283582824 mg OME, with a 95% confidence interval from −115.190 to 9.773 mg OME. The supplied direction field indicates that the point estimate favors ESPB. Nevertheless, the interval crossed the null and the p value was 0.09825103998046936, so the evidence did not establish a statistically significant reduction in 24-hour opioid consumption.

The principal interpretive issue is not merely imprecision. Heterogeneity was exceptionally high (I²=99.99%), indicating that a single average effect may inadequately represent the included studies. The pooled result could reflect very different effects in different operations, block protocols, analgesic regimens, or study designs. A clinically useful interpretation would require examination of a prediction interval and credible subgroup effects, neither of which was provided.

## Clinical interpretation

The lower confidence limit is compatible with a large reduction in opioid exposure, whereas the upper limit allows a modest increase. Thus, the analysis does not provide a precise estimate of the magnitude of benefit. Whether a reduction of approximately 52.7 mg OME would be clinically important also depends on the operative context, baseline opioid use, conversion assumptions, and the duration over which consumption was measured.

The comparator definition is clinically important. This review was intended to isolate the effect of ESPB against **no block and no injection**, rather than against sham, saline, or another regional technique. Such comparisons may be more susceptible to performance and detection bias if clinicians, participants, or outcome assessors were unblinded. Study-level blinding and risk-of-bias information were not supplied, so this concern could not be evaluated.

## Consistency between study-level and pooled metrics

The study-level effects provided for this report were Hedges’ g values, whereas the pooled statistic was labeled and computed as an MD. A valid OME MD ordinarily requires each study to contribute arm-level means and SDs in the same OME unit or an equivalent study-level MD and variance. Those source values were not included in the context. In addition, the metadata warned that the rows did not share a confirmed common outcome-unit value. This creates an unresolved traceability problem: the pooled MD is reported exactly as supplied, but the displayed standardized effects cannot independently reproduce it.

This issue is particularly important because raw MDs preserve clinical units, while standardized effects divide differences by within-study variability. Their magnitudes and interpretations are not interchangeable. The trim-and-fill standardized estimate of −2.0458 likewise cannot be used as an “adjusted” version of the −52.70852283582824 mg OME pooled MD.

## Certainty of evidence

A formal GRADE assessment was not supplied and cannot be completed reliably without study-level risk-of-bias judgments, design information, directness assessments, and complete outcome data. Qualitatively, confidence is reduced by extreme inconsistency, imprecision around the null, evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry, and uncertainty about effect-measure traceability and OME standardization.

# Limitations

1. **Incomplete search documentation:** databases, dates, search strategies, screening counts, and detailed exclusion reasons were unavailable, preventing full assessment of search comprehensiveness and PRISMA flow.
2. **Missing study characteristics:** surgical settings, block techniques, drug regimens, participant characteristics, and comparator analgesic protocols were not supplied.
3. **No study-level risk-of-bias assessment:** the internal validity of the included trials could not be evaluated or incorporated into sensitivity analyses.
4. **Unverifiable OME conversion:** original opioid drugs, doses, units, routes, conversion factors, and arm-level means and SDs were absent. It was impossible to confirm exclusion of intraoperative opioids or restriction to the exact end-of-surgery-to-24-hour interval.
5. **Effect-measure inconsistency:** supplied study-level effects were Hedges’ g, while the pooled result was a Mean Difference in mg OME. The source data needed to reconcile these scales were unavailable.
6. **Extreme heterogeneity:** I²=99.99% severely limits the generalizability and interpretability of the average effect.
7. **No prediction interval:** the likely range of effects in a new clinical setting was not provided.
8. **No moderator analyses:** possible explanations for heterogeneity could not be tested.
9. **Potential small-study effects:** Egger’s test was highly significant, although trim-and-fill imputed no studies. Both methods have limitations under severe heterogeneity.
10. **Three studies did not contribute effect sizes:** the reason for their omission was not supplied, creating possible concern about outcome availability or selective inclusion.
11. **No formal certainty assessment:** a defensible GRADE rating could not be assigned from the available information.

# Conclusion

The random-effects analysis of 16 studies and 900 participants produced a pooled Mean Difference of **−52.70852283582824 mg OME** (95% CI **−115.190 to 9.773**; p=**0.09825103998046936**) for cumulative 24-hour postoperative opioid consumption. The supplied direction field indicates that the point estimate favors ultrasound-guided ESPB, but the confidence interval includes no difference and the pooled effect is not statistically significant.

The result is undermined by extreme heterogeneity (I²=99.99%), marked funnel-plot asymmetry, and unresolved inconsistency between the study-level standardized effects and the native-scale pooled MD. On the evidence supplied, ESPB may reduce 24-hour opioid consumption in some surgical settings, but the magnitude and consistency of benefit remain uncertain. Verification of arm-level OME data and conversion factors, transparent risk-of-bias assessment, and clinically informed subgroup analyses are required before drawing firm conclusions.
