# Ultrasound-Guided Erector Spinae Plane Block versus No Block for Cumulative 24-Hour Postoperative Opioid Consumption in Adults Undergoing Surgery: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** The erector spinae plane block (ESPB) is a fascial-plane regional technique that has diffused rapidly into perioperative practice. Its opioid-sparing magnitude relative to no block at all — rather than relative to sham injection or to an alternative block — remains incompletely quantified on an interpretable clinical scale.

**Objective.** To estimate the effect of an ultrasound-guided ESPB with local anaesthetic, added to usual perioperative care, compared with no block (general anaesthesia plus usual/standard analgesia, no sham or saline injection), on cumulative postoperative opioid consumption during the first 24 hours after the end of surgery, expressed in oral morphine equivalents (mg).

**Methods.** A systematic search and screening pipeline identified primary comparative studies of ESPB versus a no-block control reporting cumulative 24-hour postoperative opioid consumption as mean, SD and n per arm. Opioid doses were converted to oral morphine equivalents (OME, mg) using standard published equianalgesic ratios, with the original drug, dose, unit and conversion factor recorded per study; multiple opioids within a study were summed after conversion; standard errors were converted to SDs (SD = SE × √n) and medians with IQRs were converted to means and SDs by standard methods where required. Two-hour and 48-hour totals and intraoperative opioid doses were excluded by protocol. Random-effects pooling used restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Nineteen primary study records entered the extraction stage and 17 contributed an analysable effect size (n = 1,006 participants). All 17 study-level effects favoured ESPB and all 17 confidence intervals excluded the null. The random-effects pooled Mean Difference (MD) was **−38.59 mg OME** (95% CI −61.46 to −15.72; p = 0.00094), a result that **favours ultrasound-guided ESPB** — i.e. ESPB recipients consumed approximately 39 mg fewer oral morphine equivalents over the first 24 postoperative hours than no-block controls. Heterogeneity was extreme: Q = 879.932 (p < 0.001), I² = 99.9%, τ² = 2259.89 (τ ≈ 47.5 mg OME), so that the approximate 95% prediction interval spans from a large benefit to a possible small harm. Egger's test indicated strong funnel asymmetry (intercept 2.0046, p < 0.001) while trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0); overall small-study-effect risk was graded **high**.

**Conclusions.** ESPB is associated with a statistically significant and clinically substantial reduction in 24-hour opioid consumption versus no block, consistent in direction across every contributing study. However, the magnitude of that reduction is highly uncertain: near-total heterogeneity, an open-label comparison against a no-injection control, and strong evidence of small-study effects mean the pooled −38.59 mg OME should be read as an upper-bound-leaning estimate of direction-certain but magnitude-uncertain benefit. Certainty in the effect size is low.


## 1. Introduction

Perioperative opioid exposure remains a principal driver of postoperative nausea and vomiting, sedation, ileus, respiratory depression, delayed mobilisation and — in a minority of opioid-naïve patients — persistent postoperative opioid use. Multimodal and regional analgesic strategies are therefore central to enhanced recovery pathways, and the choice among them is increasingly made on the basis of quantified opioid-sparing effect rather than on mechanism alone.

The erector spinae plane block (ESPB), first described in 2016, deposits local anaesthetic in the interfascial plane deep to the erector spinae muscle group and superficial to the transverse process. It is technically straightforward under ultrasound guidance, uses a sonographic target that is bony and therefore reliable, and sits at a distance from the pleura and neuraxis that makes it attractive in anticoagulated or anatomically difficult patients. These properties have driven unusually fast adoption across thoracic, breast, abdominal, spinal and cardiac surgery.

What has lagged behind adoption is a stable estimate of effect size on a clinically interpretable scale. Much of the published synthesis literature pools standardized mean differences, which are convenient across heterogeneous opioid regimens but are difficult to translate into a dose a clinician can recognise. Furthermore, the comparator matters: ESPB versus an alternative block, ESPB versus sham injection, and ESPB versus no block at all are three different clinical questions with three different expected effect sizes, and conflating them inflates heterogeneity while obscuring the decision that an anaesthetist actually faces.

This review addresses one of those questions in isolation. **In adults undergoing surgery, what is the effect of an ultrasound-guided ESPB with local anaesthetic, added to usual perioperative care, compared with no block (general anaesthesia plus usual/standard analgesia, with no sham or saline injection), on cumulative postoperative opioid consumption during the first 24 hours after the end of surgery, expressed in oral morphine equivalents (mg)?** The no-block comparator was specified deliberately: it is the pragmatic alternative in institutions where ESPB is not yet part of the pathway, and it is the comparator that yields the largest, most policy-relevant contrast — while also, as we discuss, carrying the greatest susceptibility to performance and detection bias.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

This report follows PRISMA 2020 reporting conventions for systematic reviews incorporating quantitative synthesis. The review question, eligibility criteria, outcome definition, opioid-conversion rules and effect-direction convention were fixed before synthesis, and are reproduced verbatim in §2.2–§2.4 below.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

| Domain | Inclusion | Exclusion |
|---|---|---|
| **Population** | Adults (≥18 y) undergoing surgery of any type under general anaesthesia | Paediatric cohorts; non-surgical pain; chronic pain populations |
| **Intervention** | Ultrasound-guided ESPB with local anaesthetic (single-shot or catheter), plus usual perioperative care | Landmark/blind ESPB; ESPB without local anaesthetic; ESPB as part of an indistinguishable multi-block bundle |
| **Comparator** | No block — general anaesthesia plus usual/standard analgesia only, with **no** sham or saline injection | Sham/placebo injection controls; active comparator blocks (PVB, TPVB, serratus anterior, QL, TAP, epidural); ESPB-vs-ESPB dose/volume comparisons |
| **Outcome** | Cumulative postoperative opioid consumption from end of surgery to 24 h (recovery room plus ward, including PCA and rescue doses), reported as mean, SD and n per arm, or convertible to that form | 2-hour or 48-hour totals; intraoperative opioid dose; time-to-first-rescue only; "number of patients requiring rescue" only |
| **Design** | Primary comparative studies (randomised and controlled designs) reporting newly collected data | Systematic reviews, meta-analyses, narrative reviews, editorials, case reports, protocols; duplicate or split reports of the same cohort |

### 2.3 Information sources and screening flow

Records were retrieved, de-duplicated, and passed through a staged document-type gate, a population/intervention/comparator relevance gate, and an outcome-availability gate. Two records were removed at the document-type gate and are reported transparently in §9 (Data Quality & Validation Notes): one was a systematic review and meta-analysis of 19 randomised trials that contained no newly collected primary data, and one was a near-duplicate/split fragment of an already-included report, detected by full-text content similarity and removed to prevent double-counting of the same participants.

Following screening, **19 primary study records** satisfied all eligibility criteria and entered data extraction. Of these, **17 yielded an analysable 24-hour opioid-consumption effect size** and were carried into quantitative synthesis; **2 studies (identifiers study_12 and study_16) were eligible on design and comparator but did not contribute a computable effect size** for this outcome at this timepoint — in practice because the required mean/SD/n triplet for the 24-hour window could not be reconstructed from the published report within the pre-specified conversion rules (no SE, no median/IQR, or reporting confined to an excluded timepoint). These two studies are counted as included in the review but are necessarily absent from the pooled estimate, and their omission is a potential source of selective-availability bias.

### 2.4 Outcome harmonisation and conversion to oral morphine equivalents

All reported opioid doses were converted to oral morphine equivalents (OME, mg) using standard published equianalgesic ratios, with the original drug name, administered dose, unit and the conversion factor applied recorded for every study. Where a study reported more than one opioid (e.g. intraoperative-ward tramadol plus PCA morphine rescue), the converted values were summed. Standard errors and SEMs were converted to standard deviations as SD = SE × √n. Medians with interquartile ranges were converted to means and standard deviations using a standard published method. Per protocol, 2-hour totals, 48-hour totals and intraoperative opioid doses were never substituted for the 24-hour postoperative total.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure, and an important measurement caveat

The user-specified metric for this review is the **raw mean difference in oral morphine equivalents (mg)**, and the pooled result is reported on that scale. The analysis record carries `measure_label = "Mean Difference (MD)"`, `effect_measure = "MD"`, `computation_scale = "MD"`, and `native_pooling_used = true` — so the headline statistic is correctly named a **pooled Mean Difference (MD)** and is not a Hedges' g.

Two transparency points must accompany that label, and neither should be glossed over.

1. **A recorded effect-measure conflict.** The pipeline flagged that although the source papers explicitly report mean differences, *the contributing rows do not share a common `outcome_units` value*. Mean difference is a scale-dependent metric (unlike risk ratios, odds ratios or hazard ratios, which are unitless). When contributing rows do not carry a verified common unit string, an MD pool is only valid to the extent that the upstream OME conversion genuinely placed every study on the same milligram scale. The conversion protocol was designed to do exactly that, and we believe it did; but the unit field itself was not uniform, the pipeline explicitly recorded that it therefore did not trust the MD signal from unit metadata alone, and readers should treat "mg OME" as a reconstructed rather than a natively verified unit.

2. **A scale mismatch between the study-level and pooled columns.** The per-study effect sizes supplied for this synthesis (Table 1, and the forest plot) are expressed as **Hedges' g**, whereas the pooled estimate is on the **MD (mg OME)** scale. These two columns are therefore not on a common axis, and the pooled −38.59 cannot be checked by eye against the g values in Table 1. For the same reason, the small-study-effect diagnostics in §7 — Egger's intercept and the trim-and-fill adjusted estimate of −2.1483 — are evidently computed on the standardized scale, not the milligram scale, and must be interpreted as such. We report both faithfully rather than silently harmonising them.

### 4.2 Direction convention

Opioid consumption is an outcome where a **lower** raw value is the better one. In the raw mean-difference metric, a **negative MD** (fewer milligrams of oral morphine equivalent in the ESPB arm) is therefore what favours the block. Accordingly, the `favors` field for this analysis records **"Ultrasound-guided erector spinae plane block (ESPB) with local anaesthetic, plus usual perioperative care"**, and all directional statements in this report are taken from that field rather than inferred from the sign.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A single outcome/timepoint group was analysed (cumulative 24-hour postoperative opioid consumption); no `meta_analysis_groups` with multiple entries were present, so there is one pooled estimate rather than several, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data.

The pooled Mean Difference is **−38.59 mg OME (95% CI −61.46 to −15.72; p = 0.00094)**, which **favours ultrasound-guided ESPB**. In plain terms: averaged across 17 studies and 1,006 patients, adding an ultrasound-guided ESPB to usual care was associated with consuming roughly **39 mg fewer oral morphine equivalents** in the first 24 hours after surgery than receiving no block — with the data compatible with anything from a modest ~16 mg saving to a large ~61 mg saving.

For clinical calibration, 38.6 mg OME is on the order of 13 mg of intravenous morphine, or roughly a quarter to a third of the total 24-hour PCA morphine demand reported in many of the no-block arms in this literature. If real at that magnitude, it is not a marginal effect; it is the difference between a patient who needs sustained PCA titration and one managed largely on non-opioid multimodal analgesia with occasional rescue.

The statistical significance of this result is, however, far less informative than it looks, because the confidence interval around it is derived under a heterogeneity burden (τ² = 2259.89) so large that the between-study standard deviation (τ ≈ 47.5 mg OME) **exceeds the pooled effect itself**. The approximate 95% prediction interval — the range in which the true effect of a new, exchangeable ESPB trial would be expected to fall — is roughly **−143 to +66 mg OME**. That interval includes zero and includes a direction of harm. The honest reading is therefore: *the average effect across this body of evidence is a significant benefit; the effect in any one future setting is not reliably predictable from these data.*

## 6. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity in this synthesis is at the practical ceiling of the measure.

- **Cochran's Q = 879.932** on 16 degrees of freedom, **p < 0.001**. Under homogeneity, Q would be expected to be about 16; the observed value is roughly 55 times that.
- **I² = 99.9%**, i.e. essentially all of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to real between-study differences rather than to within-study sampling error.
- **τ² = 2259.89 mg² OME**, giving τ ≈ **47.5 mg OME**. The between-study SD of true effects is about 1.23 times the magnitude of the pooled effect.

Heterogeneity of this order is common in MD syntheses of opioid consumption and has several identifiable structural sources, all of which plausibly operate here:

1. **Baseline opioid requirement varies by surgery.** ESPB is studied in breast, thoracic, abdominal, spinal, cardiac, bariatric and nephrectomy surgery, and the no-block control-arm 24-hour consumption in these settings can differ by a factor of five or more. An MD scale is additive, so a block that produces a constant *proportional* reduction will produce wildly different *absolute* reductions across these populations. This alone can generate near-total I² without any true inconsistency in biological effect.
2. **Background multimodal analgesia differs.** "Usual analgesia" ranges from scheduled paracetamol plus NSAID plus dexamethasone to opioid-only PCA. A rich non-opioid background compresses the opioid-sparing headroom available to the block.
3. **Opioid conversion amplifies dispersion.** Converting tramadol, pethidine, nalbuphine, fentanyl and morphine to a common OME scale relies on equianalgesic ratios whose published values vary substantially; conversion error is multiplicative and inflates between-study variance on the milligram scale.
4. **Block technique and dose vary.** Volume (typically 20–40 mL per side), concentration, agent, uni- versus bilateral placement, level of injection and single-shot versus catheter all differ, and all plausibly modify effect magnitude.
5. **Rescue protocols differ.** The measured outcome is partly a property of the protocol: a trial with a liberal PCA bolus setting and a low NRS rescue threshold will record higher consumption in both arms and a larger absolute gap than one with conservative settings.

The heterogeneity decomposition is visualised at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

**Consequence for interpretation.** With I² = 99.9%, the pooled mean difference should be understood as a *summary of a distribution*, not an estimate of a common effect. The one thing the distribution does tell us unambiguously is its sign: every one of the 17 studies lies on the benefit side, and 17/17 confidence intervals exclude the null. The direction of effect is robust; the magnitude is not transportable.

## 7. Moderators

No formal moderator model (meta-regression or pre-specified subgroup analysis) was fitted in this synthesis, and no study-level covariates — surgical type, block volume or concentration, uni- versus bilateral block, background multimodal regimen, index opioid, blinding status or risk-of-bias domain — were available as structured fields for the contributing rows. **We therefore cannot report moderator coefficients, subgroup estimates, or the proportion of τ² explained.** Given I² = 99.9%, this is a material gap: with heterogeneity this extreme, moderator analysis is not an optional refinement but the main route to a usable estimate, and its absence is one reason the certainty rating in §10 is low.

What can be said descriptively is limited to the precision–magnitude relationship visible in the data, rendered in the bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`. Reading Table 1 against study size, the largest standardized effects cluster in the smaller trials (e.g. n = 40 studies at g ≈ −3.2 and −3.3; n = 56 at g ≈ −4.4; n = 60 at g ≈ −4.7), while several of the larger trials sit nearer the modest end (n = 100 at g ≈ −0.58; n = 80 at g ≈ −1.46). The relationship is not clean — one 106-participant study reports g ≈ −3.72 — but the overall gradient is the classic small-study pattern, and it is corroborated statistically in §8. Any such observation is hypothesis-generating only: an unmodelled bivariate gradient cannot distinguish bias from genuine effect modification correlated with trial size (small single-centre trials are also disproportionately conducted in high-opioid-requirement surgical populations by highly experienced block operators).

**Recommended moderator analyses for future updates**, in priority order: (i) surgical category; (ii) presence/absence of scheduled non-opioid multimodal background; (iii) bilateral versus unilateral block; (iv) total local anaesthetic mass; (v) blinding of outcome assessors; (vi) study precision (as a bias probe, via PET-PEESE or precision-weighted meta-regression).

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

**Table 3. Small-study-effect diagnostics.**

| Diagnostic | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 2.0046 |
| Egger's test p-value | < 0.001 (reported as 0.0) |
| Trim-and-fill: studies imputed (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill: adjusted estimate | −2.1483 (standardized scale) |
| Overall risk rating | **High** |

Egger's regression indicates **strong funnel-plot asymmetry (p < 0.001)** with a positive intercept of 2.0046, consistent with small-study effects: smaller, less precise trials report systematically larger benefits than larger, more precise ones. Trim-and-fill, by contrast, **imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0)** and left the estimate essentially at the observed value (−2.1483 on the standardized scale on which these diagnostics were computed).

This discordance should not be read as reassurance. As the analysis record notes, the pattern — significant Egger asymmetry with null trim-and-fill — typically arises when asymmetry is driven by heterogeneity, outliers, or a smooth precision gradient that the rank-based imputation algorithm does not classify as "missing" on the deficient side of the funnel. Trim-and-fill is known to be insensitive under exactly these conditions, and with I² = 99.9% those conditions are maximally met. **The overall high risk-of-bias rating is therefore appropriate, and the pooled estimate should be treated as potentially inflated.**

Three mechanisms plausibly contribute, and they are not mutually exclusive:

1. **Publication and reporting selection.** Small negative ESPB trials are less likely to be written up and published than small positive ones; the complete absence of any null or reversed estimate among 17 studies is itself suspicious for a literature of this size and heterogeneity.
2. **Performance and detection bias intrinsic to the comparator.** The protocol-mandated control here is *no injection at all* — not sham, not saline. Patients and ward staff therefore know who received the block. In an outcome that is entirely demand-driven (PCA presses and nurse-administered rescue), unblinding can move the measured endpoint directly, independent of any afferent blockade. This is the single largest threat to validity in this review and it inflates effect size in the same direction as publication bias.
3. **Heterogeneity-induced asymmetry.** Where small trials are systematically conducted in high-baseline-consumption surgical populations, the MD scale mechanically produces larger absolute effects in smaller studies without any bias at all.

**Recommended confirmatory approaches**, as flagged in the analysis record: PET-PEESE, selection/sensitivity models, and meta-regression on study precision. None of these was available for the present synthesis, so no bias-adjusted effect estimate is offered here. Readers should assume the true average effect is **smaller in magnitude than −38.59 mg OME**, though the direction is unlikely to reverse.

## 9. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following validation flags were raised during processing and are reported in full.

1. **One document excluded at the document-type gate as a systematic review / meta-analysis** (subject `manual:7ca2deb8`). The record was explicitly titled and conducted as a systematic review and meta-analysis: multi-database literature search, PRISMA reporting, PROSPERO registration, an included-studies table of 19 RCTs, Cochrane risk-of-bias assessment, pooled SMD/RR estimates with I² heterogeneity, forest plots, subgroup and sensitivity analyses, and publication-bias testing. It reported no newly collected primary data and was therefore excluded from the primary-study set. *Note that this excluded review covered 19 RCTs and the present synthesis draws on 19 primary records — some overlap in the underlying trials is likely, so this report should not be treated as independent corroboration of that review.*

2. **One document excluded as a near-duplicate / split fragment** (subject `manual:b564785a`), identified by full-text content similarity as a fragment of the already-included report `d639f241_SJA-14-186` (`manual:e57336c1`). It was removed to prevent double-counting of the same participants in the pooled estimate.

3. **Effect-measure conflict recorded (see §4.1).** The source papers explicitly report mean differences, but the contributing rows did not share a common `outcome_units` value. Because MD is scale-dependent, the pipeline did not trust the unit metadata alone and fell through to standardized-effect pooling logic at the measure-selection step. Native-scale pooling is nonetheless recorded as used (`native_pooling_used = true`) and the headline statistic is correctly labelled a **Mean Difference (MD)** — but the "mg OME" unit rests on the review's own conversion protocol rather than on verified uniform unit metadata.

4. **Scale mismatch between study-level and pooled outputs (see §4.1).** Study-level effects were supplied as Hedges' g while the pooled estimate is on the MD scale; the publication-bias diagnostics (Egger's intercept, trim-and-fill adjusted value −2.1483) are on the standardized scale. These quantities are not directly comparable to the −38.59 mg OME pooled value and are not presented as such.

5. **Two eligible studies contributed no effect size.** Of 19 eligible primary studies, 17 produced an analysable 24-hour effect size (`n_studies = 19`, `n_effect_sizes = 17`). Studies study_12 and study_16 met eligibility criteria but could not be converted into the required mean/SD/n form for the 0–24 h window under the pre-specified rules, and are absent from all pooled statistics.

No outcome rows were flagged as unclassifiable, and no additional outcome/timepoint groups requiring separate pooling were identified.

## 10. Discussion

This synthesis of 17 studies and 1,006 adults finds that adding an ultrasound-guided erector spinae plane block to usual perioperative care, compared with receiving no block at all, is associated with a **pooled mean difference of −38.59 mg oral morphine equivalents (95% CI −61.46 to −15.72; p = 0.00094) in cumulative opioid consumption over the first 24 postoperative hours — a result that favours ESPB.**

**What is well supported.** The *direction* of effect is as consistent as a meta-analysis of this kind can be: 17 of 17 studies favour ESPB, and 17 of 17 confidence intervals exclude the null. Across eight-fold variation in effect magnitude, multiple surgical populations, varied local anaesthetic regimens and varied index opioids, not a single contributing study found no effect or a reversed effect. That degree of unanimity is not readily explained by chance, and it aligns with the mechanistic expectation that interfascial local anaesthetic spread to dorsal and (variably) ventral rami produces real somatic afferent blockade. The clinical claim that ESPB reduces 24-hour opioid demand relative to no block is, in our judgement, well supported.

**What is not well supported.** The *magnitude* of that reduction is highly uncertain, for three converging reasons. First, heterogeneity is at the measurement ceiling (I² = 99.9%, τ ≈ 47.5 mg OME), so the between-study spread of true effects exceeds the pooled effect and the prediction interval crosses zero. Second, small-study effects are strongly evident (Egger p < 0.001) and the null trim-and-fill result provides no reassurance, as the analysis record itself notes; the pooled value should be read as plausibly inflated. Third, and most fundamentally, the pre-specified comparator is *no injection*, so no contributing study can have blinded patients or ward staff to allocation. For a patient-demand-driven endpoint like PCA consumption, expectancy effects alone can produce a measurable gap. This design feature is not a flaw in the review — it is the question that was asked — but it places a hard ceiling on the internal validity of the magnitude estimate.

**Reconciling these.** The most defensible reading is a two-tier conclusion: *high confidence in benefit, low confidence in size*. A clinician deciding whether to add ESPB to a pathway where no block is currently offered can reasonably expect a reduction in 24-hour opioid demand; a clinician budgeting for how much reduction, or modelling downstream effects on nausea, length of stay or persistent opioid use, should not plan on 38.6 mg OME. A more conservative working figure — perhaps half that, pending PET-PEESE or selection-model adjustment and surgery-specific subgrouping — would be more prudent.

**Relation to existing literature.** One systematic review and meta-analysis covering 19 RCTs was identified and correctly excluded from the primary-study set (§9). Because its trial pool likely overlaps substantially with ours, agreement between that review and this one would not constitute independent replication. The broader ESPB literature has repeatedly reported large standardized opioid-sparing effects alongside very high heterogeneity, and the present analysis reproduces both features — including, notably, the finding that heterogeneity is not resolved by restricting the comparator to no-block alone. That is informative: comparator heterogeneity is evidently not the dominant source, and attention should shift to surgical population, background multimodal regimen, and block dose.

**Clinical implications.** (i) ESPB is a reasonable addition to multimodal pathways in which no regional technique is currently used, on the basis of direction-consistent evidence. (ii) The absolute opioid-sparing benefit should be estimated locally, surgery by surgery, not imported from this pooled figure. (iii) The near-total heterogeneity means a unit that introduces ESPB should audit its own 24-hour consumption rather than assume the pooled effect.

**Research implications.** The marginal value of another small, unblinded, single-centre ESPB-versus-nothing trial is close to zero — such trials will add to τ² and to funnel asymmetry without resolving either. What is needed is: (a) adequately powered multicentre trials with **sham-injection controls** and blinded outcome assessment, which would separate afferent blockade from expectancy; (b) consistent reporting of 24-hour consumption as mean and SD in a declared unit, with the index opioid and conversion factor stated; (c) individual-participant-data synthesis, which would permit the surgery-stratified and dose-response modelling that aggregate data cannot support here.

---

## 11. Limitations

1. **Extreme heterogeneity (I² = 99.9%, τ² = 2259.89).** The pooled mean difference summarises a broad distribution of true effects rather than estimating a common effect. The approximate 95% prediction interval (≈ −143 to +66 mg OME) includes no effect and includes harm.
2. **High risk of small-study effects.** Egger's test is strongly significant (intercept 2.0046, p < 0.001); trim-and-fill's null result (k₀ = 0) is uninformative under this degree of heterogeneity and should not be read as reassurance. No bias-adjusted estimate (PET-PEESE, selection model) was available, so the pooled value may be inflated.
3. **No blinding is possible in the specified comparison.** The comparator is no injection rather than sham, so performance and detection bias affect every contributing study in the direction of favouring ESPB, for an endpoint (patient-demand opioid consumption) that is especially susceptible to expectancy.
4. **No moderator analysis.** Surgical type, block dose and laterality, background multimodal analgesia, index opioid and blinding status were unavailable as structured covariates; no meta-regression or subgroup estimates could be produced, and no part of τ² could be attributed.
5. **Effect-measure and unit caveats.** Contributing rows did not share a common `outcome_units` value, and the MD scale is unit-dependent; the "mg OME" scale therefore rests on the review's conversion protocol rather than on verified uniform metadata. Separately, study-level effects were supplied as Hedges' g while the pooled estimate is on the MD scale, so the two cannot be visually cross-checked, and the bias diagnostics are on the standardized scale.
6. **Conversion-induced error.** Converting tramadol, pethidine, nalbuphine, fentanyl and morphine to OME uses equianalgesic ratios with substantial published variation; conversion error is multiplicative and inflates both point-estimate error and between-study variance. Means and SDs reconstructed from medians and IQRs add further approximation.
7. **Incomplete outcome capture.** Two of 19 eligible studies (study_12, study_16) contributed no computable 24-hour effect size. If their omission is related to the direction or size of their findings, selective-availability bias is introduced.
8. **Small trials, single outcome, single timepoint.** No contributing study exceeded 106 participants. Per protocol, 2-hour and 48-hour totals and intraoperative doses were excluded, so nothing can be said here about the duration of the opioid-sparing effect beyond 24 hours, nor about pain scores, nausea, block complications, mobilisation or length of stay.
9. **No formal risk-of-bias instrument was applied** at the study level within this synthesis (e.g. RoB 2 domain ratings), so the "high" rating reported reflects small-study-effect diagnostics and design features rather than a per-domain assessment.
10. **Certainty of evidence.** By GRADE logic, the body of evidence would be downgraded for risk of bias (no blinding, high small-study-effect risk) and for inconsistency (I² = 99.9%), yielding **low certainty for the magnitude** of effect, while the unanimity of direction supports **moderate confidence that some benefit exists**.

---

## 12. Conclusion

Across 17 studies and 1,006 adults undergoing surgery, ultrasound-guided erector spinae plane block with local anaesthetic, added to usual perioperative care, was associated with a **pooled Mean Difference of −38.59 mg oral morphine equivalents (95% CI −61.46 to −15.72; p = 0.00094)** in cumulative opioid consumption over the first 24 postoperative hours compared with no block. This result **favours ultrasound-guided ESPB**, and the direction of effect was unanimous — all 17 contributing studies favoured the block and all 17 confidence intervals excluded the null.

The magnitude, however, carries low certainty. Heterogeneity was essentially total (I² = 99.9%; τ² = 2259.89, τ ≈ 47.5 mg OME, exceeding the pooled effect itself), Egger's test showed strong funnel asymmetry (p < 0.001) with a null trim-and-fill result that is uninformative under such heterogeneity, and the protocol-specified no-injection comparator precludes blinding for a demand-driven endpoint. The pooled figure should be treated as potentially inflated and not transportable to any specific surgical population without local verification.

**Bottom line for practice:** ESPB plausibly and consistently reduces 24-hour postoperative opioid requirement relative to offering no block, and is a reasonable addition to multimodal pathways; but the size of that reduction should not be planned around 38.6 mg OME. **Bottom line for research:** further small unblinded ESPB-versus-nothing trials add little. Sham-controlled, blinded, adequately powered multicentre trials with standardised OME reporting — and, ideally, individual-participant-data synthesis permitting surgery-stratified and dose-response modelling — are what would convert a direction-certain finding into a magnitude-certain one.
