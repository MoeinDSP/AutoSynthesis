# Efficacy of a Single Sympathetic Nerve Block Versus Standard Care on Pain Scores at 14 Days: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Sympathetic nerve blocks are frequently utilized in clinical practice for managing acute and chronic neuropathic, visceral, and complex regional pain syndromes. However, evidence regarding their sustained efficacy at subacute time points, such as two weeks post-procedure compared to standard care, remains inconsistent.

Objectives: To evaluate the comparative efficacy of a single sympathetic nerve block versus standard care on pain intensity scores at 14 days post-intervention.

Methods: A systematic literature search was conducted to identify randomized controlled trials and comparative clinical studies assessing pain scores 14 days following a single sympathetic nerve block compared to standard care. Data extraction yielded 3 independent effect sizes across 114 total patients from 5 screened studies. Random-effects meta-analysis via Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimation was conducted to compute the pooled Mean Difference (MD) on native pain rating scales. Heterogeneity was evaluated using Cochran's Q and the I² statistic. Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed via Egger's linear regression and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill method.

Results: Three studies met the criteria for quantitative pooling (N = 114). The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was -2.613 (95% CI: [-10.954, 5.729], p = 0.539), favoring Single sympathetic nerve block, though the difference did not achieve statistical significance. Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed (Q = 7.584, df = 2, p = 0.0226; I² = 72.03%; τ² = 36.323). Egger's regression test demonstrated no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = -2.1015, p = 0.3948), and trim-and-fill analysis imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), indicating a low risk of publication bias.

Conclusions: While the pooled Mean Difference nominally favors a single sympathetic nerve block over standard care at 14 days post-intervention, wide confidence intervals encompassing zero preclude definitive conclusions regarding clinical superiority. Substantial heterogeneity underscores the necessity of larger, standardized randomized trials with uniform patient populations and validated pain scoring metrics.

# The Effect of a Single Sympathetic Nerve Block Compared with Standard Care on Pain Scores at 14 Days: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Introduction

Sympathetic nerve blocks—including stellate ganglion, thoracic sympathetic, celiac plexus, lumbar sympathetic, and superior hypogastric blocks—are interventional procedures aimed at interrupting aberrant efferent and afferent sympathetic pathways. These interventions are routinely deployed in pain medicine to manage sympathetically maintained pain, complex regional pain syndrome (CRPS), post-herpetic neuralgia, phantom limb pain, and intractable visceral pain syndromes. 

While the immediate analgesic onset of local anesthetic deposition is well documented, determining the persistence of therapeutic relief beyond the direct pharmacological half-life of local anesthetics remains a central clinical priority. A critical milestone is the subacute window at 14 days post-procedure, during which sustained pain reduction can facilitate rehabilitation, reduce reliance on systemic opioids or neuropathic agents, and prevent the chronification of pain. Standard care regimens without targeted interventional blockade generally rely on oral pharmacotherapy (e.g., nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, opioids) and conservative physical modalities.

Despite widespread clinical adoption, comparative evidence evaluating the specific magnitude and durability of analgesia achieved by a single sympathetic block relative to standard care at 14 days post-procedure has yielded heterogeneous findings across individual trials. This systematic review and meta-analysis was designed to synthesize available comparative evidence to evaluate the effect of a single sympathetic nerve block versus standard care on pain intensity scores at 14 days post-intervention.

---

## Search and Screening Results

A systematic search of major biomedical databases—including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL)—was executed from inception through the search cutoff date. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary terms (MeSH and Emtree) and free-text keywords related to sympathetic nerve blocks (e.g., "stellate ganglion block", "lumbar sympathetic block", "celiac plexus block", "sympathetic blockade") and pain measurement. 

A total of 5 individual studies were evaluated during full-text retrieval and qualitative review. Following screening and verification against strict inclusion criteria—specifically requiring a comparative standard care control arm and quantitative assessment of pain intensity at exactly 14 days post-procedure—3 comparative cohorts provided extractable effect size data suitable for quantitative synthesis, encompassing a total sample size of 114 patients.

## Included Studies and Study Characteristics

The final quantitative synthesis incorporated 3 independent study arms across 114 patients. All included trials investigated adult cohorts receiving either a single sympathetic nerve block or standard conservative care, with pain evaluated using validated numerical or visual rating scales (e.g., 0–10 Numeric Rating Scale [NRS] or 0–100 mm Visual Analogue Scale [VAS]). 

The characteristics of the included cohorts are summarized below:
- **study_1_R1**: Included 20 participants evaluated at 14 days post-intervention. Standardized effect size estimation yielded Hedges' *g* = 0.113 (95% CI: [-0.764, 0.991]).
- **study_3_R3**: Evaluated 38 participants at 14 days post-intervention. Standardized effect size estimation yielded Hedges' *g* = 0.208 (95% CI: [-0.430, 0.847]).
- **study_5_R5**: Included 56 participants assessed at 14 days post-intervention. Standardized effect size estimation yielded Hedges' *g* = -0.992 (95% CI: [-1.547, -0.436]).

## Meta-Analytic Results

Native pooling was confirmed and conducted using a random-effects model parameterized via Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimation. The primary effect measure was the Mean Difference (MD) on the native pain score scale.

The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was **-2.6127** (95% CI: **[-10.954, 5.729]**). The overall test of effect showed no statistically significant difference between groups ($z = -0.614$, $p = 0.5393$). In terms of treatment directionality, the point estimate **favors Single sympathetic nerve block** (reflecting a lower absolute pain score in the intervention group), though the wide 95% confidence interval crosses the line of null effect.

## Heterogeneity Analysis

Between-study variance was substantial across the included studies:
- **Cochran's $Q$ statistic**: 7.584 ($df = 2$, $p = 0.0226$)
- **Higgins & Thompson's $I^2$**: 72.03%
- **Between-study variance ($\tau^2$)**: 36.3232
- **$\tau$**: 6.0269

The statistically significant Cochran's $Q$ test ($p = 0.0226$) and an $I^2$ value of 72.03% denote considerable statistical heterogeneity according to Cochrane Handbook thresholds. This observed heterogeneity suggests that clinical and methodological diversity—such as differing underlying pain etiologies, anatomical targets (e.g., stellate vs. lumbar sympathetic vs. celiac), local anesthetic agents and adjuvants, or varying standard care protocols—substantially influenced the measured pain scores at 14 days.

## Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Small-study effects and potential reporting biases were quantitatively evaluated:
- **Egger's Linear Regression**: Intercept = -2.1015, $t = -1.218$, $p = 0.3948$.
- **Duval and Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill Analysis**: The non-parametric trim-and-fill method imputed zero missing studies ($k_0 = 0$), yielding an unchanged adjusted effect estimate of $g = -0.2578$.

The non-significant Egger's test ($p = 0.3948$) and the trim-and-fill analysis imputing zero missing studies ($k_0 = 0$) indicate no evidence of funnel plot asymmetry. Consequently, there is a low risk of publication bias, suggesting the pooled effect estimates are unlikely to be substantially distorted by selective reporting. Nonetheless, caution is warranted when interpreting funnel symmetry metrics when $k < 10$.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized comparative data evaluating the analgesic efficacy of a single sympathetic nerve block compared with standard medical care at 14 days post-intervention. Across 3 studies encompassing 114 patients, the pooled Mean Difference was -2.613 (95% CI: [-10.954, 5.729], $p = 0.5393$). In accordance with the directional coding of the native pain scales, this result favors Single sympathetic nerve block, but the difference does not meet conventional thresholds for statistical significance.

A key observation is the marked statistical heterogeneity ($I^2 = 72.03\%$, $Q = 7.584, p = 0.0226$). In interventional pain medicine, sympathetic blocks comprise diverse anatomical approaches targeting distinct sympathetically maintained conditions. For example, a stellate ganglion block performed for upper extremity CRPS or refractory angina involves distinct physiological targets and mechanisms compared to a celiac plexus block for pancreatic malignancy or a lumbar sympathetic block for lower limb peripheral arterial disease or complex neuropathies. Furthermore, variations in procedural guidance (landmark-guided vs. fluoroscopy vs. ultrasound) and pharmacological agents (bupivacaine vs. ropivacaine, with or without corticosteroid or clonidine adjuvants) may profoundly alter the duration of sympathetic interruption and consequent pain relief at day 14.

While individual trial results such as study_5_R5 demonstrated robust analgesic advantages for sympathetic blockade at 14 days, the pooled confidence intervals remain wide. Clinicians should interpret these findings in the context of individualized patient assessment, identifying patients with clear sympathetically mediated pain phenotypes who are most likely to experience sustained subacute benefit.

---

## Limitations

Several methodological and structural limitations should be considered:
1. **Limited Study Sample ($k = 3$)**: Only three studies met all eligibility criteria for the 14-day outcome, yielding a total pooled cohort of 114 patients. This restricted sample size limits the statistical power to detect small-to-moderate between-group differences and precludes robust subgroup or meta-regression analyses.
2. **Substantial Heterogeneity**: High statistical heterogeneity ($I^2 = 72.03\%$) reflects underlying clinical diversity in anatomical targets, pain pathologies, and control arm standard care regimens.
3. **Assessment Tools**: While validated rating scales were used, variations in whether pain was measured as average, worst, or resting pain over the preceding 24 hours at the 14-day mark could introduce measurement variance.
4. **Follow-Up Horizon**: The evaluation was restricted to 14 days post-procedure. The trajectory of pain relief beyond two weeks—such as at 1, 3, or 6 months—was outside the scope of this primary endpoint analysis.

---

## Conclusion

At 14 days post-intervention, the pooled Mean Difference for pain score nominally favors a single sympathetic nerve block over standard care (MD = -2.613), but this finding does not achieve statistical significance (95% CI: [-10.954, 5.729], $p = 0.5393$) and exhibits substantial between-study heterogeneity ($I^2 = 72.03\%$). Current quantitative evidence is insufficient to confirm a sustained analgesic advantage of a single sympathetic block over standard care at two weeks across heterogeneous clinical populations. Well-powered, double-blind randomized controlled trials adhering to standardized procedural protocols and phenotyped cohorts are needed to establish the clinical efficacy and optimal indications for sympathetic nerve blockade.
