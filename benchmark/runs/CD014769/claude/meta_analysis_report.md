# Statin Therapy versus Placebo for the Primary Prevention of Deep Vein Thrombosis: A PRISMA-Compliant Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis with Planned Stratification by Statin Type

## Abstract

**Background.** Statins exert pleiotropic anti-inflammatory and antithrombotic effects that may extend beyond arterial atherothrombosis to the venous circulation. Whether statin therapy reduces the incidence of deep vein thrombosis (DVT) when used for primary prevention, and whether any such effect differs across individual agents (rosuvastatin, atorvastatin, pravastatin), remains uncertain.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of statin therapy compared with placebo on the incidence of DVT during randomised trial follow-up, with rosuvastatin, atorvastatin and pravastatin trials planned as separate subgroups.

**Methods.** A systematic search was conducted for randomised placebo-controlled trials of rosuvastatin, atorvastatin or pravastatin reporting DVT events. For each trial the number of participants with a DVT event and the number randomised per arm were extracted. Effects were pooled as odds ratios (OR) using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression test and the trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** Six documents were screened into the review and yielded 4 independent, poolable effect sizes contributing 36,793 randomised participants. The pooled Odds Ratio (OR) was 0.664 (95% CI 0.367 to 1.204; p = 0.1775; k = 4; REML). Native OR pooling was used, so this is a confirmed native-scale ratio estimate rather than a standardized fallback. The direction field for this analysis records that the result favours **neither arm** — it is essentially a statistical tie, or more precisely a ratio measure whose favourability depends on which event was counted — because the confidence interval comfortably spans the null value of 1. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (Q = 9.992, df = 3, p = 0.0186; I² = 67.86%; τ² = 0.242743). Egger's test showed no funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept = −0.2668, p = 0.9089) and trim-and-fill imputed only one potentially missing study (k₀ = 1, adjusted estimate −0.1714 on the standardized scale), although overall small-study-effect risk was rated *moderate* on account of the small number of trials rather than any positive evidence of bias. The pre-specified stratification by statin type could not be executed as separate pooled estimates: with only four effect sizes distributed across three distinct agents, no statin-specific subgroup contained enough independent trials to support a credible subgroup synthesis.

**Conclusions.** In this random-effects meta-analysis of four placebo-controlled comparisons, statin therapy was not associated with a statistically significant change in the incidence of DVT, and the pooled odds ratio favoured neither arm. The point estimate is numerically below 1, but the confidence interval is wide, heterogeneity is substantial, and the evidence base is too thin to resolve agent-specific effects. These findings do not support — and do not exclude — a protective effect of statins on venous thromboembolism; adequately powered trials with prospectively adjudicated DVT endpoints are required.

## 1. Introduction

Venous thromboembolism (VTE), comprising deep vein thrombosis (DVT) and pulmonary embolism, is a leading cause of preventable morbidity and mortality worldwide. Although anticoagulation remains the cornerstone of both treatment and situational prophylaxis, there is no widely adopted pharmacological strategy for the *primary* prevention of a first venous thrombotic event in the general or cardiovascular-risk population.

Interest in 3-hydroxy-3-methylglutaryl coenzyme A (HMG-CoA) reductase inhibitors — statins — as candidate agents for venous thromboprophylaxis stems from their well-documented pleiotropic properties. Beyond low-density lipoprotein reduction, statins attenuate C-reactive protein and interleukin-6 signalling, down-regulate tissue factor expression on monocytes and endothelium, reduce thrombin generation and fibrinogen-mediated platelet activation, and improve endothelial nitric oxide bioavailability. Because venous thrombosis is increasingly understood as an inflammation-driven process involving endothelial activation, neutrophil extracellular traps and tissue-factor-bearing microvesicles rather than purely mechanical stasis, these mechanisms offer a biologically plausible route by which statins could lower DVT incidence.

Observational cohorts and case-control studies have repeatedly reported lower VTE rates among statin users, but such designs are vulnerable to confounding by indication, healthy-adherer bias and differential surveillance. Randomised placebo-controlled trials provide the least biased estimate, yet in most statin trials DVT was a non-prespecified, sparsely reported or adverse-event-derived outcome, and no individual trial was powered for it. A further open question is whether any benefit is class-wide or agent-specific: rosuvastatin, atorvastatin and pravastatin differ substantially in lipophilicity, potency, hepatic metabolism and magnitude of C-reactive protein lowering, so a stratified analysis is scientifically warranted.

This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore addresses a single focused question: **what is the effect of statin therapy compared with placebo on the incidence of DVT for the primary prevention of venous thromboembolism, stratified by statin type (rosuvastatin, atorvastatin, pravastatin)?**

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting standards

The review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. The population, intervention, comparator, outcome and study-design (PICOS) framework was specified a priori:

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Adults enrolled in randomised trials of statin therapy without a requirement for prior VTE (primary-prevention setting) |
| **Intervention** | Statin therapy — rosuvastatin, atorvastatin or pravastatin, at any dose, administered orally |
| **Comparator** | Matched placebo |
| **Outcome** | Incidence of DVT events during trial follow-up, extracted as the number of participants with a DVT event and the total number randomised in each arm |
| **Study design** | Randomised, placebo-controlled trials |

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

Records were eligible if they (i) reported a randomised comparison of rosuvastatin, atorvastatin or pravastatin against placebo; (ii) reported DVT events in a form permitting construction of a 2 × 2 table (events and totals per arm); and (iii) were primary trial reports rather than narrative reviews, editorials, pooled commentaries or duplicate publications of an already-included trial. Records were excluded if DVT could not be separated from a composite venous or arterial endpoint, if no placebo arm existed, or if the publication represented a secondary/overlapping report of a trial already contributing data, in which case the most complete report was retained to avoid double-counting participants.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

A structured search of the biomedical literature was performed targeting randomised placebo-controlled statin trials reporting venous thromboembolic outcomes, combining controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for the three index agents (*rosuvastatin*, *atorvastatin*, *pravastatin*), for the intervention class (*statin*, *HMG-CoA reductase inhibitor*), and for the outcome (*deep vein thrombosis*, *venous thromboembolism*, *venous thrombosis*), restricted to randomised controlled trial designs with a placebo comparator. Reference lists of retrieved trial reports and relevant syntheses were screened for additional candidates.

### 2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Titles, abstracts and then full texts were screened against the eligibility criteria. For each included trial the following were extracted: statin agent and dose, number of participants randomised to each arm, and number of participants experiencing a DVT event in each arm. Effect sizes were computed from these arm-level counts.

### 2.5 Effect measure

The effect measure was pre-specified by the review question as the **odds ratio (OR)**, computed from the DVT event counts and arm totals. It should be noted transparently that the source trial reports for at least part of this evidence base express their venous-thromboembolism results as **hazard ratios**, not odds ratios. The analysis platform flagged this discrepancy (`effect_measure_conflict`: "user requested OR, but the paper(s) explicitly report HR — user request kept as authoritative"). The explicit analytic request for an OR was retained as authoritative, and all pooling was performed on the OR scale. Critically, `native_pooling_used` is **true** for this synthesis: the pooled value is a genuine, natively pooled odds ratio computed on the ratio scale, not a standardized fallback conversion. Readers comparing this report with trial publications that report hazard ratios should expect numerically similar but not identical values, since ORs and HRs diverge as event rates rise and depend on different modelling assumptions (odds of ever having an event versus instantaneous hazard).

Alongside the ratio-scale synthesis, the pipeline also records a standardized per-study representation for each comparison (labelled `hedges_g` in the data structure) for use in the funnel-based small-study diagnostics. These standardized values are reported in Section 4 for completeness and for auditability of the bias analyses, but they are **not** the headline effect measure and should not be interpreted as the treatment effect; the confirmed native-scale result is the odds ratio.

### 2.6 Synthesis methods

Effect sizes were combined using a **random-effects model** with between-study variance (τ²) estimated by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**, a choice appropriate when the included trials differ in agent, dose, population and follow-up duration and a common true effect cannot be assumed. Heterogeneity was characterised by Cochran's Q with its associated p-value, by the I² statistic (proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error) and by τ² (the absolute between-study variance on the analysis scale). A 95% confidence interval accompanies the pooled estimate.

**Planned stratification.** The review question specified that rosuvastatin, atorvastatin and pravastatin trials be maintained as separate subgroups. This stratification was attempted but, as detailed in Section 6, could not be delivered as three independent pooled estimates given the number of contributing trials.

### 2.7 Small-study effects and publication bias

Funnel-plot asymmetry was assessed with **Egger's regression test** of the standardized effect against its precision, and the **Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill** procedure was applied to estimate the number of potentially missing studies (k₀) and the correspondingly adjusted estimate. Both procedures are acknowledged to be underpowered when fewer than roughly ten studies are available, and their results are interpreted accordingly.

## 3. Search and Screening Results

The systematic search and screening process yielded **6 documents** that passed title/abstract and full-text screening into the review corpus. From these, **4 independent, non-overlapping effect sizes** were extractable and eligible for quantitative pooling. The reduction from six screened documents to four analysable comparisons reflects the standard PRISMA attrition at the data-extraction stage: documents that were secondary or overlapping reports of an already-represented trial were excluded so that no cohort contributed twice, and documents that did not provide DVT events and arm totals in an extractable 2 × 2 form could not be converted into an effect size. The four retained comparisons contribute **36,793 randomised participants** in total.

A PRISMA-style accounting of the synthesis stage is therefore:

- Documents entering the review after screening: **6**
- Independent comparisons contributing quantitative data: **4**
- Documents not contributing a poolable effect size: **2** (overlapping/duplicate trial reporting or non-extractable DVT outcome data)
- Participants in the quantitative synthesis: **36,793**

No study was excluded on the basis of its results or the direction of its findings.

Several features of this evidence base are worth highlighting before the pooled result is interpreted.

**Extreme imbalance in trial size.** The analysed samples span two orders of magnitude, from 587 to 17,802 participants. Under an inverse-variance random-effects weighting scheme, the two largest comparisons (study_2_R2 and study_3_R3, together 30,507 participants, 82.9% of the total sample) dominate the precision of the synthesis, while the smallest comparison (study_4_R4, 587 participants, 1.6% of the total) contributes little weight to the pooled estimate but a great deal of leverage to the heterogeneity statistics and to the funnel-based bias diagnostics.

**Directional discordance.** Three of the four comparisons (study_2_R2, study_3_R3, study_4_R4) point in one direction while study_1_R1 points in the opposite direction, and the confidence intervals of study_1_R1 and the three others do not overlap. This single directional outlier is the dominant source of the between-study heterogeneity documented in Section 6. Notably, the two largest comparisons produce almost identical standardized estimates (−0.4402 and −0.4402), indicating that the discordance is not a smooth gradient but a discrete split between one divergent trial and a tightly concordant remainder.

**Rare-event outcome.** DVT is an uncommon event even in large cardiovascular prevention cohorts. Odds ratios derived from small absolute event counts have wide sampling distributions and are sensitive to continuity corrections and to zero-cell handling; this is an intrinsic feature of the outcome rather than a defect of any individual trial, but it constrains how precisely any pooled ratio can be estimated.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Results

### 5.1 Primary pooled estimate

Pooling the four comparisons under a REML random-effects model on the odds-ratio scale gives:

> **Pooled Odds Ratio (OR) = 0.664 (95% CI 0.367 to 1.204), p = 0.1775, k = 4, N = 36,793, REML random-effects model.**

The effect measure is a **confirmed native-scale odds ratio** (`native_pooling_used` = true); this is not a standardized fallback value. The accompanying `pooled_g` field in the analysis record carries the identical numeric value (0.6643) because the computation scale and the reporting scale are both the odds-ratio scale for this synthesis.

**Direction of effect.** Per the recorded direction field, the pooled result favours **neither arm** — it is, in the analysis platform's own characterisation, "essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted." This is the authoritative statement of direction for this synthesis and should not be overridden by casual inspection of the point estimate. Although 0.664 lies numerically below the null value of 1 and would, taken in isolation and assuming DVT events were the counted event, be read as a 33.6% relative reduction in the odds of DVT, the 95% confidence interval (0.367 to 1.204) spans the null comfortably: the data are compatible with anything from a 63.3% relative reduction in the odds of DVT to a 20.4% relative increase. With p = 0.1775, the null hypothesis of no difference between statin therapy and placebo is not rejected at any conventional significance threshold.

### 5.2 Interpretation of the magnitude

The clinical reading of this result is that of an **inconclusive but not reassuringly null** finding. The point estimate is of a size that, were it real, would be clinically meaningful: a one-third reduction in the odds of a first DVT from an oral agent already prescribed to tens of millions of people would represent a substantial public-health gain. But the interval is too wide to establish it, and the width is driven by two separate problems operating simultaneously — a small number of contributing trials (k = 4) and substantial between-trial heterogeneity (I² = 67.86%), the latter inflating the random-effects standard error through τ².

It is also important to be explicit that a non-significant pooled estimate with this interval does **not** demonstrate equivalence. The data neither establish benefit nor exclude it; a true protective effect of the magnitude suggested by the point estimate remains entirely compatible with these results, as does a modest harm.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **substantial**:

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 9.992 |
| Degrees of freedom | 3 |
| Q p-value | 0.0186 |
| I² | 67.86% |
| τ² (between-study variance) | 0.242743 |
| Estimator | REML |

Cochran's Q is statistically significant (p = 0.0186), formally rejecting the hypothesis that all four comparisons estimate a single common effect. The I² value of 67.86% indicates that approximately two-thirds of the total observed variability across comparisons reflects genuine between-study differences in the underlying effect rather than within-study sampling error — conventionally classified as substantial heterogeneity, at the boundary of the "moderate" (30–60%) and "substantial" (50–90%) bands. The τ² of 0.242743 quantifies the same dispersion in absolute terms and is the component responsible for widening the random-effects confidence interval relative to what a fixed-effect analysis would have produced.

Three caveats apply to these statistics. First, with only three degrees of freedom, **both Q and I² are estimated very imprecisely**; the confidence interval around an I² of 67.86% based on four studies is extremely wide and would plausibly extend from near zero to above 90%. Second, inspection of Table 1 makes clear that the heterogeneity is **structurally attributable to a single discordant comparison** (study_1_R1), with the remaining three being mutually consistent and the two largest being nearly identical. This is heterogeneity of a specific, identifiable kind rather than diffuse scatter. Third, because DVT is a rare outcome, apparent heterogeneity can be generated by differences in **outcome ascertainment** — whether DVT was prospectively adjudicated, captured as a reported adverse event, or identified only when symptomatic — as much as by differences in true pharmacological effect.

Plausible clinical and methodological sources of heterogeneity include: differences in statin agent, potency and lipophilicity; differences in baseline VTE risk across the enrolled populations; differences in follow-up duration (and hence in person-time at risk for a rare event); differences in whether DVT was a pre-specified endpoint; and differences in background use of antiplatelet or anticoagulant therapy. Given k = 4, none of these can be formally tested.

In light of the substantial heterogeneity, the random-effects model is the appropriate specification, and the pooled estimate should be read as the **mean of a distribution of effects** rather than as a single common effect applicable to all statins in all populations. A prediction interval for the effect in a new trial would be wider still than the reported confidence interval and would unambiguously include both benefit and harm.

## 7. Moderator and Subgroup Analyses

### 7.1 Planned stratification by statin type — not estimable as separate pooled subgroups

The review question explicitly requested that rosuvastatin, atorvastatin and pravastatin trials be maintained as **separate subgroups**, each with its own pooled odds ratio. This stratification **could not be delivered as three independent pooled estimates**, and this is stated plainly rather than substituted with an invented value.

The reason is arithmetic. Only **four** independent comparisons were available for the entire synthesis, distributed across **three** distinct statin agents. Under any allocation of four trials to three agents, at least two of the three subgroups must contain a single trial, and no subgroup can contain more than two. A "pooled" estimate from a single trial is simply that trial's own effect size and carries no between-study variance information; a two-trial subgroup yields a τ² estimate that is effectively uninformative. Consequently:

- **No statin-specific pooled odds ratio, confidence interval or heterogeneity statistic is reported here**, because none can be estimated with any credibility from the available data.
- **No formal test for subgroup differences** (between-subgroup Q, or meta-regression on statin type) was performed. With three levels of a categorical moderator and four data points, such a model would be saturated or over-parameterised and would produce a spuriously precise answer.
- The single pooled estimate reported in Section 5 — OR 0.664 (95% CI 0.367 to 1.204) — is therefore a **class-level, agent-pooled estimate** that collapses rosuvastatin, atorvastatin and pravastatin into one analysis. It should not be cited as evidence about any individual agent.

This is an important negative methodological finding in its own right: the current randomised evidence base on statins and primary DVT prevention is not large enough to answer the agent-specific question that clinicians and guideline committees most want answered.

### 7.2 Other moderators

No other moderator analyses (by dose, follow-up duration, baseline cardiovascular risk, or outcome-adjudication method) were conducted, for the same reason — four effect sizes cannot support meta-regression. The relationship between effect size and study precision is displayed descriptively in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`), and the heterogeneity decomposition is visualised in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`, but these are exploratory displays, not inferential tests.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | −0.2668 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.9089 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 1 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate (standardized scale) | −0.1714 |
| Overall small-study-effect risk rating | **Moderate** |

**Egger's test** showed no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept = −0.2668, p = 0.9089). The p-value is far from any conventional threshold, and the intercept is small in magnitude. **Trim-and-fill** imputed only a single potentially missing study (k₀ = 1), with a correspondingly minimal adjustment to the summary estimate (−0.1714 on the standardized scale used for this diagnostic). Taken together, these two procedures provide **no positive evidence of small-study effects or publication bias** in this meta-analysis.

The overall risk rating of **"moderate"** should therefore be read as reflecting factors beyond these specific tests rather than as a positive detection of bias. Those factors include: (i) the very limited number of included comparisons (k = 4), which leaves Egger's test severely underpowered — with four points a regression of effect on precision has almost no ability to detect genuine asymmetry — and renders trim-and-fill unreliable; (ii) a search strategy restricted to the published literature, with limited interrogation of grey literature, trial registries and unpublished results; and (iii) the substantial heterogeneity documented in Section 6, which can both mimic and mask funnel asymmetry, since a genuinely discordant small or large trial distorts the funnel independently of any suppression mechanism.

The appropriate conclusion is **"no bias detected" rather than "bias ruled out."** A further specific concern for this literature is that DVT was frequently a non-prespecified or adverse-event-derived outcome in statin trials; outcomes of that type are systematically less likely to be reported at all when null, creating a selective-reporting pathway that funnel-based diagnostics are poorly suited to detect.

## 9. Discussion

This meta-analysis of four placebo-controlled randomised comparisons, encompassing 36,793 participants, found a pooled Odds Ratio of 0.664 (95% CI 0.367 to 1.204; p = 0.1775) for the incidence of DVT with statin therapy relative to placebo. The direction field for this result records that it favours **neither arm**, consistent with a confidence interval that spans the null.

Three observations frame the interpretation.

**First, the point estimate is suggestive but the interval is not.** A pooled odds ratio of 0.664 is not a trivial numerical signal, and it is directionally concordant with the large observational literature reporting lower VTE rates among statin users and with the mechanistic rationale summarised in the Introduction. But the interval reaches from a substantial reduction to a modest increase in the odds of DVT, and the associated p-value of 0.1775 offers no statistical support for rejecting the null. Treating this as a positive finding would be an overreach; treating it as evidence of no effect would be an equal and opposite error, since a clinically important benefit sits squarely inside the interval.

**Second, heterogeneity is substantial and structured.** With I² = 67.86% and a significant Q test (p = 0.0186), the four comparisons do not share a common effect. The pattern in Table 1 is informative: three comparisons agree closely (including the two largest, which are nearly identical) and one dissents sharply in the opposite direction. This concentrated discordance is more consistent with a specific explanatory difference — a different agent, a different population's baseline risk, or a different outcome-ascertainment method — than with random scatter. Unfortunately, with k = 4, that explanation cannot be identified statistically, only hypothesised.

**Third, the question actually posed — the agent-stratified question — remains unanswered.** The pre-specified subgroup analysis by statin type could not be performed, because four trials cannot be meaningfully distributed across three agents. This matters clinically. Rosuvastatin, atorvastatin and pravastatin differ markedly in potency, lipophilicity and C-reactive-protein-lowering capacity, and if the venous effect of statins is mediated by anti-inflammatory rather than lipid-lowering mechanisms, one would positively expect agent-level differences. Collapsing the agents into a single class estimate, as was necessary here, may itself be a source of the observed heterogeneity — that is, the substantial I² may partly *be* the unmeasured subgroup effect. This hypothesis is plausible, testable in principle, and untestable with the present data.

**Relation to the wider literature.** The present findings sit between the optimistic observational signal and the more cautious randomised picture. They are broadly compatible with prior syntheses suggesting a possible but unproven reduction in VTE with statins, and they reinforce the recurring message that the randomised evidence base for this specific outcome is thin because DVT was rarely a pre-specified endpoint in cardiovascular statin trials. Nothing in these results supports a change in clinical practice: statins should continue to be prescribed on their established cardiovascular indications, and venous thromboprophylaxis decisions should continue to rest on validated risk-stratified anticoagulation strategies.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

The following data-handling and validation issues arose during this synthesis and are stated plainly:

1. **Effect-measure conflict (resolved in favour of the user request).** The analysis was explicitly specified to use the **odds ratio**. The underlying trial publication(s) for at least part of this evidence base, however, report their venous-thromboembolism results as **hazard ratios**. The platform recorded this as an explicit conflict (`effect_measure_conflict`: "user requested OR, but the paper(s) explicitly report HR — user request kept as authoritative"). The user-specified OR was retained as the authoritative measure and all pooling was carried out on the odds-ratio scale. Readers should expect the values reported here to differ from the hazard ratios printed in the source trials; the two are different statistics with different modelling assumptions and are not interchangeable, particularly as event rates increase.

2. **Native pooling confirmed.** `native_pooling_used` is **true** for this synthesis. The pooled estimate of 0.664 is a genuine native-scale odds ratio computed on the ratio scale, not a standardized surrogate. The per-study values in Table 1 are the standardized representations retained by the pipeline (and used in the Egger/trim-and-fill diagnostics); they are reported for auditability only and are not the headline effect measure.

3. **Documents screened versus comparisons pooled.** Six documents entered the review but only four yielded poolable effect sizes. The shortfall reflects exclusion of overlapping or duplicate reports of trials already represented in the dataset — a deliberate step taken to avoid double-counting the same randomised participants — and exclusion of records whose DVT data could not be reconstructed as events and totals per arm.

4. **Pre-specified subgroup analysis not estimable.** The requested stratification by statin type (rosuvastatin / atorvastatin / pravastatin) was **not performed**, because four effect sizes across three agents leave at least two subgroups with a single trial each. No statin-specific pooled value, confidence interval or heterogeneity statistic is reported, and no between-subgroup test was run. No placeholder or imputed subgroup figure has been substituted.

5. **Small-study-effect risk rated "moderate" despite null diagnostics.** Egger's test (p = 0.9089) and trim-and-fill (k₀ = 1) detected no asymmetry, yet the overall rating is *moderate*. This rating reflects the structural weakness of those tests at k = 4, the restricted search for grey and unpublished literature, and the confounding influence of substantial heterogeneity — not a positive detection of bias.

6. **Rare-event outcome.** DVT event counts in primary-prevention trials are small in absolute terms. Odds ratios derived from sparse 2 × 2 tables are sensitive to zero-cell handling and continuity corrections, which adds a layer of model dependence to both the individual estimates and the pooled summary.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small number of comparisons (k = 4).** This is the overriding limitation. It constrains the precision of the pooled estimate, renders the heterogeneity statistics imprecise, makes Egger's test and trim-and-fill unreliable, and makes the pre-specified subgroup analysis impossible.

2. **Agent-stratified question unanswerable.** The central comparative question — whether rosuvastatin, atorvastatin and pravastatin differ in their effect on DVT — cannot be addressed with the available data, and the class-level estimate reported here should not be attributed to any individual agent.

3. **Substantial unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 67.86% with a significant Q test, driven by one clearly discordant comparison, means the pooled value represents a mean of differing effects rather than a single common effect, and no moderator analysis could identify the source.

4. **Extreme weighting asymmetry.** Two comparisons account for 82.9% of the participants and therefore dominate the summary, while the smallest (587 participants, 1.6%) exerts disproportionate influence on heterogeneity and bias diagnostics. The pooled estimate is, in practical terms, close to a weighted statement about two trials.

5. **Measure mismatch between analysis and source reporting.** The odds-ratio scale used here diverges from the hazard-ratio reporting of the source trials. Although the user specification was authoritative and native OR pooling was used, this mismatch limits direct comparability with the published trial results and with other syntheses that pooled hazard ratios.

6. **Outcome ascertainment heterogeneity.** DVT was not uniformly a pre-specified, prospectively adjudicated endpoint across contributing trials. Variation between adjudicated endpoints and adverse-event-derived counts is a plausible and untestable source of both heterogeneity and selective reporting.

7. **Residual risk of reporting bias.** Null results for non-prespecified safety-derived outcomes are systematically less likely to be published. Funnel-based diagnostics cannot detect this mechanism reliably at k = 4, and the search did not comprehensively interrogate grey literature or registry results.

8. **No risk-of-bias or certainty grading reported.** Formal domain-level risk-of-bias assessment and GRADE certainty rating were not available in the analysis dataset; readers should assume certainty in the pooled estimate is, at best, low, given imprecision, heterogeneity and indirectness of outcome ascertainment.

9. **Participants, not events, as the denominator for scale.** The 36,793 figure describes randomised participants; the number of actual DVT events is far smaller and is the true determinant of statistical power for this outcome. The apparent size of the dataset substantially overstates its informativeness for a rare endpoint.

## 12. Conclusion

In a REML random-effects meta-analysis of four placebo-controlled randomised comparisons comprising 36,793 participants, statin therapy was associated with a pooled **Odds Ratio of 0.664 (95% CI 0.367 to 1.204; p = 0.1775)** for the incidence of deep vein thrombosis. The recorded direction for this result is that it favours **neither arm** — essentially a statistical tie on a ratio measure whose favourability depends on which event was counted — since the confidence interval spans the null. The effect measure is a confirmed native-scale odds ratio (native pooling used), though it was computed on an OR scale specified by the analytic request rather than the hazard-ratio scale used in the source trial reports.

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (Q = 9.992, df = 3, p = 0.0186; I² = 67.86%; τ² = 0.242743) and traceable to a single discordant comparison. Egger's test (p = 0.9089) and trim-and-fill (k₀ = 1) detected no funnel asymmetry, but with only four studies these diagnostics are underpowered, and the overall small-study-effect risk is rated moderate; the finding should be read as "no bias detected," not "bias excluded."

The pre-specified stratification by statin type **could not be performed**: four effect sizes distributed across three agents leave no subgroup with enough independent trials to pool, and no agent-specific estimate is reported.

**Bottom line.** The current randomised evidence neither establishes nor excludes a protective effect of statin therapy on the primary prevention of DVT, and it is wholly insufficient to distinguish between rosuvastatin, atorvastatin and pravastatin. The numerically favourable point estimate justifies continued investigation, not a change in practice. Resolving the question will require randomised trials — or pooled individual-participant-data analyses of existing trials — with DVT as a pre-specified, prospectively adjudicated endpoint, adequate event accrual, and head-to-head or stratified representation of the individual statin agents.
