# Higher (Liberal) versus Lower (Conservative) Oxygenation Targets and All-Cause Mortality in Critically Ill Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Randomised Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Supplemental oxygen is administered to almost every critically ill adult, yet the optimal arterial oxygenation target remains contested. Observational data linking hyperoxaemia to harm have motivated multiple randomised trials comparing higher (liberal) with lower (conservative) oxygen targets, but individual trials have generally been underpowered for mortality.

**Objective.** To determine whether, in adults (≥18 years) admitted to an intensive care unit (ICU) — including after cardiac arrest, with acute respiratory failure or ARDS, with septic shock, or receiving mechanical ventilation — a higher/liberal fraction of inspired oxygen or arterial oxygenation target, compared with a lower/conservative or low-normal target, affects all-cause mortality at the longest follow-up reported.

**Methods.** We systematically identified randomised controlled trials allocating ICU patients to a higher versus a lower oxygenation target and reporting all-cause mortality with an intention-to-treat denominator at the longest available follow-up (180-day, 90-day, or hospital discharge preferred; 28/30-day or ICU mortality used only when nothing later was reported). For factorial trials, all participants assigned to a higher oxygen target were compared with all assigned to a lower target, summed across the other randomised factor. Event counts and randomised denominators were extracted per arm and pooled on the native risk-ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** Nine randomised trials contributing nine independent effect sizes and 5,732 randomised participants were included. The pooled risk ratio (RR) for all-cause mortality with a higher/liberal oxygen target was **1.0017 (95% CI 0.939 to 1.069; p = 0.9590; k = 9; n = 5,732)**. The direction field for this analysis records the result as favouring **neither arm** — it is essentially a statistical tie. Between-study heterogeneity was nil (Q = 8.655, df = 8, p = 0.3722; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), indicating that the nine trials are statistically consistent with a single common effect. At the study level, only one trial (R4, n = 201) reached nominal significance in isolation. Egger's test showed no funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept = 0.0219, p = 0.6854) and trim-and-fill imputed a single hypothetical missing study (k₀ = 1, adjusted effect 0.0262 on the standardized scale), implying no material shift after adjustment; overall publication-bias risk was nonetheless rated **moderate** on non-statistical grounds.

**Conclusions.** Across nine randomised trials in critically ill adults, a higher/liberal oxygenation target conferred neither a mortality benefit nor a detectable mortality harm relative to a lower/conservative target, with a precise null point estimate and no heterogeneity. The 95% confidence interval still permits up to a ~6% relative reduction or a ~7% relative increase in deaths, so clinically modest effects — particularly within specific phenotypes such as post-cardiac-arrest brain injury or ARDS — cannot be excluded and remain the appropriate focus of future trials.

## 1. Introduction

Oxygen is the most frequently administered drug in intensive care. Historically, clinicians have erred towards generous oxygen delivery on the premise that the consequences of hypoxaemia are immediate and irreversible whereas those of hyperoxaemia are subtle. Over the past two decades that premise has been challenged. Hyperoxaemia promotes reactive oxygen species formation, coronary and cerebral vasoconstriction, absorption atelectasis, pulmonary epithelial injury, and attenuation of hypoxic pulmonary vasoconstriction; large observational cohorts — particularly after cardiac arrest — have repeatedly reported U-shaped or monotonically harmful associations between arterial oxygen tension and death. These signals prompted a generation of randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comparing a deliberately higher (liberal) oxygenation target with a lower (conservative, or low-normal) target in mixed ICU populations and in specific syndromes: post-cardiac-arrest care, acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure and ARDS, septic shock, and invasive mechanical ventilation generally.

Individually, these trials have been inconclusive. Some were stopped early for futility or safety, several were explicitly designed as pilot or feasibility studies, and nearly all were powered for physiological or composite endpoints rather than for mortality. The result is a literature in which point estimates scatter on both sides of the null and in which narrative reviews have reached opposing conclusions. The clinical question is consequential and universal: the oxygen target is set, implicitly or explicitly, for every ventilated patient in every ICU.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses a single, pre-specified question: **in adults (≥18 years) admitted to the ICU, does a higher/liberal inspired-oxygen fraction or arterial oxygenation target, compared with a lower/conservative target, affect all-cause mortality at the longest follow-up reported?** By convention adopted throughout this report, a **risk ratio above 1.0 (positive log RR) indicates more deaths in the higher/liberal oxygen arm — that is, harm from liberal oxygen** — and a risk ratio below 1.0 indicates fewer deaths with the higher target.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Reporting standard and protocol

The review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement. The analytic pipeline comprised structured retrieval of candidate records, automated and manual relevance screening against explicit eligibility criteria, de-duplication, exclusion of non-primary publications (narrative reviews, editorials, prior meta-analyses, protocols without results), full-text assessment, and structured extraction of arm-level mortality events and randomised denominators. No separate pre-registered protocol (e.g. PROSPERO) was lodged for this analysis; this is recorded as a limitation and contributes to the moderate publication-bias rating (Section 9).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Adults aged ≥18 years admitted to an intensive care unit, including after cardiac arrest, with acute respiratory failure or ARDS, with septic shock, or receiving invasive mechanical ventilation. Mixed-ICU and syndrome-specific populations were both eligible.

**Intervention.** A higher or liberal oxygenation strategy, operationalised in the primary reports as an elevated SpO₂ target (e.g. >96%, or 96–100%), an elevated PaO₂ target (e.g. >13 kPa / ~100 mmHg), a fixed high FiO₂ (e.g. 0.8–1.0), or deliberate hyperoxia.

**Comparator.** A lower, conservative, or low-normal oxygenation target (e.g. SpO₂ 88–92% or 90–94%, PaO₂ 8–12 kPa, titration to the lowest FiO₂ compatible with adequate saturation).

**Outcome.** All-cause mortality — the number of participants dying from any cause (events) out of the number randomised to each arm (intention-to-treat denominator) — taken at the **longest follow-up for which a death count was reported for all randomised participants**. Where multiple timepoints were available, the latest was used (180-day > 90-day > hospital discharge). A 28- or 30-day count was used only when nothing later was reported, and ICU mortality only when no later all-cause death count existed anywhere in the report.

**Design.** Randomised controlled trials, including factorial designs. In factorial trials, all participants assigned to a higher oxygen target were compared with all assigned to a lower target, summed across the levels of the other randomised factor, preserving the full randomised denominator.

**Exclusions.** Non-randomised and observational designs; paediatric and neonatal populations; perioperative-only or pre-hospital-only interventions without ICU admission; trials comparing oxygen delivery *devices* rather than oxygenation *targets*; trials reporting no all-cause death count on an intention-to-treat denominator; reviews, editorials and duplicate reports of the same randomised cohort.

### 2.3 Screening outcome

After de-duplication and sequential title/abstract and full-text screening, **nine randomised trials met all eligibility criteria and contributed nine independent effect sizes** to the quantitative synthesis, representing **5,732 randomised participants** in total. Where a trial had generated multiple publications (primary report plus long-term follow-up or secondary analyses), a single report was retained per randomised cohort so that no participant contributed more than once to the pooled estimate; this one-effect-size-per-trial structure is reflected in the pipeline summary (9 studies, 9 effect sizes).

Two features of the evidence base are worth noting before interpretation. First, the sample is dominated by a single large trial: study_5_R5 alone contributes 2,888 of 5,732 randomised participants (50.4% of the total enrolment), and the three largest trials (R5, R3, R6) together account for 80.8%. Second, the small trials (R1, R7, R8, with 32, 103 and 120 participants respectively) carry wide confidence intervals that straddle the null by a wide margin and are individually uninformative about mortality; their inclusion improves the representativeness of the synthesis but adds little statistical information.

Risk-of-bias considerations common to this literature deserve explicit mention. Oxygen-target trials cannot blind bedside clinicians or respiratory therapists to allocation, so performance bias is intrinsic; however, all-cause mortality is an objective, hard endpoint with minimal detection-bias susceptibility, and intention-to-treat denominators with near-complete ascertainment of vital status limit attrition bias. Separation between arms — the actual achieved difference in SpO₂/PaO₂ — is the principal threat to internal validity and varied across trials; incomplete separation biases estimates towards the null.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure and scale

The pre-specified effect measure for this review was the **risk ratio (RR)** for all-cause mortality, higher/liberal target versus lower/conservative target. The analysis dataset records `effect_measure = "RR"`, `measure_label = "Risk Ratio (RR)"`, `effect_measure_source = "explicit_user_request"`, `computation_scale = "RR"` and, critically, **`native_pooling_used = true`**. The pooled result reported in Section 5 is therefore a **confirmed native-scale risk ratio**, computed from arm-level event counts and randomised denominators on the log-RR scale and exponentiated for presentation — it is *not* a standardized-metric fallback, and it should be named "pooled risk ratio", never "Hedges' g". No conflict between requested and detected effect measures was recorded (`effect_measure_conflict = null`).

The study-level values in Table 1 are reported in the pipeline as standardized effect sizes (g) with corresponding confidence intervals. These are presented for visual comparability across trials of very different size and for the small-study diagnostics in Section 9; they are a monotone re-expression of the same underlying arm-level mortality contrast and preserve its direction, such that a **negative g corresponds to RR < 1 (fewer deaths with liberal oxygen)** and a **positive g corresponds to RR > 1 (more deaths with liberal oxygen)**. The inferential conclusion of this review rests on the native RR pooling.

### 4.2 Distribution of study-level estimates

The nine standardized estimates range from −0.3326 (R4) to +0.1720 (R2), a span of just over half a standard-deviation unit, with five estimates negative and four positive. The distribution is tightly clustered around zero: the four largest trials (R5, R3, R6, R9), which together contribute 88.4% of participants, have estimates of −0.0118, +0.0269, −0.0400 and +0.1439 respectively — all within ±0.15 of the null. Eight of the nine 95% confidence intervals include zero. The sole exception is **study_4_R4 (g = −0.3326, 95% CI −0.611 to −0.054, n = 201)**, which in isolation suggests *fewer* deaths in the higher-oxygen arm; this is a modest-sized trial whose nominally significant result sits at the extreme of the observed distribution and is not corroborated by the larger trials.

The spread of study-level estimates and their precision-weighted alignment are displayed in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png` (distribution of effect sizes) and `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png` (effect size against study size, bubble area proportional to weight), the latter showing the characteristic funnel-shaped narrowing of estimates towards the null as sample size increases.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects meta-analysis with REML estimation of the between-study variance was fitted to the nine log risk ratios.

**Table 2. Pooled result — all-cause mortality at longest reported follow-up**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | Risk Ratio (RR) — native pooling confirmed |
| Trials (k) | 9 |
| Randomised participants (n) | 5,732 |
| **Pooled RR (higher vs lower oxygen target)** | **1.0017** |
| 95% confidence interval | **0.939 to 1.069** |
| p-value | **0.9590** |
| Favours | **Neither arm** (essentially a tie) |
| Model / estimator | Random effects, REML |
| τ² | 0.0 |
| I² | 0.0% |
| Cochran's Q (df = 8) | 8.655 (p = 0.3722) |

The pooled risk ratio of **1.0017** is almost exactly unity: a 0.17% relative excess of deaths in the higher/liberal oxygen arm, which is indistinguishable from no difference (p = 0.9590). The `favors` field for this analysis records the result as favouring **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**, and that is how the finding should be stated — this meta-analysis does not favour liberal oxygen, nor does it favour conservative oxygen.

Because only one meta-analytic group is defined in the analysis (all nine trials contribute to the single outcome "all-cause mortality at longest reported follow-up"), no separate subgroup pooling by outcome or timepoint is required or reported. All nine trials are pooled together on one common outcome definition.

**Interpretation of the interval.** The 95% confidence interval (0.939 to 1.069) is narrow by the standards of critical-care mortality meta-analyses and is the substantive result. It excludes effects larger than a **6.1% relative reduction** or a **6.9% relative increase** in the risk of death. Against a representative ICU baseline mortality of approximately 35% (typical of the mixed-ICU and post-arrest populations represented here), these bounds correspond to an absolute risk difference of roughly **2.1 fewer to 2.4 more deaths per 100 patients** treated to a higher rather than a lower oxygen target. Large effects in either direction — of the magnitude implied by earlier observational hyperoxia literature, or by the early-stopped signal in some pilot trials — are confidently excluded. Small but clinically non-trivial effects are not.

The forest plot of individual and pooled estimates is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`. A cumulative meta-analysis, ordered by accrual of evidence, is shown at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`; the cumulative estimate converges rapidly on the null once the larger trials enter, and the confidence band narrows without the point estimate crossing meaningfully away from 1.0 — a pattern consistent with a genuinely null effect rather than with an effect obscured by imprecision.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **absent**:

- **Cochran's Q = 8.655 on 8 degrees of freedom, p = 0.3722** — no evidence of excess variation beyond sampling error (the observed Q is essentially equal to its null expectation of df = 8).
- **I² = 0.0%** — none of the observed variability in study-level effects is attributable to true between-study differences.
- **τ² = 0.0** — the REML estimate of between-study variance is zero, so the random-effects model collapses numerically to a fixed-effect (common-effect) model; the reported pooled RR and interval are consequently not inflated by any heterogeneity allowance.

This is a notable and reassuring finding given the clinical diversity of the included populations — post-cardiac-arrest, ARDS/acute hypoxaemic respiratory failure, septic shock, and general mechanical ventilation — and the heterogeneity of the intervention specifications (SpO₂ bands, PaO₂ bands, fixed high FiO₂). Trials with such varied case-mix and target definitions nonetheless produced mortality estimates mutually consistent with a single common value near 1.0. A graphical summary of the heterogeneity diagnostics is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

Two cautions temper this interpretation. First, with k = 9 and several very small trials, Cochran's Q has limited power to detect moderate heterogeneity, and I² estimated at exactly 0% is a boundary estimate whose confidence interval would extend appreciably above zero. Second, statistical homogeneity of *overall* mortality effects does not exclude clinically important *qualitative* heterogeneity within subpopulations — for example, opposing effects in post-arrest hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury versus hypoxaemic respiratory failure could partially cancel in a pooled analysis. Homogeneity here is best read as "no evidence of inconsistency", not as proof of uniformity of effect across all critically ill phenotypes.

## 7. Moderators

No moderator (meta-regression or subgroup) analyses were pre-specified or fitted in this synthesis, and none can be supported by the available data for three reasons.

1. **No residual variance to explain.** With τ² = 0.0 and I² = 0.0%, there is no between-study heterogeneity for covariates to account for. Any meta-regression fitted to these data would, by construction, have no systematic signal to detect; apparent moderator effects would be artefacts of sampling noise.
2. **Insufficient degrees of freedom.** With k = 9, conventional guidance (≥10 studies per covariate) is not met even for a single moderator. Clinically desirable moderators — admission category (post-cardiac-arrest vs ARDS/respiratory failure vs septic shock vs general ventilated), magnitude of achieved SpO₂/PaO₂ separation between arms, target modality (SpO₂ vs PaO₂ vs fixed FiO₂), follow-up horizon (180-day vs 90-day vs hospital vs 28-day vs ICU), and baseline control-arm mortality — each define subgroups of two to four trials. Such analyses would be exploratory at best and are at high risk of ecological and aggregation bias.
3. **Dominance by a single trial.** Any subgroup containing study_5_R5 would effectively report that trial's result, while any subgroup excluding it would rest on ≤2,844 participants.

The one descriptive pattern worth recording, visible in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, is a size–estimate gradient: the smallest trials produce the most extreme estimates in both directions (R1 at g = −0.2788; R4 at g = −0.3326; R2 at g = +0.1720), while the four largest cluster within ±0.15 of zero. This is the expected signature of sampling variability rather than of a true moderating effect of trial size, and it is consistent with the null small-study diagnostics reported below. **A single timepoint harmonisation decision also functioned as an implicit design moderator:** because the protocol required the longest available all-cause death count, trials contribute at different horizons (180-day, 90-day, hospital discharge, or — only where nothing later existed — 28/30-day or ICU mortality). Longer horizons generally attenuate RRs towards the null as competing causes of death accumulate; the absence of heterogeneity suggests this mixing did not materially distort the pooled estimate, but it cannot be formally tested here.

## 8. Publication Bias

Small-study effects were assessed with two complementary diagnostics applied to the nine study-level estimates.

- **Egger's regression test for funnel-plot asymmetry:** intercept = **0.0219**, **p = 0.6854**. The intercept is near zero and the p-value is far from any conventional significance threshold, providing **no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry**.
- **Duval & Tweedie trim-and-fill:** **k₀ = 1** hypothetical missing study imputed, with an adjusted effect of **0.0262** on the standardized scale. Imputing a single study implies that the pooled estimate would **not shift materially** after adjustment for any putative suppression.

Taken together, these diagnostics offer **no statistical signal of publication bias or small-study effects**. The visual impression from the bubble/funnel display (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) is concordant: small trials scatter symmetrically on both sides of the pooled estimate, with roughly as many negative (R1, R4, R7) as positive (R2, R8) small-trial results.

Nonetheless, the overall publication-bias risk for this review is rated **moderate**. That rating is not driven by the statistics above, which are reassuring, but by factors they cannot capture:

- **Low power of Egger's test with k = 9.** The test is widely regarded as unreliable with fewer than approximately ten studies; a non-significant result here has limited evidential value against the presence of bias.
- **Absence of a pre-registered protocol** for this review, which permits (even if it did not occur) post-hoc flexibility in eligibility and outcome-timepoint selection.
- **Potential restriction to published and English-language reports**, with no systematic grey-literature, trial-registry or unpublished-data searching documented; null oxygen-target trials, particularly small pilots stopped for futility, may remain unpublished or reported only in abstract form.

The appropriate formulation is therefore: **no detectable publication bias, but publication bias cannot be excluded.** This should not be read as positive evidence of its absence. One mitigating consideration specific to this literature is that the pooled result is itself null; publication bias classically inflates effect estimates away from the null, so its presence would, if anything, make the true effect even closer to unity rather than mask a real mortality difference.

## 9. Discussion

This meta-analysis of nine randomised trials and 5,732 critically ill adults found **no effect of a higher/liberal oxygenation target on all-cause mortality** relative to a lower/conservative target (pooled RR 1.0017, 95% CI 0.939–1.069, p = 0.9590), with complete statistical homogeneity across trials (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0, Q = 8.655, df = 8, p = 0.3722). The result favours neither arm.

Three features make this a relatively robust null. First, the point estimate is not merely non-significant but *numerically indistinguishable from unity* — it does not sit at an appreciable effect size with a wide interval, which would signal an underpowered study rather than a true null. Second, heterogeneity is zero, so the nine trials are not telling conflicting stories that have been averaged into a null; they independently agree. Third, the cumulative analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows convergence onto the null with progressive narrowing of the interval, the expected trajectory when evidence accumulates around a true value of 1.0.

These findings reconcile an apparent conflict between observational and randomised evidence. Cohort studies have consistently associated hyperoxaemia — especially after cardiac arrest — with increased mortality, and that association has been mechanistically plausible. The randomised evidence synthesised here does not reproduce it. The most likely explanations are confounding by indication in the observational literature (the sickest patients, with the highest FiO₂ requirements and most severe neurological injury, are the most likely to be exposed to extreme oxygen tensions), and the fact that pragmatic target-based trials compare moderately different oxygenation bands rather than extreme hyperoxia against normoxia. Achieved separation between arms is frequently narrower than protocol-specified, and incomplete separation biases randomised estimates towards the null — a mechanism that could, in principle, conceal a real effect of *extreme* hyperoxia that no included trial actually tested.

Clinically, the implication is that within the ranges of oxygenation targeted in contemporary ICU practice, the choice between a liberal and a conservative target is unlikely to change a patient's probability of dying. This supports a pragmatic position: neither aggressive pursuit of high-normal oxygen tensions nor anxious restriction to the lower bound is mandated by mortality evidence, and the target can reasonably be individualised to physiological considerations (cerebral oxygenation after arrest, ventilator-induced lung injury mitigation in ARDS, oxygen-supply logistics) and to secondary endpoints — ventilator-free days, organ dysfunction, neurological outcome, oxygen resource consumption — on which these trials have more to say than on survival. It also means that the substantial practice variation in oxygen targeting across ICUs is unlikely to be a major driver of differences in mortality.

Importantly, a null pooled estimate is not equivalent to equivalence. The confidence interval still admits up to a ~6% relative mortality reduction or a ~7% relative increase, which at typical ICU baseline risk translates to roughly two deaths per hundred patients in either direction — a magnitude most clinicians and patients would consider meaningful. Definitively excluding effects of that size would require several thousand further randomised patients. The highest-value future trials are therefore likely to be phenotype-specific (post-cardiac-arrest hypoxic-ischaemic brain injury; moderate-to-severe ARDS; septic shock with impaired oxygen extraction), to enforce and document genuine separation between arms, to report long-horizon (180-day or one-year) all-cause mortality alongside functional and neurocognitive outcomes, and ideally to pool individual participant data so that interaction with baseline severity and achieved oxygenation can be tested directly rather than ecologically.

## 10. Limitations

1. **Small number of trials.** With k = 9, the synthesis has limited power to detect heterogeneity (Cochran's Q), limited power to detect funnel asymmetry (Egger's test is unreliable below ~10 studies), and no capacity for credible meta-regression. The I² = 0.0% and τ² = 0.0 estimates are boundary values whose uncertainty intervals extend above zero.
2. **Dominance by one trial.** study_5_R5 contributes 2,888 of 5,732 participants (50.4%) and the three largest trials contribute 80.8%; the pooled result is substantially a restatement of the large-trial evidence, and a formal leave-one-out analysis excluding R5 would rest on a markedly smaller and less precise evidence base.
3. **Heterogeneous outcome timepoints.** Although all trials contributed all-cause mortality on an intention-to-treat denominator, the horizon differs by protocol design (longest available: 180-day, 90-day, hospital discharge; 28/30-day or ICU mortality only where nothing later existed). Mixing horizons can attenuate or distort a pooled ratio measure, and the harmonisation rule, while pre-specified and transparent, is a source of non-statistical heterogeneity that the zero I² cannot rule out.
4. **Heterogeneous interventions.** "Higher/liberal" subsumes SpO₂ >96%, PaO₂ >13 kPa, fixed high FiO₂ and deliberate hyperoxia; "lower/conservative" subsumes a similarly broad family of low-normal bands. Achieved separation between arms was variable and generally smaller than specified, biasing estimates towards the null.
5. **Clinical diversity of populations.** Post-cardiac-arrest, ARDS/respiratory failure, septic shock and general ventilated patients were pooled. Opposing true effects across these phenotypes could cancel within a homogeneous-looking pooled estimate; subgroup analysis was not feasible with k = 9.
6. **Factorial trials.** Where factorial designs were included, participants were summed across the second randomised factor as specified. This preserves the randomised comparison for oxygen but assumes no interaction between oxygen target and the co-intervention — an assumption that is conventional but untested here.
7. **Unblinded intervention.** Oxygen-target allocation cannot be concealed at the bedside, introducing unavoidable performance bias and potential differential co-intervention, though the objectivity of all-cause mortality limits detection bias.
8. **Publication-bias risk rated moderate.** No pre-registered protocol, no documented grey-literature or registry searching, and possible language restriction mean suppression of null or unfavourable small trials cannot be excluded despite reassuring Egger (p = 0.6854) and trim-and-fill (k₀ = 1) statistics.
9. **Standardized study-level display.** Individual trial estimates are presented on a standardized scale for comparability. The pooled inference is on the confirmed native risk-ratio scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), but readers should not interpret the per-study g values as native risk ratios.
10. **No formal certainty-of-evidence grading.** A GRADE assessment was not performed. Informally, the evidence for no mortality difference would likely be rated moderate certainty — downgraded for imprecision at the interval boundaries and for indirectness arising from pooled populations and variable achieved separation, but not downgraded for inconsistency.

---

## 11. Conclusion

Across nine randomised controlled trials enrolling 5,732 critically ill adults — after cardiac arrest, with acute respiratory failure or ARDS, with septic shock, or receiving mechanical ventilation — targeting a **higher (liberal) oxygenation level produced neither more nor fewer deaths than targeting a lower (conservative) level**. The pooled risk ratio for all-cause mortality at the longest reported follow-up was **1.0017 (95% CI 0.939 to 1.069; p = 0.9590; k = 9; n = 5,732)**, favouring **neither arm**, with no detectable between-study heterogeneity (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0; Q = 8.655, df = 8, p = 0.3722) and no statistical evidence of publication bias (Egger's p = 0.6854; trim-and-fill k₀ = 1), albeit with an overall bias risk rated moderate because small-study diagnostics are underpowered at k = 9 and grey literature was not systematically searched.

The randomised evidence therefore does not support the strong harm signal attributed to hyperoxaemia in observational cohorts, nor does it support liberal oxygenation as beneficial. Within the oxygenation ranges actually tested, the choice of target appears to be mortality-neutral and may reasonably be guided by physiology, secondary outcomes and resource considerations. Because the confidence interval still permits roughly a 6% relative reduction to a 7% relative increase in deaths — about two deaths per hundred patients in either direction — clinically modest effects remain possible, and resolving them will require larger, phenotype-specific trials with verified separation between arms, long-horizon all-cause mortality, and individual-participant-data synthesis.
