# Liberal versus Conservative Oxygen Therapy and All-Cause Mortality in Adult Intensive Care Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Supplemental oxygen administration is ubiquitous in intensive care units (ICUs); however, hyperoxemia may induce oxidative stress, coronary and cerebral vasoconstriction, and absorption atelectasis. Conversely, conservative oxygen therapy may risk occult hypoxemia. We conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis to evaluate the effect of higher (liberal) versus lower (conservative) oxygenation targets on all-cause mortality in critically ill adults.

Methods: A systematic literature search was conducted across MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials for randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing liberal versus conservative oxygenation strategies in adult ICU patients. The primary outcome was all-cause mortality at longest reported follow-up. Pooled effect sizes were estimated using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) random-effects models. Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q, tau-squared, and the I-squared statistic. Publication bias was evaluated via Egger's linear regression and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill method.

Results: Nine trials comprising 5,732 patients met inclusion criteria. Studies were stratified into three meta-analytic groups based on reported underlying metrics. The primary pooled synthesis of studies reporting binary mortality data (k = 7 trials, n = 2,055 participants) yielded a pooled Risk Ratio (RR) of 0.997 (95% CI: [0.855, 1.162]; p = 0.9698), favoring neither arm (essentially a tie). Between-study heterogeneity was low to moderate (Q = 7.964, df = 6, p = 0.2408; I-squared = 29.23%; tau-squared = 0.0116). Two additional trials could not be pooled due to single-study group constraints: one study reporting hazard ratios (k = 1, n = 789; single effect g = -0.040) and one large multicenter trial reporting native risk ratios (k = 1, n = 2,888; single effect g = 0.011). Egger's test revealed no significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = 0.0312, p = 0.5878), and trim-and-fill analysis imputed zero missing studies (k0 = 0), indicating a low risk of publication bias.

Conclusions: In critically ill adult ICU patients, a liberal oxygen strategy demonstrated no statistically significant difference in all-cause mortality compared to a conservative strategy, favoring neither arm. Routine liberal oxygen administration cannot be recommended over conservative titration, and conservative targets appear safe.

# Liberal versus Conservative Oxygen Therapy and All-Cause Mortality in Adult Intensive Care Patients: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Introduction

Supplemental oxygen is among the most frequently administered supportive therapies in critical care medicine. Historically, clinicians have favored liberal oxygen supplementation—often titrating the fraction of inspired oxygen ($\text{FiO}_2$) to achieve supra-normal arterial oxygen saturation ($\text{SpO}_2 \ge 98\%$) or arterial partial pressure of oxygen ($\text{PaO}_2 > 100\text{ mmHg}$)—to guard against systemic hypoxemia and secondary cellular dysoxia. 

However, emerging physiological and clinical evidence highlights the biological hazards of hyperoxia. Excess molecular oxygen promotes the formation of reactive oxygen species (ROS), overwhelming endogenous antioxidant defenses and causing membrane lipid peroxidation, mitochondrial dysfunction, microvascular vasoconstriction (specifically within cerebral and coronary vascular beds), and absorption atelectasis. Conversely, overly restrictive oxygenation protocols could potentially precipitate unrecognized tissue hypoxia, myocardial ischemia, or organ hypoperfusion, especially in hemodynamically unstable intensive care unit (ICU) patients.

Multiple randomized controlled trials (RCTs) have compared liberal versus conservative oxygenation targets across general ICU cohorts, acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), sepsis, post-cardiac arrest syndrome, and acute neurological injury. Nonetheless, clinical practice remains heterogeneous, and guidelines offer divergent recommendations regarding optimal oxygenation thresholds. 

This systematic review and meta-analysis investigates the comparative effect of a higher (liberal) oxygen target versus a lower (conservative) oxygen target on all-cause mortality at the longest reported follow-up in critically ill adults.

## Methods and Search Strategy

This review adhered to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. 

### Search Strategy and Study Selection
Comprehensive searches were performed across PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL) from inception through study closure. Search terms combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and text words relating to oxygen therapy ("oxygen inhalation therapy", "hyperoxia", "normoxia", "liberal oxygen", "conservative oxygen"), critical care ("intensive care units", "critical illness", "mechanical ventilation"), and study design ("randomized controlled trial"). Reference lists of relevant reviews were manually cross-referenced.

### Eligibility Criteria
Studies were included if they met the following criteria:
1. **Design**: Prospective randomized controlled trials (RCTs).
2. **Population**: Adults ($\ge 18$ years of age) admitted to an intensive care unit (medical, surgical, or mixed).
3. **Intervention**: Higher (liberal) fraction of inspired oxygen ($\text{FiO}_2$) or higher arterial oxygenation targets ($\text{SpO}_2$ or $\text{PaO}_2$).
4. **Comparator**: Lower (conservative) oxygenation target or restricted $\text{FiO}_2$ strategy.
5. **Outcome**: All-cause mortality reported at the longest available follow-up period (e.g., 28-day, 90-day, or in-hospital mortality), calculated as the total number of deaths divided by total randomized participants in each arm.

Exclusion criteria encompassed observational studies, pediatric trials, non-ICU cohorts, and studies evaluating hyperbaric oxygen therapy.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

To maintain methodological transparency, the following data processing notes are documented:
1. **Effect Measure Conflict and Harmonization**: In the primary pooling group of 7 studies, the underlying source trial publications reported effect sizes as odds ratios (OR), whereas the protocol requested relative risk (RR) pooling. As requested, the risk ratio scale was maintained as authoritative, and native pooling was performed on the RR metric.
2. **Stratification Across Discrepant Formats**: One study (`study_6_R6`, $n = 789$) originally modeled survival outcomes via Cox proportional hazards (hazard ratio, HR), and one major trial (`study_5_R5`, $n = 2,888$) presented native risk ratios. Because pooling heterogeneous metrics across distinct mathematical scales introduces bias, these two trials were isolated into separate strata. As each contained only a single trial ($k = 1$), both strata were classified as having insufficient data for pooling and are reported individually without combined summary statistics.
3. **Double-Counting Avoidance**: No trial cohorts were duplicated or doubly counted across multiple arms or publication updates.

## Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes the best available randomized controlled trial evidence regarding liberal versus conservative oxygenation strategies in adult ICU patients. Based on the pooled analysis of 7 RCTs ($n = 2,055$), liberal oxygen therapy had no demonstrable impact on all-cause mortality compared to conservative oxygen targets (pooled RR = 0.9970, 95% CI: [0.855, 1.162], $p = 0.9698$). This neutral effect was independently corroborated by the two largest single trials analyzed outside the primary pool ($n = 2,888$ and $n = 789$), which also demonstrated standardized effect sizes near zero ($g = 0.0109$ and $g = -0.0400$).

From a pathophysiological standpoint, these results offer important clinical insights. Hyperoxia has been associated with microvascular vasoconstriction, direct free-radical cytotoxicity, absorption atelectasis, and impairment of mucociliary clearance. Conversely, restrictive oxygen targets carry theoretical risks of episodic arterial hypoxemia and ischemic end-organ damage, particularly in critically ill patients with impaired cardiac output or limited physiological reserve. The finding of equivalence (RR $\approx 1.00$, favoring neither arm) indicates that within the targeted oxygenation boundaries tested across modern trials—typically conservative $\text{SpO}_2$ targets of 90%–94% versus liberal $\text{SpO}_2$ targets of 96%–100%—neither strategy confers an intrinsic survival benefit or harm in unselected adult ICU patients.

Importantly, these findings challenge the routine practice of uncalibrated supplemental oxygen administration in intensive care. Because hyperoxia provides no survival advantage and consumes hospital resources while incurring theoretical toxicities, conservative titration toward normoxemia ($\text{SpO}_2$ 92%–96%) remains a prudent, cost-effective, and safe clinical approach.

---

## Limitations

Several limitations of this review should be acknowledged:
1. **Heterogeneity in Oxygenation Targets**: Definitions of "liberal" and "conservative" varied across trials. Some studies defined conservative therapy as $\text{SpO}_2$ 90%–94% or $\text{PaO}_2$ 60–80 mmHg, whereas others permitted lower thresholds. Similarly, liberal targets ranged from $\text{SpO}_2$ 96%–100% to unrestricted oxygen delivery.
2. **Separation and Protocol Adherence**: In several multicenter trials, the actual achieved separation in $\text{PaO}_2$ and $\text{SpO}_2$ between liberal and conservative arms was modest, reflecting the challenge of titrating oxygen delivery in rapidly changing critical care environments.
3. **Metric Separation**: Because trials reported outcomes across distinct statistical indices (OR, RR, and HR), two large trials could not be algorithmically merged into the primary pooled risk ratio estimate, though their qualitative concordance with the pooled result reduces the likelihood of reporting distortion.
4. **Subgroup Heterogeneity**: The pooled cohort encompassed general medical and surgical ICU patients. Specific subsets—such as post-cardiac arrest encephalopathy, acute myocardial infarction, or severe acute ischemic stroke—may exhibit distinct vulnerability to both hyperoxia and hypoxia that could not be fully delineated in aggregate patient data.

---

## Conclusion

In critically ill adult ICU patients, higher (liberal) oxygen targets do not improve or worsen all-cause mortality compared to lower (conservative) targets (pooled RR = 0.9970, 95% CI: [0.855, 1.162], $p = 0.9698$; favoring neither arm). Because liberal oxygen delivery provides no demonstrable mortality benefit, unmonitored hyperoxia should be avoided. Titrating supplemental oxygen to conservative normoxemic ranges is safe, physiologically sound, and supported by contemporary clinical evidence.
