# Higher Versus Lower Oxygen Targets in Critically Ill Adults: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis of All-Cause Mortality

## Abstract

**Background:** Excess oxygen exposure may cause harm, whereas overly restrictive oxygenation may increase hypoxaemic risk. We evaluated all-cause mortality after assignment to a higher (liberal) versus lower (conservative or low-normal) inspired-oxygen fraction or arterial oxygenation target among adults admitted to intensive care.

**Methods:** Randomised comparisons in critically ill adults were eligible, including patients after cardiac arrest, with acute respiratory failure or acute respiratory distress syndrome, with septic shock, or receiving mechanical ventilation. The prespecified outcome was all-cause mortality at the longest follow-up for which a death count was reported, using intention-to-treat denominators. Factorial-trial cells were to be combined across the other randomised factor. Risk ratios (RRs) were pooled with a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Heterogeneity and small-study effects were assessed using Cochran’s Q, I², τ², Egger’s test, and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Nine studies contributed nine effect estimates. Eight studies (4,999 participants) were included in the RR meta-analysis; one study (789 participants) formed a separate, single-study group because its source report explicitly presented a hazard ratio, despite the analysis pipeline retaining RR as the requested measure. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.995608629552019 (95% CI 0.930 to 1.066; p=0.899111573330355), favoring neither strategy. Heterogeneity was absent (I²=0.0%; τ²=0.0; Q=7.976, p=0.3347). The single-study group was insufficient for pooling. Egger’s intercept was 0.0303 (p=0.6048), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀=0); the assessed publication-bias risk was low.

**Conclusions:** The available randomised evidence does not show that higher rather than lower oxygen targets materially alter all-cause mortality. The confidence interval remains compatible with a modest benefit or harm. Interpretation is limited by unavailable search, screening, risk-of-bias, intervention, and citation details and by separation of one time-to-event estimate from the count-based RR synthesis.

## Introduction

Oxygen is among the most frequently administered treatments in intensive care. Although correction of hypoxaemia is essential, higher inspired-oxygen fractions and arterial oxygenation targets can increase exposure to hyperoxaemia, oxidative injury, vasoconstriction, and pulmonary toxicity. Conversely, conservative oxygen strategies could expose patients to unrecognised or prolonged hypoxaemia. These competing mechanisms make mortality evidence from randomised trials clinically important.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among adults aged 18 years or older admitted to an intensive care unit—including patients after cardiac arrest, with acute respiratory failure or acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS), with septic shock, or receiving mechanical ventilation—what is the effect of assignment to a higher (liberal) inspired-oxygen fraction or arterial oxygenation target, compared with a lower (conservative or low-normal) target, on all-cause mortality at the longest follow-up reported?

The direction of interpretation was prespecified: an RR greater than 1 indicates more deaths with the higher/liberal target and therefore favors the lower/conservative target; an RR less than 1 indicates fewer deaths with the higher/liberal target. An RR of 1 is the null.

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies were randomised comparisons enrolling adults aged at least 18 years in an intensive-care setting and assigning participants to higher versus lower oxygen exposure, defined through inspired-oxygen fraction or an arterial oxygenation target. Relevant populations included, but were not restricted to, patients after cardiac arrest, those with acute respiratory failure or ARDS, those with septic shock, and patients receiving mechanical ventilation.

The outcome was death from any cause. For each trial, the intended extraction rule was the number of participants who died divided by the number randomised in each oxygen-target arm, using the longest follow-up at which a death count was reported. A later all-cause mortality measure was preferred to ICU mortality; 28- or 30-day mortality was to be used only when no later count was available. Intention-to-treat denominators were required even when outcome ascertainment was incomplete. For factorial trials, all cells assigned to the higher oxygen target were to be combined and compared with all cells assigned to the lower target, summing across levels of the other randomised factor.

### Search and selection process

The supplied results contained 9 studies and 9 effect estimates. Eight studies entered the count-based RR synthesis, and one study was kept as a separate single-study outcome-measure group. Database names, search dates, complete search strings, deduplication counts, numbers screened at title/abstract and full-text stages, reasons for full-text exclusion, protocol registration, and reviewer procedures were not supplied. Consequently, a numerical PRISMA flow diagram and a reproducible search strategy cannot be reported from the available material. No study beyond the nine supplied records was added or inferred.

## Meta-Analytic Results

### Primary all-cause mortality group

Eight studies involving 4,999 participants were synthesised using a random-effects model with restricted maximum-likelihood estimation. The pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)** was **0.995608629552019** (95% CI **0.930 to 1.066**; p=**0.899111573330355**). The supplied favorability classification was **neither strategy (tie or undetermined)**.

Thus, the point estimate was essentially null. The confidence interval includes both a possible relative reduction and a possible relative increase in mortality with a higher oxygen target. Specifically, on the RR scale, it remains compatible with approximately a 7.0% relative reduction through a 6.6% relative increase; this range should not be interpreted as demonstrating equivalence because no equivalence margin was prespecified.

A cumulative meta-analysis plot was generated at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`. Because the ordering variable and sequential estimates were not provided numerically, no additional claims about temporal or sample-size stability are made from that plot alone.

### Separate single-study group

The second all-cause mortality group contained one study and 789 participants. It was marked **insufficient_data**, so no pooled Risk Ratio, pooled confidence interval, or heterogeneity statistic was estimated. The source explicitly reported an HR, whereas the requested and retained analysis label was RR. Presenting this record separately avoids combining non-identical effect measures and prevents double-counting.

## Heterogeneity

For the eight-study RR meta-analysis, Cochran’s Q was **7.976** with p=**0.3347**. Estimated inconsistency was **I²=0.0%**, and the between-study variance was **τ²=0.0**. These results provide no statistical evidence of between-study heterogeneity in the pooled effects.

However, I²=0.0% does not establish clinical homogeneity. The eligible population spans several critical-care syndromes, and the exact oxygen targets, achieved separation, treatment duration, and mortality follow-up were not supplied. With only eight pooled studies, heterogeneity tests also have limited power to detect small or moderate dispersion. A heterogeneity summary figure is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## Moderators

No prespecified or data-supported subgroup analysis or meta-regression was provided. Accordingly, treatment effects could not be compared by diagnosis, baseline severity, invasive ventilation status, post-cardiac-arrest status, oxygen-target contrast, achieved oxygenation, duration of intervention, follow-up time, trial size, or risk of bias.

A bubble chart was generated at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, but the moderator variable, regression coefficient, uncertainty, and test result were not supplied. The chart is therefore treated as descriptive only and does not support an inferential moderator conclusion.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **0.0303** with p=**0.6048**. The trim-and-fill procedure estimated **k₀=0** missing studies and returned an adjusted analysis-scale summary of **0.0099**. On the supplied assessment, publication-bias risk was classified as **low**.

These diagnostics did not identify small-study asymmetry, but they cannot exclude selective publication or selective outcome reporting. In particular, asymmetry tests have low power when based on only eight pooled studies, and trim-and-fill depends on assumptions that may not hold when study populations and oxygen protocols differ.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

In this synthesis of randomised oxygen-target comparisons among critically ill adults, eight studies with 4,999 participants yielded a pooled RR of 0.995608629552019 (95% CI 0.930 to 1.066). The estimate is almost exactly null, was not statistically significant (p=0.899111573330355), and was classified as favoring neither higher nor lower oxygen targets. Statistical heterogeneity was not detected. A ninth study with 789 participants could not contribute to the same pooled estimate because its source explicitly presented a time-to-event effect measure.

### Clinical interpretation

The findings provide no evidence that assignment to a higher/liberal oxygen target changes all-cause mortality compared with a lower/conservative or low-normal target. Nevertheless, absence of evidence for a difference is not proof of equivalence. The 95% CI permits a modest benefit or harm from the higher-target strategy. Absolute effects cannot be estimated from the supplied summary because arm-level death counts and an applicable baseline mortality risk were unavailable.

The result should also be interpreted as an average across eligible intensive-care populations and intervention definitions. Similar relative effects at the aggregate level do not establish that all subgroups respond identically. Clinically important differences could still exist in patients with profound hypoxaemia, after cardiac arrest, with ARDS, or with septic shock, or according to the magnitude and duration of oxygen-target separation.

### Relation to statistical consistency

The lack of observed heterogeneity strengthens the conclusion that the available study estimates were statistically compatible with a common near-null effect. It does not resolve potential differences in trial conduct, target adherence, crossover, rescue oxygen use, or follow-up. Nor does it rule out heterogeneity that the limited number of studies could not detect.

### Implications for research

Future syntheses should obtain arm-level death counts and randomised denominators directly from trial reports, document the exact longest mortality time point, and distinguish count-based RRs from time-to-event HRs. Individual-participant or harmonised trial-level analyses could evaluate effect modification by baseline oxygenation, diagnosis, ventilation status, severity, and achieved exposure. Trials should consistently report mortality at common time points, adherence to assigned targets, hypoxaemic and hyperoxaemic exposure, and adverse events.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

- The evidence set contained **9 studies and 9 effect estimates**, but only **8 studies (4,999 participants)** were pooled in the primary RR analysis.
- One record, **study_6_R6 (789 participants)**, was assigned to a separate outcome-measure group. Its paper reportedly presented an **HR**, while the user-requested analysis retained **Risk Ratio (RR)** as authoritative. Because this group had only one study, it was explicitly marked insufficient for pooling.
- The supplied study-level field name referred to a generic standardised-effect output, but the meta-analysis metadata confirmed native **RR** pooling for the primary group. This report therefore labels the pooled statistic as a Risk Ratio and does not describe it as Hedges’ g.
- No arm-level deaths, randomised denominators, mortality time points, or factorial-cell data were supplied for independent verification of the extraction rules.
- No bibliographic metadata were supplied, so review articles, duplicate reports, overlapping trial publications, and eligibility decisions could not be independently checked. The separate treatment of the one HR record avoids combining it with the count-based RR synthesis but does not independently verify whether it is a unique trial population.

## Limitations

First, the search and selection process was incompletely documented. Without databases, dates, strategies, screening counts, excluded full texts, or a protocol, search completeness and adherence to a prespecified review process cannot be evaluated fully.

Second, study identities and trial characteristics were unavailable. The clinical populations, oxygen protocols, achieved target separation, intervention durations, follow-up times, and cointerventions could not be described or examined as effect modifiers.

Third, arm-level event counts and intention-to-treat denominators were not provided. The analysis could therefore not independently verify the mortality calculations, the choice of longest follow-up, handling of missing mortality data, or combination of factorial cells.

Fourth, one study was associated with an explicit HR-versus-RR measure conflict. Keeping it separate was statistically appropriate given the supplied grouping, but it reduced the count-based evidence available for pooling and prevented a unified synthesis of all nine records.

Fifth, no risk-of-bias assessment was supplied. Potential bias from randomisation, deviations from intervention, missing outcomes, outcome measurement, or selective reporting therefore cannot be incorporated into certainty judgments. A formal GRADE assessment is not possible from the available information.

Sixth, publication-bias analyses were based on only eight pooled studies. The non-significant Egger test and k₀=0 trim-and-fill result are reassuring but underpowered and assumption-dependent.

Finally, the aggregate RR cannot determine whether oxygen targets affect particular patient subgroups, nor can it quantify absolute mortality differences without a baseline risk.

## Conclusion

Across eight randomised comparisons involving 4,999 critically ill adults, assignment to a higher/liberal rather than lower/conservative oxygen target was not associated with a detectable difference in all-cause mortality at the longest reported follow-up: pooled **Risk Ratio (RR) 0.995608629552019** (95% CI **0.930 to 1.066**; p=**0.899111573330355**). Statistical heterogeneity was absent (I²=0.0%; τ²=0.0), and the available diagnostics suggested low publication-bias risk. One additional 789-participant study was insufficient for separate pooling because of an HR-versus-RR measure conflict. Overall, the evidence favors neither oxygen strategy and remains compatible with modest benefit or harm; stronger conclusions require fully documented trial-level mortality data, risk-of-bias assessment, and harmonised outcome measures.
