# Intranasal Corticosteroids for Persistent Middle Ear Effusion in Children with Otitis Media with Effusion: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Short-Term (≤6 Weeks) Outcomes

## Abstract

**Background.** Otitis media with effusion (OME) is the commonest cause of acquired hearing loss in childhood. Intranasal corticosteroids are widely prescribed on the rationale that reducing nasal and peritubal mucosal inflammation improves Eustachian tube function and accelerates clearance of middle ear fluid, but the short-term efficacy of this strategy remains contested.

**Objective.** To determine whether intranasal corticosteroid spray (mometasone, beclomethasone, budesonide or fluticasone), given alone or added to a co-treatment received equally by both arms, reduces the persistence of middle ear effusion at the first assessment up to 1 month (≤6 weeks) after starting treatment, compared with no steroid treatment.

**Methods.** Randomised and quasi-randomised controlled trials in children with OME were eligible. The outcome was the number of children (or ears, where reported per ear with both ears combined) with persistent effusion at the first ≤6-week assessment, defined as a type B (or type C2/non-type-A, where that was the trial's definition of failure) tympanogram, "not cured", "no improvement", or persistent effusion on otoscopy/otomicroscopy. Where trials reported resolution, cure or improvement, persistence was derived as total minus resolved. In multi-arm trials only the intranasal-steroid arm and the no-steroid arm receiving the same co-treatment were used. Risk ratios (RR) were pooled on the native RR scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Five trials contributing five independent effect sizes and 464 participants were included. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for persistent middle ear effusion was 0.492 (95% CI 0.286 to 0.846; p = 0.0104; k = 5; n = 464; REML; native RR pooling used). The automated direction field for this analysis returned "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)", reflecting that for ratio measures the favoured arm depends on which event was tabulated; under the protocol-specified convention for this review (the event counted is *persistence*, so RR < 1 denotes fewer persistent effusions with intranasal steroids), the point estimate lies numerically in the direction of the intranasal corticosteroid arm. Heterogeneity was substantial: Q = 26.423 (p < 0.001), I² = 84.83%, τ² = 0.311047. Individual standardized study-level estimates ranged from −1.246 (95% CI −1.725 to −0.767) to −0.263 (95% CI −0.673 to 0.148), with two large, precise trials driving the pooled benefit and three smaller trials individually compatible with no effect. Egger's test was non-significant (intercept −1.4346, p = 0.4937) and trim-and-fill imputed one potentially missing study (k₀ = 1; adjusted estimate −0.5541 on the pooled computation scale), but the overall small-study-effect risk was rated moderate.

**Conclusions.** Pooled data from five small trials suggest a statistically significant reduction in persistent middle ear effusion at ≤6 weeks with intranasal corticosteroids, but the estimate rests on marked between-study heterogeneity (I² = 84.83%), a prediction interval that crosses unity, a small evidence base (k = 5), and a moderate residual risk of small-study effects. The result should be regarded as hypothesis-generating rather than as a secure basis for routine prescribing, and it speaks only to very short-term effusion status, not to hearing, language or need for ventilation tubes.


## 1. Introduction

Otitis media with effusion (OME, "glue ear") is defined as the presence of fluid in the middle ear cleft without the signs and symptoms of acute infection. It is extremely common in early childhood: the majority of children experience at least one episode before school age, and a substantial minority develop persistent, bilateral disease with conductive hearing loss of 20–40 dB. Although most episodes resolve spontaneously within three months, persistent OME is associated with hearing impairment, listening difficulty in noise, delayed speech and language development in vulnerable children, balance disturbance, and reduced quality of life. Surgical management with ventilation (tympanostomy) tubes is effective but carries anaesthetic risk, cost, and a non-trivial rate of otorrhoea, tympanosclerosis and persistent perforation. Consequently, there is a long-standing search for an effective medical treatment that can shorten the natural history of effusion and avert surgery.

The pathophysiological rationale for intranasal corticosteroids in OME is coherent. Effusion is thought to arise from a combination of Eustachian tube dysfunction, adenoidal hypertrophy and obstruction of the tubal orifice, mucosal inflammation with goblet-cell metaplasia, and biofilm-associated low-grade infection. Allergic and non-allergic rhinitis are frequent co-morbidities. Topical intranasal corticosteroids reduce nasopharyngeal and peritubal mucosal oedema, shrink adenoid tissue, down-regulate inflammatory mediators, and might therefore restore tubal ventilation and promote clearance of middle ear fluid, while delivering minimal systemic exposure compared with oral steroids. Against this, the Eustachian tube orifice may receive little deposited drug from conventional nasal spray devices, intranasal steroids act slowly over weeks, and adherence in young children is imperfect.

Clinical guidelines have generally been unenthusiastic. Major guideline bodies recommend against routine use of intranasal or systemic steroids for OME, citing trials with short-lived or absent benefit. Nonetheless, prescribing persists, and newer trials — often combining intranasal steroids with adjuncts such as antihistamines, mucolytics, antibiotics or autoinflation given equally to both arms — have reported early advantages in effusion clearance. Earlier syntheses have tended to pool heterogeneous timepoints (from 2 weeks to 9 months) and heterogeneous outcome definitions, which can obscure a genuinely different pattern of early versus late effect: a treatment may accelerate resolution without changing the eventual proportion resolved.

This review therefore focuses deliberately and narrowly on the **earliest post-treatment assessment, at or before 6 weeks (≈1 month)**, and on a single, concretely countable outcome: the number of children (or ears) still having middle ear effusion. The comparison is intranasal corticosteroid versus no steroid treatment, where any co-treatment is given identically to both arms, so that the contrast isolates the steroid component.

**Review question (PICO).**
- **Population:** children with otitis media with effusion.
- **Intervention:** intranasal corticosteroid spray (mometasone, beclomethasone, budesonide, fluticasone), alone or added to a co-treatment given equally to both arms.
- **Comparator:** no steroid treatment — no treatment, watchful waiting, or the same co-treatment alone.
- **Outcome:** persistent middle ear effusion at the first assessment up to 1 month (≤6 weeks) after starting treatment.
- **Effect direction convention:** a risk ratio below 1 denotes fewer children/ears with persistent effusion in the intranasal steroid arm — i.e. more resolution of OME — at up to 1 month.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

The review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement. Analytic choices — in particular the restriction to the first ≤6-week assessment, the conversion of resolution/cure data into persistence counts, and the pre-specification of the risk ratio as the summary measure — were fixed before pooling.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Included:** randomised or quasi-randomised controlled trials enrolling children with a clinical and/or tympanometric diagnosis of OME; allocating at least one arm to an intranasal corticosteroid spray; including at least one arm receiving no steroid (no treatment, watchful waiting, or an identical co-treatment without steroid); and reporting dichotomous effusion status at a first follow-up occurring no later than 6 weeks after treatment initiation.

**Excluded:** studies of acute otitis media, chronic suppurative otitis media or cholesteatoma without an OME stratum; adult-only populations; trials of oral/systemic corticosteroids without an intranasal arm; trials in which the comparator arm received a different co-treatment (so that the steroid effect could not be isolated); studies reporting only continuous outcomes (e.g. mean tympanometric peak pressure, mean hearing threshold) with no derivable count of persistent effusion; narrative or systematic reviews, editorials, conference abstracts without extractable counts, and duplicate reports of the same trial cohort.

**Multi-arm trials.** Where a trial included additional active arms (oral corticosteroid, montelukast, antihistamine alone, antibiotic alone, autoinflation alone), only the intranasal-steroid arm and the no-steroid arm receiving the *same* co-treatment were extracted; other arms were ignored, and the shared control arm was never used more than once in the same analysis.

**Multi-category outcomes.** Where a trial's headline result was an ordinal scale (complete remission / partial remission / no change / exacerbation), persistence counts were derived from the post-treatment tympanogram-type table or the cure/no-cure table per arm rather than discarding the study. Where only resolution, cure or improvement was reported, persistence was computed as total minus resolved.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

Electronic searches were run in MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL), CINAHL and Web of Science, supplemented by trial registry searching (ClinicalTrials.gov, WHO ICTRP) and backward/forward citation chasing of included trials and of prior systematic reviews of medical therapy for OME. The search combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for the condition ("otitis media with effusion", "glue ear", "serous otitis media", "secretory otitis media", "middle ear effusion", "tympanometry") with terms for the intervention ("intranasal", "nasal spray", "corticosteroid", "glucocorticoid", "steroid", "mometasone", "beclomethasone", "budesonide", "fluticasone", "triamcinolone") and a sensitivity-maximising randomised-trial filter. No language or date restrictions were applied at the search stage.

### 2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Records were de-duplicated and screened on title and abstract against the eligibility criteria, after which potentially eligible reports were assessed in full text. For each included trial, the following were extracted per arm: number randomised and number analysed, intranasal corticosteroid molecule and dose/duration, co-treatment (if any), timing of the first post-baseline assessment, the method of ascertaining effusion (tympanometry, otoscopy, otomicroscopy, or a composite), the unit of analysis (child or ear), and the number of children/ears with persistent effusion at that first assessment. The analysis unit used was that reported by the trial: children where child-level data were given, ears where results were presented per ear with both ears combined.

### 2.5 Effect measures and synthesis

The pre-specified summary measure was the **risk ratio (RR)** for persistent effusion, pooled on its native scale. The effect-measure source for this analysis is recorded as an explicit analytic request, with no conflict detected between the requested and the derivable measure, and **native pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`): the reported pooled statistic is therefore a genuine risk ratio and not a standardized-mean-difference fallback. Pooling used a random-effects model with between-study variance estimated by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**, which is appropriate when substantial heterogeneity is anticipated and the number of studies is small.

For descriptive and graphical purposes the pipeline additionally reports each trial's effect on a standardized (Hedges' *g*-type) scale with 95% confidence intervals; these per-study standardized values are presented in Section 4 for comparability of magnitude and precision across trials, but they are **not** the basis of the pooled estimate, which was computed on the RR scale.

Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q test, the I² statistic (proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error), and τ² (absolute between-study variance on the pooling scale). Small-study effects and potential publication bias were examined using Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. Given k = 5, these tests were interpreted as exploratory only, in line with the conventional recommendation that funnel-plot asymmetry tests be reserved for syntheses of ten or more studies.

## 3. Search and Screening

The multi-database search, registry screening and citation chasing described above yielded a pool of records that were de-duplicated and then screened in two stages (title/abstract, then full text) against the eligibility criteria in Section 2.2. Reports were excluded at full text principally because they (i) were reviews, commentaries or guideline documents rather than primary trials; (ii) studied systemic rather than intranasal corticosteroids; (iii) reported no assessment earlier than 3 months, so that no ≤6-week timepoint could be extracted; (iv) used a comparator arm whose co-treatment differed from that of the steroid arm, confounding the steroid contrast; or (v) reported only continuous tympanometric or audiometric outcomes with no recoverable count of children or ears with persistent effusion.

**Five trials met all eligibility criteria and contributed data to the quantitative synthesis** (k = 5 studies, 5 independent effect sizes, 464 participants/ears in total). Each trial contributed exactly one effect size to the analysis, so there is no clustering of multiple effect estimates within a single study and no need for a multilevel or robust-variance adjustment; the units of analysis are statistically independent at the study level.

## 5. Effect Sizes

### 5.1 Study-level estimates

The per-study standardized estimates reported by the pipeline are shown in Table 1 and visualised in the forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) and the effect distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`). Exact values are:

- **Study 1 (R1):** −0.342764 (95% CI −0.967 to 0.282), n = 40 — confidence interval crosses the null.
- **Study 2 (R2):** −1.245715 (95% CI −1.725 to −0.767), n = 80 — large, statistically significant estimate.
- **Study 3 (R3):** −0.372316 (95% CI −0.804 to 0.060), n = 84 — crosses the null, marginally.
- **Study 4 (R4):** −0.262736 (95% CI −0.673 to 0.148), n = 92 — crosses the null.
- **Study 5 (R5):** −1.155081 (95% CI −1.482 to −0.828), n = 168 — large, statistically significant estimate, and the most precise single estimate in the set.

All five point estimates fall on the same side of the null, and in every case the direction corresponds to fewer persistent effusions in the intranasal corticosteroid arm. However, the magnitudes separate into two clearly distinguishable clusters: a **small-effect cluster** (Studies 1, 3 and 4; standardized estimates −0.26 to −0.37, all with confidence intervals overlapping no effect) and a **large-effect cluster** (Studies 2 and 5; standardized estimates −1.16 and −1.25, both statistically significant and with confidence intervals that do not overlap those of the small-effect cluster). The non-overlap of the confidence intervals for Study 4 (−0.673 to 0.148) and Study 2 (−1.725 to −0.767) is, on its own, strong evidence that the trials are not estimating a single common effect.

The effect distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`) displays this bimodal, rather than unimodal, spread of estimates. The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) plots each effect against its sample size/precision and shows that the dichotomy does not map onto study size in a simple monotonic way: the smallest trial (n = 40) sits in the small-effect cluster, while the largest (n = 168) sits in the large-effect cluster — a pattern that is the *opposite* of the classic small-study-effect signature, in which small trials show the most extreme benefits.

### 5.2 Scale of the pooled analysis

As set out in Section 2.5, the summary analysis was conducted on the **native risk ratio scale**, with `measure_label = "Risk Ratio (RR)"`, `computation_scale = "RR"`, and `native_pooling_used = true`. The pooled statistic reported in Section 6 is therefore a pooled risk ratio and should be named as such. It is **not** a Hedges' *g*, and it is not a standardized fallback. The standardized per-study values in Table 1 serve only to convey relative magnitude and precision on a common dimensionless scale.

### 6.2 Direction of effect — how to read this result

The automated direction classifier for this analysis returned **"neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)"**. This is the field that governs the formal statement of which arm is favoured, and it is reported here as returned: the automated classification did **not** assign the result to either arm, because for a ratio measure the favoured arm cannot be determined from the numeric sign alone — it depends entirely on whether the tabulated event is a good outcome (resolution) or a bad one (persistence).

Two things follow, and they must be kept distinct:

1. **Formally, per the direction field, no arm is designated as favoured by the automated analysis.** The reader should not infer favourability from the fact that 0.492 is below 1.
2. **Substantively, under this review's pre-specified convention,** the event counted is *persistence* of effusion, and the protocol states explicitly that RR < 1 means fewer children/ears with persistent middle ear effusion in the intranasal steroid arm — i.e. more resolution of OME. On that convention, the point estimate of 0.492 and its confidence interval (0.286 to 0.846, entirely below 1) lie numerically in the direction of the intranasal corticosteroid arm, corresponding to roughly a halving of the risk of persistent effusion at the first short-term assessment, with the interval compatible with anything from a 71% relative reduction to a 15% relative reduction.

The honest summary is therefore: the pooled ratio is statistically distinguishable from unity (p = 0.0104) and points, on the review's own counting convention, toward benefit from intranasal corticosteroids — but the pipeline's direction field does not itself certify a favoured arm, and the magnitude of any such benefit is poorly determined because of the heterogeneity documented in Section 7.

### 6.3 Clinical translation (illustrative)

If the baseline risk of persistence at ≤6 weeks in untreated or co-treatment-only children were, say, 60% — a plausible figure for short follow-up in OME, where spontaneous resolution is slow — an RR of 0.492 would correspond to approximately 30% persistence with intranasal steroid, an absolute risk reduction of about 30 percentage points and a number needed to treat of roughly 3. Applying instead the upper confidence bound (RR 0.846) gives about 51% persistence, an absolute reduction of 9 points and an NNT near 11. These illustrative figures should be treated with caution: they assume a baseline risk not derived from the pooled data, and they inherit all of the heterogeneity caveats below.

### 6.4 Cumulative analysis

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows how the pooled estimate evolves as studies are successively added. With only five trials, the cumulative curve has not stabilised: the running estimate remains sensitive to the inclusion of the two large-effect trials (Studies 2 and 5), and the cumulative confidence bounds do not narrow toward a plateau in the way that would signal a settled evidence base. This is a direct visual expression of the fact that the body of evidence is immature.

### 6.5 Absence of multiple outcome groups

The context data contain one `meta_analysis` entry and no multi-entry `meta_analysis_groups` structure, and no group is flagged `insufficient_data`. All included studies address the same outcome (persistent effusion) at the same pre-specified timepoint window (first assessment ≤6 weeks). Accordingly, a single pooled estimate is reported above, and there are no additional group-specific k, effect, CI or heterogeneity values to report, and no groups that had to be declared unpoolable.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity in this synthesis is **substantial and is the dominant feature of the result**.

- **Cochran's Q = 26.423 on 4 degrees of freedom, p < 0.001** (reported as 0.0). Q is more than six times its expected value under homogeneity (E[Q] = df = 4), decisively rejecting the null hypothesis that all five trials estimate a common effect. Notably, Q tests are typically underpowered with few studies, so reaching p < 0.001 with k = 5 indicates genuinely pronounced dispersion rather than a marginal signal.
- **I² = 84.83%.** Approximately 85% of the observed variability across trials reflects real between-study differences in effect rather than within-study sampling error. By conventional thresholds (25% / 50% / 75% for low / moderate / considerable), this is in the *considerable* range.
- **τ² = 0.311047** on the pooling (log-RR) scale, implying a between-study standard deviation of approximately 0.558 in log-RR units. This is a large dispersion for a dichotomous clinical outcome: it means that plausible true risk ratios across settings span roughly a three-fold range on either side of the mean.

The heterogeneity summary figure (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`) presents these diagnostics together.

**Prediction interval.** Because τ² is large, the mean effect is a poor guide to what would be expected in a new trial. An approximate 95% prediction interval, derived from τ² = 0.311047 and the standard error implied by the reported confidence interval, spans roughly **RR 0.07 to RR 3.6** (this interval is an approximation computed for interpretive purposes and is not among the pipeline's reported quantities). It includes 1.0 comfortably. In plain terms: although the *average* effect across these five trials is statistically distinguishable from no effect, the evidence does **not** establish that a new, comparable trial would find any benefit at all — it might find a large benefit, no benefit, or even harm.

**Where the heterogeneity comes from.** Inspection of Table 1 and the forest plot shows that the dispersion is not diffuse but structured: Studies 2 and 5 are mutually consistent large-effect trials, and Studies 1, 3 and 4 are mutually consistent small/null-effect trials, with essentially no overlap between the clusters. Plausible explanations — none of which can be tested here, because the necessary study-level covariates were not available in the structured dataset — include:

1. **Differences in the co-treatment given to both arms.** A steroid added on top of an already effective co-treatment (e.g. antibiotic, mucolytic, autoinflation) has less room to show incremental benefit than a steroid given alone against watchful waiting. This "ceiling" mechanism can easily generate two clusters.
2. **Differences in the outcome definition.** Trials using a strict definition of failure (type B tympanogram only) will record lower persistence than trials counting any non-type-A result (including type C2) as failure, and the resulting risk ratios need not be comparable.
3. **Differences in the exact follow-up week within the ≤6-week window.** Two weeks versus six weeks after initiation are materially different points on the resolution curve for a condition with substantial spontaneous remission.
4. **Unit of analysis (children vs ears).** Ear-level analyses that combine both ears without accounting for within-child correlation yield artificially narrow confidence intervals and can inflate the apparent weight and significance of a trial.
5. **Case-mix:** proportion with allergic rhinitis or adenoidal hypertrophy, duration of effusion before enrolment, age distribution, and prior episodes — all of which modulate the responsiveness of OME to anti-inflammatory therapy.
6. **Methodological quality,** especially blinding of the outcome assessor. Trials comparing steroid with *no treatment* (rather than placebo spray) are open-label; tympanometric reading and otoscopic judgement of "persistent effusion" are then susceptible to detection bias, which tends to exaggerate apparent treatment effects.

Given I² = 84.83%, the random-effects model is the correct analytic choice, but it should be understood as *describing* a distribution of effects rather than estimating a single underlying truth. Readers should weight the prediction interval at least as heavily as the confidence interval.

## 8. Moderators

**No formal moderator analysis was possible.** The structured dataset supplied for synthesis contained no study-level covariates (corticosteroid molecule, dose and duration, nature of the co-treatment, exact assessment week, outcome ascertainment method, unit of analysis, mean age, allergic-rhinitis prevalence, baseline effusion duration, blinding status, or risk-of-bias domain ratings). Consequently:

- **No meta-regression was fitted.** With k = 5 studies, meta-regression would in any case be inadmissible: the widely applied rule of thumb requires at least ten studies per covariate, and fitting even a single moderator to five effect sizes would be severely overfitted and uninterpretable.
- **No subgroup analyses were performed.** Pre-specified subgroups of interest (steroid alone vs steroid added to co-treatment; tympanometry- vs otoscopy-defined persistence; child- vs ear-level analysis; placebo-controlled vs no-treatment-controlled) could not be constructed from the available data. Any post hoc split of five studies into two groups of two or three would have very low power and a high risk of spurious findings.

The only exploration available was **graphical**. The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) relates effect magnitude to study size/precision. It shows **no monotonic size–effect gradient**: the two trials with the largest effects have n = 80 and n = 168 (the largest trial in the set), while the three with the smallest effects have n = 40, 84 and 92. Study size therefore does not explain the clustering, which strengthens the suspicion that the real moderators are design and definitional features — co-treatment, outcome threshold, blinding, and assessment timing — rather than precision.

**The practical consequence is important:** the 85% of variance attributable to between-study heterogeneity remains entirely unexplained. We cannot say for which children, with which steroid, on which background treatment, or at which follow-up point the large effects seen in Studies 2 and 5 are to be expected. This substantially limits the clinical actionability of the pooled estimate.

## 9. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Two complementary assessments were undertaken, both interpreted as exploratory because k = 5 is well below the minimum of ten studies conventionally required for funnel-plot asymmetry testing.

**Egger's regression test.** Intercept = **−1.4346**, **p = 0.4937**. There is no statistically significant evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry. The intercept is, however, non-trivial in magnitude and negative, i.e. nominally in the direction that would be produced by small studies reporting more favourable results — but with five studies the test has very low power, and the wide uncertainty around the intercept makes this numeric hint uninformative on its own.

**Trim-and-fill (Duval–Tweedie).** The procedure imputed **k₀ = 1** hypothetically missing study, with an adjusted estimate of **−0.5541** on the pooled computation scale (equivalent to a risk ratio of approximately 0.57 if read on the log-RR scale). Imputing a single study is a minimal adjustment, and the adjusted estimate remains on the same side of the null as the unadjusted one. In other words, the trim-and-fill correction does not overturn the direction of the finding, although it does attenuate its magnitude.

**Overall small-study-effect risk rating: moderate.**

**Interpretation (as supplied with the analysis).** Egger's regression test showed no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.49), and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed only a single hypothetically missing study (k₀ = 1), which would be unlikely to meaningfully shift the pooled effect estimate. Together, these indicate little direct statistical evidence of small-study effects or publication bias in this meta-analysis.

**Caveat (as supplied with the analysis).** The "moderate" risk rating should still be taken seriously, since Egger's test has low statistical power when the number of included studies is small, meaning a non-significant result cannot confirm the absence of bias; the rating likely also reflects non-statistical considerations such as limited searching of grey literature, reliance on published reports, or heterogeneity in study precision. The appropriate conclusion is that **publication bias was not detected but cannot be confidently excluded.**

To this we add two field-specific considerations. First, the OME medical-therapy literature is known to contain small single-centre trials, many published in specialty journals with limited indexing, so the unpublished or unindexed fraction may be larger than in better-curated fields. Second, in the present dataset the *largest* trial reports one of the two largest effects, which is not the pattern expected from classical publication bias; this is mildly reassuring, but with k = 5 it is equally consistent with chance.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 Principal findings

Pooling five randomised comparisons (464 analysis units), the risk of persistent middle ear effusion at the first assessment within 6 weeks of starting treatment was estimated at a **pooled Risk Ratio of 0.492 (95% CI 0.286 to 0.846, p = 0.0104)** for intranasal corticosteroid versus no steroid treatment, using REML random-effects pooling on the native RR scale. The pipeline's direction field returned "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)", so no arm is formally certified as favoured by the automated analysis; under the review's own pre-specified convention — in which the counted event is persistence and RR < 1 indicates fewer persistent effusions with intranasal steroids — the estimate lies numerically in the direction of benefit from intranasal corticosteroids, corresponding to approximately a halving of short-term persistence risk.

This apparently encouraging headline is, however, materially qualified by three features of the evidence. First, heterogeneity is considerable (I² = 84.83%, τ² = 0.311047, Q = 26.423, p < 0.001), and the five trials separate into two non-overlapping clusters rather than scattering around a common value. Second, the implied prediction interval for a future trial (approximately RR 0.07 to 3.6) comfortably includes no effect, so the synthesis does not establish that benefit would be reproduced in a new study. Third, the evidence base is small (k = 5, no trial above 168 units, median 84), and the pooled estimate is heavily dependent on two trials; cumulative analysis shows no stabilisation of the running estimate.

### 10.2 Relation to the existing literature

The finding sits in tension with the prevailing guideline position, which discourages intranasal or systemic corticosteroids for OME. That position derives principally from syntheses that pooled longer follow-up (3 months and beyond) and from the influential observation that any early steroid-associated advantage in effusion clearance tends to dissipate over subsequent months, with no demonstrable benefit for hearing, language outcomes, or avoidance of ventilation tubes.

The present analysis is not in direct contradiction of that literature, because it answers a deliberately narrower question: *does effusion clear faster in the first few weeks?* A treatment can plausibly accelerate resolution of a self-limiting condition without altering its ultimate course. Such an effect is real but of limited clinical value if the condition would have resolved anyway and if no durable functional benefit accrues. The pattern observed here — a statistically significant short-term advantage sitting on top of considerable heterogeneity — is precisely what one would expect if some trials captured a transient acceleration (possibly amplified by open-label outcome assessment) while others did not.

An alternative, and less flattering, reading is also available. The two large-effect trials may differ from the three null trials in blinding, in outcome threshold, or in co-treatment, in ways that inflate their estimates. With no study-level covariates available, this cannot be adjudicated. The non-significant Egger test and minimal trim-and-fill adjustment argue against gross publication bias as the sole explanation, but they cannot exclude design-related bias, which funnel-plot methods do not detect.

### 10.3 Mechanistic plausibility

A genuine short-term effect is biologically defensible. Intranasal corticosteroids reduce nasopharyngeal mucosal oedema and adenoid volume and suppress local inflammatory mediators within days to weeks, which could improve Eustachian tube patency and allow drainage of existing effusion. Children with concomitant allergic rhinitis or marked adenoidal hypertrophy would be the most likely responders — and, notably, the proportion of such children is exactly the kind of case-mix variable that could generate the two-cluster pattern seen here. Conversely, in children whose effusion is driven primarily by biofilm or by structural tubal dysfunction, topical anti-inflammatory therapy would be expected to do little, consistent with the null trials.

### 10.4 Implications for practice

The present result does **not** warrant a change in routine practice. Reasons: (i) the formal direction field does not certify a favoured arm; (ii) the prediction interval includes no effect; (iii) the outcome is a surrogate — the presence or absence of fluid at a single early timepoint — rather than hearing, language, quality of life, or avoidance of surgery, none of which were assessable here; (iv) the follow-up window (≤6 weeks) is far shorter than the 3-month watchful-waiting period recommended before intervention in most guidelines, so an advantage at 4–6 weeks may simply vanish by the time a management decision is actually made; and (v) adverse effects, adherence and cost were outside the scope of this synthesis.

A defensible reading for clinicians is: intranasal corticosteroids may bring forward the clearance of middle ear fluid in some children, but current evidence is too heterogeneous and too short-term to identify who those children are or to demonstrate that the acceleration translates into any outcome that matters to a child or family.

### 10.5 Implications for research

Priorities follow directly from the limitations. Future trials should (a) be adequately powered, ideally multicentre, with several hundred children; (b) use a **placebo nasal spray** so that effusion assessment is blinded; (c) pre-specify a single, explicit definition of persistence (e.g. type B tympanogram confirmed by otomicroscopy) and report the full tympanogram-type distribution per arm; (d) analyse at the **child** level, or explicitly account for within-child correlation if ears are the unit; (e) report both a short-term (4–6 week) and a durable (3-, 6- and 12-month) assessment in the same cohort, so that acceleration can be distinguished from cure; (f) include hearing thresholds and need for ventilation tubes as co-primary or key secondary outcomes; and (g) record and report candidate effect modifiers — allergic rhinitis status, adenoid size, baseline effusion duration, age — prospectively, so that the heterogeneity documented here can eventually be explained rather than merely quantified. Individual participant data meta-analysis would be the most efficient route to resolving the two-cluster pattern.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base.** Five trials and 464 analysis units. REML τ² estimates are imprecise with k = 5, so the width of the random-effects confidence interval is itself uncertain, and the prediction interval is only indicative.
2. **Considerable, unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 84.83%, τ² = 0.311047, Q = 26.423 (p < 0.001), with two non-overlapping clusters of estimates. The pooled mean may not describe any real clinical scenario.
3. **No moderator data.** No study-level covariates were available, so no meta-regression or subgroup analysis was possible and none was performed. The sources of heterogeneity remain speculative.
4. **No formal risk-of-bias synthesis.** Domain-level risk-of-bias ratings were not available in the structured dataset, so no sensitivity analysis restricted to low-risk trials, and no GRADE certainty rating, could be produced. Given that several trials in this field compare steroid with *no treatment* rather than placebo, unblinded outcome assessment is a plausible and unquantified source of effect inflation.
5. **Mixed and partly unverified units of analysis.** The protocol permits counts of children or of ears (both ears combined). Ear-level counts that ignore within-child correlation overstate precision and may have over-weighted the affected trials. Which trials used which unit could not be verified from the available data.
6. **Heterogeneous outcome definitions collapsed into one binary.** Persistence was accepted as type B, type C2/non-type-A, "not cured", "no improvement" or persistent effusion on otoscopy, and ordinal remission scales were back-converted to counts. This maximises study inclusion but introduces definitional noise that is itself a likely heterogeneity source.
7. **Surrogate, short-term outcome only.** The analysis addresses the presence of fluid at ≤6 weeks. It says nothing about hearing, speech and language, quality of life, recurrence, or avoidance of ventilation tubes, nor about durability beyond 6 weeks.
8. **Publication bias not excluded.** Egger's test was non-significant (p = 0.4937) and trim-and-fill imputed one study (k₀ = 1, adjusted estimate −0.5541), but with k = 5 these tests have minimal power; the overall small-study-effect risk was rated **moderate**, and bias cannot be confidently excluded.
9. **Study identification is by internal record ID.** The included trials are identified as records R1–R5 rather than by full bibliographic citation, because resolvable citation metadata were not present in the structured dataset; independent verification of the extracted counts against the primary reports was therefore not possible within this synthesis.
10. **Direction not formally certified.** The pipeline's `favors` field returned "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)". Any statement that the result favours intranasal corticosteroids rests on the review's pre-specified counting convention (persistence as the event), not on the automated direction assignment.
11. **Intervention lumping.** Mometasone, beclomethasone, budesonide and fluticasone differ in potency, bioavailability and device characteristics, and were pooled as a class; dose and duration also varied and could not be examined.

## 12. Conclusion

Across five randomised comparisons in children with otitis media with effusion (464 analysis units), intranasal corticosteroid treatment — given alone or added to a co-treatment received equally by both arms — was associated with a **pooled Risk Ratio for persistent middle ear effusion of 0.492 (95% CI 0.286 to 0.846, p = 0.0104)** at the first assessment within 6 weeks of starting treatment, estimated by REML random-effects pooling on the native risk-ratio scale (native pooling confirmed; the statistic is a risk ratio, not a standardized mean difference). The automated direction field returned "neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)", so no arm is formally certified as favoured; under this review's pre-specified convention, in which persistence is the counted event and RR < 1 denotes fewer persistent effusions on intranasal steroid, the estimate lies in the direction of benefit from intranasal corticosteroids.

This signal must be read alongside its context: heterogeneity was considerable and unexplained (I² = 84.83%, τ² = 0.311047, Q = 26.423, p < 0.001), with the trials separating into two non-overlapping clusters; the implied prediction interval for a new trial crosses unity; the evidence base is small and dominated by two trials; no moderator or risk-of-bias analysis was possible; and residual risk of small-study effects was rated moderate, with publication bias not detected but not excluded (Egger's p = 0.4937; trim-and-fill k₀ = 1, adjusted estimate −0.5541).

**Bottom line.** There is suggestive but not dependable evidence that intranasal corticosteroids accelerate the short-term clearance of middle ear effusion in children with OME. The finding is best regarded as hypothesis-generating. It does not establish durable benefit, does not address hearing or the need for ventilation tubes, and does not identify which children might respond. Adequately powered, placebo-controlled, child-level trials with standardised outcome definitions and both short- and long-term assessment are required before intranasal corticosteroids could be recommended for this indication.
