# Day-of-Surgery Pain After Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy versus Retrograde Intrarenal Surgery for Renal Stones: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Visual Analogue Scale Scores on Postoperative Day 0

## Abstract

**Background.** Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) and retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) are the two dominant minimally invasive options for renal calculi. Stone-free rates are frequently compared, but immediate postoperative pain — a patient-centred outcome that drives analgesic requirement, ambulation and same-day discharge — is less systematically synthesised. We quantified the difference in visual analogue scale (VAS) pain on the day of surgery (postoperative day 0).

**Methods.** We systematically searched for comparative studies of PCNL (standard, mini-, super-mini- or micro-PCNL) versus RIRS (flexible ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy) in adults with renal stones reporting VAS pain at the first postoperative assessment within ~24 h (preferentially the earliest value at or after 6 h). Mean, SD and n per arm were extracted for the PCNL and RIRS arms only; "±" notation was checked against table footnotes and standard errors were converted to SD (SD = SE × √n). Group labels were verified against column headers so that the sign of the mean difference was consistent across studies. Data were pooled with a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML) on the mean-difference scale. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ²; small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Seven studies contributing seven effect estimates and 802 randomised/enrolled participants were included. The pooled effect was a **Mean Difference (MD) of 1.166 (95% CI 0.234 to 2.099; p = 0.0142; k = 7; n = 802)** in the raw VAS units reported by the primary papers. Per the pipeline's direction field, the result **favours percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL: standard, mini-, super-mini or micro-PCNL)**; readers should note that under the prespecified sign convention for this review a positive MD denotes a higher (worse) day-0 VAS score in the PCNL arm, and this discrepancy is flagged explicitly in the Data Quality & Validation Notes. Heterogeneity was extreme (Q = 96.439, df = 6, p < 0.001; I² = 95.03%; τ² = 1.4557), with study-level estimates ranging from −0.529 to 2.165 and six of seven studies pointing in the same direction. The approximate 95% prediction interval spanned zero (≈ −2.2 to 4.5). Egger's test showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept 0.7651, p = 0.9492) and trim-and-fill imputed a single study (k₀ = 1), giving a near-unchanged adjusted estimate of 1.1183; overall publication-bias risk was nevertheless rated moderate because of the small number of studies.

**Conclusions.** Across seven comparative studies, day-of-surgery VAS pain differed between PCNL and RIRS by approximately 1.2 VAS points, a difference that reached statistical significance but was accompanied by I² = 95% and a prediction interval crossing the null. The magnitude — but not the existence — of the difference is therefore uncertain, and the pooled point estimate should not be used as a precise clinical expectation for any individual patient or PCNL variant.


## 1. Introduction

Renal calculi affect roughly 10% of adults over a lifetime, and the operative management of stones that are too large or too hard for shock-wave lithotripsy has consolidated around two endoscopic approaches. Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) accesses the collecting system through a transparenchymal tract and has progressively miniaturised — from standard (≥24 Fr) tracts through mini- (14–22 Fr), super-mini- (10–14 Fr) and micro-PCNL (≤5 Fr) — in an explicit attempt to preserve stone clearance while reducing parenchymal trauma, bleeding and pain. Retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS), performed with a flexible ureteroscope and holmium or thulium laser lithotripsy through the natural urinary tract, avoids a renal puncture entirely but is constrained by instrument size, intrarenal pressure and the need for ureteral access.

Comparative literature between these approaches has concentrated on stone-free rates, operative time, haemoglobin drop, transfusion and length of stay. Postoperative pain has been reported far more variably, despite its direct relevance to opioid exposure, time to mobilisation, eligibility for ambulatory or same-day-discharge pathways, and patient preference. Pain is also the outcome through which the whole rationale for tract miniaturisation is most plausibly expressed: if a smaller tract means less nociceptive input, the PCNL-versus-RIRS pain gap should narrow with each generation of instrument.

Pooling pain across studies is nevertheless methodologically delicate. The visual analogue scale (VAS) is reported on 0–10, 1–10 and 0–100 mm formats; assessment times range from recovery-room arrival to the morning of postoperative day 1; "±" in tables may denote standard deviation or standard error; and some reports label the RIRS arm "Group 1" while others label PCNL "Group 1", which can silently invert the sign of a mean difference. This review therefore applied a tightly specified extraction protocol to a single, clearly defined timepoint — the day of surgery (postoperative day 0) — and synthesised the results on the native mean-difference scale.

**Objective.** In adults undergoing surgery for renal stones, to estimate the difference in postoperative VAS pain on the day of surgery between PCNL (any miniaturisation class) and RIRS.

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Information sources and strategy

A systematic search of the biomedical literature was conducted for comparative studies (randomised controlled trials and comparative cohort/registry analyses) of PCNL versus RIRS in adults with renal calculi. The search combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for the population (kidney calculi, renal stone, nephrolithiasis, staghorn), the intervention (percutaneous nephrolithotomy, PCNL, mini-PCNL, miniperc, super-mini PCNL, SMP, micro-PCNL, microperc), the comparator (retrograde intrarenal surgery, RIRS, flexible ureteroscopy, fURS, ureterorenoscopy, laser lithotripsy, holmium, thulium) and the outcome (pain, visual analogue, VAS, analgesia, analgesic requirement). Reference lists of retrieved reports and of prior systematic reviews on PCNL versus RIRS were hand-searched.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Included** if the record (i) enrolled adults (≥18 years) with renal stones; (ii) compared a PCNL-type procedure (standard, mini-, super-mini- or micro-PCNL) with RIRS/flexible ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy; (iii) reported postoperative pain on a VAS scored 0–10 or 1–10 (or on a 0–100 mm scale, rescaled to 0–10 with the conversion recorded); and (iv) reported, for both arms, a mean, a dispersion measure convertible to an SD, and the arm sample size, at a day-of-surgery timepoint.

**Day-of-surgery definition.** The day-0 value was the first postoperative pain assessment within approximately 24 hours of the procedure (e.g. 1 h, 6 h, 8 h, 12 h). Where several same-day times were reported, the earliest value at or after 6 h was taken. Where only a 1-hour value existed, that value was used. Where a single untimed postoperative VAS was reported, it was treated as the day-of-surgery value. Values at 24 h, at the "first postoperative day morning", at 48 h, or later were **not** used.

**Excluded:** narrative and systematic reviews, duplicate reports of the same cohort, paediatric series, ureteric-stone-only populations, studies reporting only analgesic consumption or only categorical pain, and studies reporting pain exclusively at ≥24 h.

### 2.3 Data extraction

For each eligible study, the mean, SD and n were extracted separately for the PCNL arm and the RIRS arm. Where a trial had more than two arms (e.g. mini-PCNL vs standard PCNL vs RIRS), only the PCNL-type arm and the RIRS arm relevant to the comparison were extracted; no arm was used twice. Table footnotes were inspected to determine whether "±" denoted SD or SE; standard errors were converted using SD = SE × √n. Group labelling was reconciled against table column headers and the Methods-section group definitions, because several reports in this field label the RIRS arm "Group 1", so that the sign of every mean difference followed a single convention across the dataset.

### 2.4 Screening yield

Screening produced **seven studies** meeting all criteria, contributing **seven independent effect estimates** and **802 participants** in total (pipeline summary: n_studies = 7, n_effect_sizes = 7; meta-analysis n_total = 802). Because each study contributed exactly one estimate at one timepoint, there was no within-study clustering and no unit-of-analysis adjustment was required. All seven estimates addressed a single outcome construct (day-0 VAS pain), so a single meta-analytic group was formed; the context data contained no `meta_analysis_groups` entries requiring separate reporting, and no group was marked `insufficient_data`.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure

The effect measure was prespecified by the review question as a **Mean Difference (MD)** on the VAS scale, and the analysis pipeline recorded `effect_measure = "MD"`, `computation_scale = "MD"`, `measure_label = "Mean Difference (MD)"`, `effect_measure_source = "explicit_user_request"` and `native_pooling_used = true`. The pooled result is therefore a **confirmed native-scale mean difference**, expressed in VAS points, and is *not* a standardized fallback. It must not be described as Hedges' *g*.

One caveat attaches to the measure: the pipeline recorded an `effect_measure_conflict` noting that the instrument range was stated for only 5 of 7 rows and that no 0–100 rescaling was applied, so the MD is pooled in the papers' raw units. If any contributing study in fact used a 0–100 mm scale without that being recorded, its contribution would be on a ten-fold larger numerical scale — a possibility explicitly considered in the heterogeneity and sensitivity discussion below.

### 4.2 Sign convention and direction

Under the prespecified direction key for this review, a **positive** MD denotes a higher day-of-surgery VAS pain score (more pain) after PCNL than after RIRS, and a **negative** MD denotes less pain after PCNL. The pooled point estimate is positive (+1.166). The analysis record's `favors` field, however, names **"Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL: standard, mini-, super-mini or micro-PCNL)"** as the favoured arm, and — in accordance with the reporting rules for this report — the favoured arm is stated from that field rather than inferred from the sign. The two statements are not reconcilable with each other, and this inconsistency is reported plainly rather than silently resolved; see §9, Data Quality & Validation Notes. Readers making clinical use of this synthesis should rely on the raw, extraction-level statement in Table 1 — six of seven studies recorded a higher day-0 VAS in the PCNL arm — and on the magnitude of the pooled difference, rather than on the direction label alone.

### 4.3 Precision

The implied standard error of the pooled estimate, derived from the reported 95% confidence interval, is approximately 0.476 VAS points — a wide interval relative to the point estimate, reflecting the large between-study variance component rather than a shortage of participants (802 in total is a reasonable sample for a continuous outcome).

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model fitted by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** gave:

> **Pooled Mean Difference (MD) = 1.166 VAS points (95% CI 0.234 to 2.099), p = 0.0142, k = 7 studies, n = 802 participants.**
> Favoured arm as recorded in the analysis output: **Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL: standard, mini-, super-mini or micro-PCNL)**.
> Heterogeneity: Q = 96.439 (df = 6), p < 0.001; I² = 95.03%; τ² = 1.4557 (τ ≈ 1.21 VAS points).

The forest plot is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`; the distribution of study-level estimates is shown at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`.

Three features of this result deserve emphasis.

**(i) The difference is statistically detectable but imprecisely sized.** The 95% confidence interval for the mean effect (0.234 to 2.099) excludes zero, so the hypothesis of no average day-0 VAS difference is rejected at the conventional threshold. But the interval spans nearly two VAS points, which covers everything from a difference almost certainly imperceptible to patients (0.2 points, far below the commonly cited ~1.3-point minimal clinically important difference for acute pain on a 0–10 VAS) to a difference that would be unmistakable at the bedside (2.1 points).

**(ii) The prediction interval crosses the null.** Using τ² = 1.4557 and the implied standard error of the mean effect, the approximate 95% prediction interval for the true effect in a new, exchangeable setting is roughly **−2.2 to +4.5 VAS points** (t-based, df = k − 2 = 5). In other words, while the *average* day-0 pain difference across these seven studies is positive, the model does not exclude the possibility that in a future comparable population the difference would run in the opposite direction. This is the practically important consequence of I² = 95%.

**(iii) Cumulative stability.** The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows how the running pooled estimate behaves as studies accumulate. With two studies of markedly larger effect (study_3_R3 and study_4_R4, both above 2.0) and one of opposite sign (study_5_R5), the running estimate does not settle onto a stable plateau; its position depends materially on which studies have been accrued, which is a direct visual expression of the τ² reported above.

Because all seven studies measured the same construct at the same prespecified timepoint, a single pooled figure is appropriate; no separate timepoint- or outcome-specific subgroups required independent reporting, and no analysis group in the dataset was flagged as having too few studies to pool.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity in this synthesis is extreme by any conventional benchmark:

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 96.439 (df = 6) | Observed dispersion ≈ 16× the degrees of freedom |
| Q p-value | < 0.001 | Homogeneity decisively rejected |
| I² | 95.03% | ~95% of observed variance attributable to between-study differences, not sampling error |
| τ² | 1.4557 | Between-study SD of true effects ≈ 1.21 VAS points |
| Range of estimates | −0.529 to 2.165 | 2.69 VAS-unit spread |

A graphical summary of the heterogeneity diagnostics is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

With τ ≈ 1.21 VAS points, the between-study variation in the true effect is of the same order as the pooled effect itself (1.166). Practically, this means that the seven studies are *not* estimating a single common day-0 pain difference; they are estimating a family of effects whose members differ from one another by about as much as the average effect differs from zero.

Plausible sources of this dispersion, in approximate order of likely importance:

1. **PCNL miniaturisation class.** Standard PCNL (≥24 Fr), mini-PCNL, super-mini-PCNL and micro-PCNL impose very different degrees of parenchymal and capsular trauma. The review deliberately pooled across all classes to answer the stated question, but a 24 Fr tract and a 4.85 Fr needle-sheath are not biologically equivalent nociceptive insults, and this alone could generate effects ranging from near-zero to >2 VAS points.
2. **Nephrostomy versus tubeless/totally tubeless exit strategy.** A large-bore nephrostomy tube is itself a potent source of flank pain on day 0; tubeless PCNL abolishes that component. Conversely, RIRS arms almost universally leave a ureteral stent, which generates its own irritative pain and which in some series dominates the day-0 score.
3. **Timing within day 0.** The protocol permitted values from 1 h to approximately 12 h post-procedure. Pain trajectories after both procedures are steepest in the first hours, so a 1-hour value and an 8-hour value are not interchangeable; a study that happened to report only a 1-hour value will sit at a different point on the recovery curve than one reporting at 6–8 h.
4. **Analgesic protocol.** VAS at any fixed time is a *treated* pain score. Studies with scheduled multimodal analgesia or regional/peritubal local anaesthetic infiltration for PCNL will show compressed between-arm differences; studies with on-demand analgesia will show expanded ones. Where analgesia was titrated to a VAS target, the between-arm difference may migrate into the (unreported) analgesic-consumption outcome instead.
5. **Scale and reporting heterogeneity.** Instrument range was stated for only 5/7 rows and no rescaling was applied. An unrecorded 0–100 mm scale, or an SE-labelled-as-SD dispersion that escaped detection, would inflate both the point estimate and the apparent heterogeneity of the affected study.
6. **Case mix.** Stone burden, Hounsfield density, lower-pole versus non-lower-pole location, and single-versus-multiple tracts all modify operative trauma and were not uniform across studies.

Given I² = 95.03%, the random-effects summary should be read as the *centre of a distribution* of effects, not as a transferable point estimate, and the prediction interval should be reported alongside it in any clinical summary.

## 7. Moderators

No formal moderator analysis (meta-regression or prespecified subgroup analysis) could be conducted with confidence on this dataset, and none was returned by the analysis pipeline. The reason is arithmetical rather than conceptual: with k = 7, standard guidance requires roughly ten studies per covariate before a meta-regression coefficient is interpretable, so even a single-covariate model would be fitted on a degrees-of-freedom budget too small to distinguish real moderation from noise.

What the data do permit is descriptive inspection of effect size against study precision, shown in the bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`. Two observations follow:

- **Effect magnitude does not track sample size monotonically.** The largest study (study_4_R4, n = 216) reports one of the two largest effects (2.165), while the second largest (study_2_R2, n = 160) reports one of the smallest positive effects (0.560). The single negative estimate comes from the smallest study (study_5_R5, n = 68). This pattern argues against a simple small-study-effect explanation for the dispersion — a point consistent with the formal bias tests in §8.
- **The dataset is effectively bimodal.** Five studies cluster between 0.56 and 0.72 (a tight band near 0.6 VAS points), two studies sit above 2.0, and one sits at −0.53. A pooled estimate of 1.166 lies in a sparsely populated region *between* the two main clusters — i.e. it describes no individual study well. The distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`) makes this visible. Had study-level covariates (miniaturisation class, tube status, assessment hour, VAS range) been uniformly reported, the obvious hypothesis to test would be that the two high-effect studies used standard-calibre PCNL with nephrostomy drainage and/or a 0–100 mm scale, while the 0.6-band studies used miniaturised, tubeless techniques. This hypothesis is not testable with the data at hand and is offered only as a direction for future individual-participant-data work.

An exploratory leave-one-out sensibility check follows directly from the forest and cumulative plots: removal of either of the two high-effect studies would pull the pooled estimate down toward the 0.6-point cluster, and removal of the single negative study would move it up. No single study, however, drives the sign of the result, since six of seven estimates share the same sign.

## 8. Publication Bias

Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | 0.7651 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.9492 |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | 1 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate | 1.1183 |
| Overall bias risk rating | Moderate |

Egger's regression test provides essentially no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.9492, far from conventional significance), and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed only a single hypothetical missing study (k₀ = 1), implying that any correction to the pooled effect would be negligible — the adjusted estimate of 1.1183 differs from the observed 1.1665 by less than 0.05 VAS points, a quantity of no clinical consequence whatsoever. Taken together, these statistics suggest that small-study effects and publication bias are unlikely to be materially distorting the summary estimate.

**Caveat.** The "moderate" overall risk rating therefore appears to be driven by factors beyond these two tests — most plausibly the limited number of included studies (Egger's test is substantially underpowered with fewer than about ten studies, and trim-and-fill is unreliable in small or highly heterogeneous meta-analyses, both of which conditions apply here with k = 7 and I² = 95%), restricted grey-literature searching, or an outcome-reporting pattern that favours positive results. These null findings should be read as an **absence of detectable bias rather than as evidence that no bias exists**.

A further, bias-adjacent concern specific to this outcome is **selective outcome reporting within studies**. Postoperative pain is rarely a primary endpoint in PCNL-versus-RIRS trials; it is usually one of several secondary outcomes, often reported at whichever timepoint shows the clearest separation. Trials that measured day-0 VAS and found no difference may have reported only analgesic consumption, or only a 24-hour value — neither of which would be captured by this review's eligibility window. Such selective *timepoint* reporting would not generate funnel asymmetry detectable by Egger's test but would bias the pooled estimate away from the null.

## 9. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-handling issues were recorded during analysis and are reported here without mitigation or softening:

1. **Effect-measure conflict — instrument range incompletely documented.** The pipeline flagged that the VAS instrument range was stated for **only 5 of 7 extracted rows**. Consequently **no 0–100 mm to 0–10 rescaling was applied**, and the mean differences were pooled in the raw units reported by the primary papers. If any undocumented row used a 0–100 mm scale, that study's contribution would be on a ten-fold larger numeric scale, which would simultaneously inflate the pooled MD and inflate τ². This is a credible partial explanation for the two outlying estimates above 2.0 and should be resolved before the point estimate is quoted as a VAS-point difference.

2. **Direction label inconsistent with the prespecified sign convention.** The analysis record's `favors` field names **Percutaneous nephrolithotomy** as the favoured arm, while the review's prespecified direction key states that a positive mean difference (as observed, +1.166) indicates a *higher* — i.e. worse — day-0 VAS score after PCNL, which would favour RIRS. As required, the favoured arm has been reported from the `favors` field throughout this report, but the conflict is unresolved in the source data and is disclosed here so that readers do not treat the direction label as independently verified. The underlying extraction-level fact is unambiguous and is the safer basis for interpretation: six of the seven included studies recorded a higher day-0 VAS in the PCNL arm (Table 1).

3. **Scale label of study-level estimates.** Study-level estimates in the analysis dataset are stored under the field name `hedges_g`, whereas the pooled summary carries `measure_label = "Mean Difference (MD)"` with `native_pooling_used = true` and `computation_scale = "MD"`. The pooled figure reported in §5 is therefore a confirmed native-scale mean difference and must not be described as Hedges' *g*; the study-level column in Table 1 is reproduced verbatim from the dataset with its original field name noted. Where the two labelling systems disagree, the deterministic Effect Measure Summary appended to this report governs.

4. **Unit-of-analysis integrity.** Each study contributed exactly one effect estimate (7 studies, 7 effect sizes), so no cohort was double-counted and no multi-arm trial contributed a shared control arm more than once. In multi-arm trials, only the PCNL-type and RIRS arms were extracted, as prespecified.

5. **Dispersion type.** SD was required for all rows; "±" notation was checked against table footnotes, and standard errors, where identified, were converted via SD = SE × √n. Residual risk remains for any study in which the footnote was absent or ambiguous and "±" was assumed to be SD.

6. **No analysis group was excluded for insufficient data.** All seven eligible studies addressed the single day-0 timepoint and were pooled together; the dataset contained no additional outcome/timepoint groups requiring separate k, pooled effect, CI or heterogeneity reporting.

## 10. Discussion

This synthesis of seven comparative studies and 802 adults with renal stones found a statistically significant difference in day-of-surgery VAS pain between PCNL and RIRS of approximately **1.17 VAS points (95% CI 0.234 to 2.099; p = 0.0142)**, pooled on the native mean-difference scale by REML random-effects modelling. Six of the seven contributing studies recorded a higher day-0 score in the PCNL arm; one recorded the reverse.

**Biological plausibility of a PCNL–RIRS pain gap.** A difference in immediate postoperative pain between a transparenchymal and a transurethral approach is mechanistically expected. PCNL creates a tract through skin, muscle, renal capsule and parenchyma, frequently with a nephrostomy tube traversing the flank; this generates somatic, capsular and drain-related nociception that has no counterpart in RIRS. RIRS generates visceral discomfort from ureteral access sheath trauma, intrarenal pressure and, almost always, a ureteral stent — real but typically later-peaking and differently characterised. A day-0 advantage for the retrograde approach is therefore the prior one would expect, and six of seven studies are consistent with it.

**But the magnitude is not established.** The headline finding of this review is less the point estimate than its instability. With I² = 95.03%, τ² = 1.4557 and a prediction interval of roughly −2.2 to +4.5, the seven studies are plainly estimating different things. The effect distribution is close to bimodal: a tight cluster of five studies near 0.6 VAS points — below most published thresholds for a minimal clinically important difference in acute pain — and two studies above 2.0, with one negative outlier. A pooled value of 1.166 is an arithmetic compromise that describes none of these groups. Clinically, the honest statement is: *RIRS is probably associated with somewhat less pain on the day of surgery, but whether that difference is subclinical (≈0.5 points) or substantial (≈2 points) depends on factors this dataset cannot identify.*

**The likely identity of those factors.** The most natural candidates are PCNL calibre and drainage strategy. The entire clinical rationale for mini-, super-mini- and micro-PCNL is that reducing tract size reduces trauma and pain; if that rationale holds, a meta-analysis that pools all PCNL variants is pooling across a true effect gradient, and the observed heterogeneity is the gradient itself made visible. Similarly, tubeless and totally tubeless PCNL removes one of the dominant day-0 pain generators. An unrecorded 0–100 mm VAS in one or both outlying studies is a competing, purely artefactual explanation that the flagged instrument-range gap (5/7 rows documented) leaves open. Distinguishing these explanations requires either complete scale documentation or individual-participant data, and is the clearest priority for future work.

**Relation to the wider evidence.** The broader PCNL-versus-RIRS literature consistently reports higher stone-free rates and shorter operative times for PCNL in larger stones, and lower complication and transfusion rates, shorter hospital stay and faster recovery for RIRS. A modest day-0 pain advantage for RIRS fits that pattern and strengthens the case for RIRS in settings prioritising ambulatory pathways and opioid minimisation. It does not, on its own, override stone-burden-driven indications: a 1-point VAS difference on one day is a weak counterweight to a materially lower chance of stone clearance or the need for staged procedures.

**Clinical implications.** For a patient with a stone amenable to either approach, these data support counselling that pain on the day of surgery is likely to be somewhat greater after PCNL than after RIRS, that the difference averages around one VAS point but varies widely between centres and techniques, and that it is likely to be smaller with miniaturised and tubeless PCNL. For units performing PCNL, the data reinforce the value of pain-sparing adjuncts — tract miniaturisation where oncologically/technically appropriate, peritubal or intercostal local anaesthetic infiltration, early nephrostomy removal or tubeless exit strategies, and protocolised multimodal analgesia.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Extreme heterogeneity.** I² = 95.03% and τ² = 1.4557 mean the pooled MD is the mean of a broad distribution rather than an estimate of a common effect. Any single-number summary of this literature, including ours, overstates the precision of current knowledge.
2. **Small number of studies.** With k = 7, meta-regression was not interpretable, subgroup analysis by PCNL calibre or drainage strategy was not possible, Egger's test was underpowered, and trim-and-fill was unreliable. The between-study variance τ² is itself estimated with few degrees of freedom, making the confidence and prediction intervals approximate.
3. **Pooling across PCNL variants.** Standard, mini-, super-mini- and micro-PCNL were combined as specified by the research question. These are mechanically distinct interventions and almost certainly have distinct pain profiles; their aggregation is a deliberate design choice but a genuine limitation on interpretability.
4. **Incomplete VAS scale documentation.** Instrument range was stated for only 5/7 rows and no rescaling was applied, so an undetected 0–100 mm scale cannot be excluded as a contributor to both the pooled magnitude and the heterogeneity.
5. **Timepoint heterogeneity within "day 0".** Eligible assessments ranged from 1 h to ~12 h post-procedure, and a single untimed postoperative VAS was accepted as day 0. Pain changes rapidly over this window, so the pooled estimate blends different points on the recovery trajectory.
6. **VAS as a treated outcome.** Pain scores are conditional on the analgesic protocol, which was not standardised across studies and could not be adjusted for. Studies with aggressive scheduled analgesia will understate the untreated pain difference; analgesic consumption, which would complete the picture, was not the outcome of this review.
7. **Unresolved direction label.** As set out in §9, the `favors` field and the prespecified sign convention disagree. This report follows the `favors` field as instructed, but the discrepancy constrains how confidently the direction of benefit can be stated from the pooled output alone.
8. **Risk of bias in included studies not formally tabulated.** Study designs (randomised versus comparative cohort), allocation concealment, and — critically for a subjective outcome — blinding of outcome assessors were not available for structured appraisal. Blinding of patients to PCNL versus RIRS is impossible, so all day-0 VAS comparisons in this field carry an irreducible risk of expectation and detection bias that tends to exaggerate differences in the anticipated direction.
9. **Selective outcome and timepoint reporting.** Day-0 pain is typically a secondary endpoint; trials with null day-0 results may have reported only later timepoints or only analgesic use, biasing the available evidence away from the null in a way no funnel-plot method can detect.
10. **Clinical importance threshold.** The lower confidence bound (0.234 VAS points) is well below any accepted minimal clinically important difference, so statistical significance here does not establish clinical significance.

---

## 12. Conclusion

In adults with renal stones, seven comparative studies (802 participants) yield a pooled **Mean Difference of 1.166 VAS points (95% CI 0.234 to 2.099; p = 0.0142)** for day-of-surgery pain between PCNL and RIRS, with the analysis output recording the result as favouring **percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL: standard, mini-, super-mini or micro-PCNL)** — a direction label that, as documented in the Data Quality & Validation Notes, conflicts with the review's prespecified convention under which this positive difference denotes more pain after PCNL and six of the seven included studies recorded a higher day-0 VAS in the PCNL arm.

What can be stated with reasonable confidence is that a difference exists and is of roughly one VAS point on average. What cannot be stated is its true size in any particular setting: heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 95.03%, τ² = 1.4557, Q = 96.439, p < 0.001), the study-level estimates were effectively bimodal (a cluster near 0.6 and two studies above 2.0, plus one negative outlier), and the 95% prediction interval crosses zero. Publication bias was not detectable (Egger's p = 0.9492; trim-and-fill k₀ = 1, adjusted estimate 1.1183) but could not be excluded given only seven studies, and overall bias risk was rated moderate.

Future comparative trials should report day-0 pain at a standardised time (e.g. 6 h), state the VAS range explicitly, report dispersion unambiguously as SD, specify PCNL calibre and exit strategy, and pair VAS with cumulative opioid-equivalent consumption. Until then, the day-of-surgery pain difference between PCNL and RIRS should be presented to patients as probable in direction but uncertain in magnitude, and should be weighed against stone-free rate, complication risk and length of stay rather than treated as a decisive consideration on its own.
