# Day-of-Surgery Postoperative Pain After Percutaneous Nephrolithotomy Versus Retrograde Intrarenal Surgery in Adults With Renal Stones: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) and retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) are established interventions for renal stones, but their immediate postoperative pain profiles may differ.

**Objective:** To evaluate postoperative pain intensity on the day of surgery (postoperative day 0) after PCNL—including standard, mini-, super-mini-, and micro-PCNL—compared with RIRS using flexible ureteroscopy and laser lithotripsy.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons involved adults with renal stones and reported day-of-surgery pain using a visual analogue scale (VAS). The intended selection rule prioritized the earliest assessment at or after 6 hours, allowed a 1-hour value when it was the only available same-day measurement, and treated an otherwise untimed single postoperative VAS assessment as day-of-surgery pain. Measurements at 24 hours, the first postoperative-day morning, 48 hours, or later were ineligible. Six effect estimates from seven included studies were available for pooling. A random-effects model estimated the pooled Mean Difference (MD; PCNL minus RIRS) using restricted maximum likelihood (REML).

**Results:** The six pooled comparisons included 702 participants. The pooled Mean Difference was 1.1131967245312981 (95% CI 0.000 to 2.226; p=0.04990472332329719), favoring RIRS. Heterogeneity was considerable (I²=96.3%; τ²=1.811268; Q=96.006; p<0.001). The study-level estimates ranged from −0.529395 to 2.164813. Egger’s intercept was 0.8292 (p=0.9657), providing no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry, whereas trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study and produced an adjusted estimate of 1.2077. Publication-bias risk was therefore classified as moderate. Instrument range was documented for only five of six pooled rows; consequently, no 0–100-to-0–10 rescaling was applied, and the MD was pooled in the papers’ reported raw units.

**Conclusions:** The pooled result was borderline statistically significant and favored RIRS for lower day-of-surgery postoperative pain. However, extreme heterogeneity, uncertain cross-study VAS scale harmonization, a small evidence base, and incomplete reporting of review and study characteristics substantially limit confidence in the magnitude and generalizability of the result.

## Introduction

Percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL) and retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) are commonly used surgical treatments for renal stones. PCNL encompasses procedures with differing tract sizes—including standard, mini-, super-mini-, and micro-PCNL—whereas RIRS generally uses flexible ureteroscopy with intracorporeal laser lithotripsy. Differences in access route, tract dilation, instrumentation, drainage, anesthesia, and postoperative management may affect immediate postoperative pain.

This review addressed the following question: among adults with renal stones, what is the effect of PCNL compared with RIRS on postoperative pain on the day of surgery (postoperative day 0)? The prespecified direction was PCNL minus RIRS: a positive Mean Difference (MD) denotes greater pain after PCNL and favors RIRS, while a negative MD denotes less pain after PCNL and favors PCNL.

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

Eligible studies directly compared an eligible PCNL technique (standard, mini-, super-mini-, or micro-PCNL) with RIRS performed using flexible ureteroscopy and laser lithotripsy in adults with renal stones. The outcome was postoperative pain intensity measured by visual analogue scale (VAS) on the day of surgery and reported with an arm-specific mean, standard deviation (SD), and sample size.

The intended time-point hierarchy was:

1. When several same-day measurements were available, select the earliest assessment at or after 6 hours.
2. When no assessment at or after 6 hours was available and only a 1-hour value was reported, use the 1-hour value.
3. Treat a single postoperative VAS measurement with no reported assessment time as a day-of-surgery value.
4. Exclude values recorded at 24 hours, on the morning of postoperative day 1, at 48 hours, or on later days.

For studies using a 0–100 mm VAS, both means and SDs were to be divided by 10 to place results on a 0–10 scale. Standard errors were not eligible substitutes for SDs. In multi-arm trials, only eligible PCNL and RIRS groups were to be extracted.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) combined six comparisons and 702 participants. The **pooled Mean Difference was 1.1131967245312981** (95% CI 0.000 to 2.226; p=0.04990472332329719), favoring **RIRS (flexible ureteroscopy with laser lithotripsy)**.

Under the specified PCNL-minus-RIRS convention, the estimate indicates higher day-of-surgery postoperative pain in the PCNL groups on average. The lower confidence limit was reported as 0.000 after rounding, and the p value was only slightly below 0.05. The evidence for a nonzero average effect should therefore be described as borderline rather than robust.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was extreme:

- **I²:** 96.3%
- **τ²:** 1.811268
- **Cochran’s Q:** 96.006
- **Q-test p value:** reported as 0.0 (conventionally interpreted as p<0.001)

The I² value indicates that most observed variation was beyond that expected from sampling error alone. The large between-study variance and the opposing estimate in `study_5_R5` reinforce the conclusion that the pooled mean does not describe a uniform treatment effect. A prediction interval was not supplied and cannot be calculated exactly from the aggregate information provided without adding unreported assumptions.

A graphical summary of heterogeneity is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

Potential clinical and methodological explanations include differences among standard and reduced-tract PCNL techniques, tubeless versus drained procedures, anesthesia and analgesia protocols, stone burden, access number and location, operative duration, and timing of pain assessment. These explanations are clinically plausible but could not be tested because study-level covariates were not supplied.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **0.8292** with **p=0.9657**, providing no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **one potentially missing study** and yielded an adjusted estimate of **1.2077**, compared with the unadjusted pooled MD of 1.1131967245312981.

These findings are not fully reassuring. Tests for funnel-plot asymmetry have low power when only six estimates are available, and trim-and-fill depends on assumptions that may be violated in the presence of extreme heterogeneity. Consistent with the supplied classification, the overall publication-bias risk is **moderate**: Egger’s test was negative, but one potentially missing comparison was imputed and selective dissemination cannot be excluded.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across six comparisons involving 702 participants, PCNL was associated with greater day-of-surgery postoperative pain than RIRS on average. The pooled Mean Difference was 1.1131967245312981 (95% CI 0.000 to 2.226), and the supplied direction favored RIRS. Five estimates favored RIRS, whereas one statistically favored PCNL.

The finding must be interpreted alongside I²=96.3% and τ²=1.811268. Such heterogeneity indicates that the magnitude—and potentially the direction—of the effect differed substantially among studies. The pooled estimate is therefore an average across dissimilar settings rather than a reliably transportable treatment effect for every PCNL technique or clinical scenario.

### Clinical interpretation

The likely direction of the average result is clinically plausible because percutaneous access can cause tract-related pain, whereas RIRS avoids a renal parenchymal access tract. Nevertheless, contemporary reduced-tract and tubeless PCNL techniques may have different pain profiles from standard PCNL. The one comparison favoring PCNL emphasizes that procedural details and perioperative care may materially alter pain outcomes.

The absolute clinical meaning of an MD of 1.1131967245312981 cannot be established securely from the supplied data. Instrument ranges were known for only five of six rows, and no 0–100-to-0–10 conversion was applied. If all observations were on a common 0–10 VAS, an approximately 1.1-point difference could be potentially relevant, but that interpretation cannot be assumed here. The estimate should instead be understood as a pooled difference in the studies’ reported raw VAS units, subject to incomplete scale verification.

### Certainty and applicability

Confidence in the result is reduced by extreme inconsistency, the small number of studies, borderline statistical significance, uncertain measurement harmonization, moderate publication-bias risk, and the absence of study-level risk-of-bias information. Applicability to a particular patient depends on the exact PCNL subtype, stone complexity, institutional technique, drainage strategy, and analgesic protocol. The evidence does not support treating standard, mini-, super-mini-, and micro-PCNL as having interchangeable pain effects in all settings.

## Data Quality and Reporting Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` were supplied, the following data-quality issues were explicit in the results:

- Seven studies were included, but only six contributed an effect estimate; the reason for the non-contributing study was not reported.
- VAS instrument range was stated for only five of six pooled rows.
- No 0–100-to-0–10 rescaling was performed; the analysis pooled MDs in the papers’ reported raw units.
- Search, screening, study-characteristic, arm-level outcome, and risk-of-bias data were not supplied.
- The numerical output reported the Q-test p value as 0.0; this should be read as a value below the output’s numerical precision, not a literal probability of zero.
- The moderator underlying the generated bubble chart was not identified, preventing inferential interpretation.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations.

1. **Incomplete review-process information:** Databases, search dates, search strings, duplicate removal, screening counts, full-text exclusions, protocol registration, and PRISMA flow information were unavailable.
2. **Uncertain scale harmonization:** The VAS range was not documented for one of six pooled estimates, and the intended conversion of 0–100 mm measurements to a 0–10 scale was not performed. Pooling raw MDs is valid only if scales are commensurate.
3. **Extreme heterogeneity:** I²=96.3% limits the interpretability and transportability of the pooled average.
4. **Sparse evidence base:** Only six estimates were pooled, reducing precision and the reliability of publication-bias tests and precluding credible moderator analysis.
5. **Missing study descriptors:** Trial design, PCNL subtype, pain-assessment timing, analgesic protocol, and participant and stone characteristics were unavailable.
6. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** Without domain-level judgments, the susceptibility of the pooled estimate to randomization, allocation, blinding, missing-data, measurement, and selective-reporting biases cannot be evaluated.
7. **Potential multiplicity and dependence could not be assessed:** The information supplied did not establish whether reports shared participants or whether multiple eligible arms or time points required dependency adjustments.
8. **No clinical-threshold analysis:** A minimally important difference was not prespecified, and uncertain VAS units prevent firm conclusions about clinical importance.

## Conclusion

The REML random-effects meta-analysis of six comparisons (702 participants) found a pooled Mean Difference of 1.1131967245312981 (95% CI 0.000 to 2.226; p=0.04990472332329719), favoring RIRS for lower postoperative pain on the day of surgery. This result was borderline statistically significant and accompanied by extreme heterogeneity (I²=96.3%; τ²=1.811268). Because VAS scale comparability was not fully verified, publication-bias risk was moderate, and study-level methodological details were unavailable, the magnitude of benefit should be interpreted cautiously. Better-reported comparative trials using standardized pain scales, clearly defined postoperative time points, consistent analgesic protocols, and stratification by PCNL subtype are needed.
