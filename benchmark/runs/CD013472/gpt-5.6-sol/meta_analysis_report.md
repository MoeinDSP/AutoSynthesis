# Music or Voice Interventions and Heart Rate During Intervention Sessions in Preterm Infants: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis of Randomized Controlled Trials

## Abstract

### Background
Music and voice interventions are used in neonatal intensive care units with the aim of improving physiologic regulation in preterm infants. This review evaluated their effect on heart rate measured while the intervention was being delivered.

### Methods
Randomized controlled trials enrolling infants born before 37 completed weeks were eligible if they compared recorded music, live singing, parental voice, or lullabies—with or without kangaroo care/skin-to-skin contact—with standard care, the usual neonatal intensive care environment, silence, or no music. The prespecified outcome was heart rate in beats per minute (bpm) during the intervention. The requested effect measure was the mean difference (MD), calculated as intervention minus control; therefore, an MD below 0 bpm favors music or voice. A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used.

### Results
The review pipeline contained 11 studies, of which 5 contributed effect sizes to the quantitative synthesis (250 infants). The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was 1.9303846362746506 bpm (95% CI −5.636 to 9.497; p = 0.617037776602486), favoring standard care/usual environment/silence/no music according to the supplied direction classification, but with substantial uncertainty and no statistically significant difference. Heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 87.43%; τ² = 60.551818; Q = 19.727, p = 0.0006). Egger’s intercept was 1.0428 (p = 0.7298), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0), although publication-bias analyses were underpowered with only five studies.

### Conclusions
The available randomized evidence does not establish that music or voice interventions reduce heart rate during intervention sessions in preterm infants. The pooled estimate was imprecise and accompanied by considerable between-study heterogeneity. Additional well-reported randomized trials using consistent intervention-period heart-rate measurements are required.

## Search/Screening

The eligibility framework was defined by the following criteria:

- **Population:** preterm infants born before 37 completed weeks of gestation.
- **Intervention:** recorded music, live singing, parental voice, or lullabies delivered during a session, with or without kangaroo care/skin-to-skin contact.
- **Comparator:** standard care, the usual neonatal intensive care unit (NICU) environment, silence, or no music.
- **Outcome:** heart rate, expressed in beats per minute (bpm), measured while the intervention was being delivered.
- **Design:** randomized controlled trials only.

Measurements obtained before a session, immediately after it, or at later follow-up were outside the outcome definition. Outcomes such as oxygen saturation, respiratory rate, pain, weight gain, length of stay, sleep, feeding, and neurodevelopment were not eligible for this synthesis.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was considerable:

- **I² = 87.43%**
- **τ² = 60.551818**
- **Cochran’s Q = 19.727**
- **Q-test p = 0.0006**

The Q test rejects the hypothesis that all observed variation is attributable to sampling error alone. The I² value indicates that a large proportion of observed variability was associated with between-study differences. The estimated between-study variance was also substantial on the MD scale. Accordingly, the pooled estimate should be understood as an average across heterogeneous trial settings and interventions rather than as a single effect expected in every NICU context.

Plausible sources of heterogeneity include the use of recorded versus live interventions, parental versus nonparental voice, music type, session duration, sound intensity, gestational or postmenstrual age, baseline clinical stability, concomitant kangaroo care, comparator noise conditions, timing and averaging of heart-rate measurements, and parallel versus cross-over trial designs. These explanations remain hypotheses because study-level characteristics were not supplied for formal evaluation.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **1.0428** with **p = 0.7298**. This was non-significant and did not provide evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies, and the supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **low**.

A trim-and-fill value of **0.4035** was also supplied under the field `trim_fill_g`. Because its scale was not identified as the native MD scale and no studies were imputed, it is not presented as an adjusted pooled MD and does not replace the primary REML result.

These findings require caution. Tests of funnel-plot asymmetry have low power when few studies are available, and only five studies entered this analysis. A non-significant Egger test therefore does not establish the absence of publication bias, selective outcome reporting, or other dissemination biases.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis found no statistically significant effect of music or voice interventions on preterm infants’ heart rate while an intervention was actively being delivered. The pooled Mean Difference was 1.9303846362746506 bpm, and its 95% confidence interval ranged from −5.636 to 9.497 bpm. Based on the supplied direction classification, the point estimate favored standard care/usual environment/silence/no music, although the interval was compatible with benefit in either direction and the p value was 0.617037776602486.

The central clinical message is therefore uncertainty rather than evidence of equivalence. Failure to reject the null hypothesis does not demonstrate that the interventions have no physiologic effect. The broad confidence interval and considerable heterogeneity indicate that the available data are compatible with materially different effects across settings or intervention types.

The heterogeneity is particularly important because “music or voice” encompasses clinically diverse exposures. Live parental singing may differ from recorded instrumental music in acoustic structure, infant familiarity, caregiver responsiveness, and integration with developmental care. Skin-to-skin contact can itself influence autonomic regulation and may modify or confound the incremental effect of music or voice when used concurrently. Variability in NICU background noise, infant maturity, respiratory support, medication, sleep state, and session length may further alter observed heart-rate responses.

Heart rate is also an intermediate physiologic outcome. A lower value is not inherently beneficial unless interpreted relative to baseline rate, gestational age, behavioral state, and clinical stability. The prespecified direction treats a lower intervention-period heart rate as favoring music or voice, but the clinical relevance of an approximately 2-bpm average difference cannot be judged confidently without contextual information and a prespecified minimally important difference.

Future trials should use concealed randomization, prespecified intervention-period windows, calibrated sound levels, and clear reporting of co-interventions. Reports should provide, for each randomized arm, the mean heart rate, standard deviation, and number analyzed during the intervention. Cross-over studies should additionally report paired-effect statistics or first-period data, within-participant correlations, sequence allocation, washout procedures, and tests for carryover. Consensus regarding the most clinically meaningful heart-rate summary would improve comparability across trials.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Several validation issues limit reproducibility and interpretation:

1. The pipeline listed 11 studies but only 5 effect sizes. Reasons why the other six studies did not contribute to pooling were not supplied.
2. Arm-level heart-rate means, standard deviations, and sample sizes were unavailable, so the raw MDs and any conversions from SEM/SE to SD could not be independently checked.
3. The study-level records were labeled as Hedges’ g, whereas the meta-analysis record specified native-scale MD pooling. The primary result was therefore reported according to the explicit meta-analysis specification, while the standardized study-level values were kept separate and were not described as bpm.
4. The available data did not indicate whether final values or change scores were used in each trial, whether shared controls were split, or whether any cross-over trials contributed first-period or paired results.
5. The trim-and-fill field `trim_fill_g = 0.4035` did not confirm a native MD scale. It was not treated as an adjusted bpm estimate.
6. Full bibliographic citations, detailed eligibility decisions, and risk-of-bias assessments were not supplied.

## Limitations

This report is limited by the aggregate nature of the supplied results. First, the search strategy and study-selection process were unavailable, preventing assessment of search completeness and full PRISMA flow reporting. Second, trial characteristics and risk-of-bias judgments were absent, so certainty of evidence could not be formally graded and the influence of allocation, blinding, attrition, or selective reporting could not be assessed.

Third, only five studies contributed data, limiting precision, moderator analysis, and the reliability of publication-bias tests. Fourth, heterogeneity was considerable, and no study-level covariates were available to explain it. Fifth, the mismatch between standardized study-level labels and the specified native-scale pooled MD prevents independent reconstruction of the synthesis. Sixth, no minimally important difference for heart rate was provided, limiting clinical interpretation of the confidence interval. Finally, the analysis concerns heart rate only during active intervention delivery and should not be extrapolated to post-session physiology, longer-term clinical outcomes, growth, feeding, neurodevelopment, pain, oxygenation, or length of hospitalization.

## Conclusion

Across five randomized trials involving 250 preterm infants, music or voice interventions did not produce a statistically significant difference in heart rate during intervention sessions compared with standard care, the usual NICU environment, silence, or no music. The REML random-effects pooled **Mean Difference was 1.9303846362746506 bpm (95% CI −5.636 to 9.497; p = 0.617037776602486)**, with the supplied direction classification favoring the comparator. Considerable heterogeneity (**I² = 87.43%**) and limited reporting substantially constrain interpretation. Better standardized and transparently reported randomized trials are needed before firm conclusions can be drawn about intervention-period cardiac effects.
