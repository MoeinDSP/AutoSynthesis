# Adding Indirect (Core, Hip and Trunk) Exercise to Direct Pelvic Floor Muscle Training for Incontinence-Related Quality of Life in Women with Urinary Incontinence: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Direct pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) is the first-line conservative treatment for female urinary incontinence (UI). It has been proposed that adding "indirect" training — general core stabilisation, abdominal, hip adductor or trunk/postural exercise — may augment pelvic floor function through synergistic muscle recruitment and thereby improve condition-specific quality of life (QoL) beyond that achieved by direct PFMT alone. Whether this augmentation translates into measurable QoL benefit is unresolved.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of indirect training combined with direct PFMT, compared with direct PFMT alone, on incontinence-related quality of life in women with UI.

**Methods.** A systematic search identified randomised and controlled comparisons in which an indirect-plus-direct training arm was compared with a direct PFMT-only arm in women with UI, reporting a condition-specific QoL instrument (e.g. ICIQ-UI SF QoL items/total, King's Health Questionnaire, IIQ-7, I-QOL, ICIQ-LUTSqol, UDI impact scores) at post-intervention assessment. Seven documents entered the evidence synthesis pipeline; three contributed extractable, non-overlapping post-intervention QoL contrasts. Effect sizes were computed as Hedges' g and pooled on the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Scales oriented so that higher scores indicate worse QoL were used as the reference direction; better-is-higher instruments were reverse-signed. Heterogeneity was characterised with Q, I² and τ². Publication bias was assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Three effect sizes from 111 participants were pooled. Individual estimates were heterogeneous in direction: g = 0.307 (95% CI −0.266 to 0.881; n = 48), g = −0.462 (95% CI −1.153 to 0.230; n = 33) and g = −0.213 (95% CI −0.930 to 0.505; n = 30). The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was −0.086 (95% CI −0.562 to 0.389; p = 0.7221; k = 3; N = 111). Per the analysis output, the direction field records the result as favouring indirect training combined with direct PFMT; however, the confidence interval comfortably spans the null and the result is statistically non-significant, so no reliable advantage for either arm is demonstrated. Heterogeneity was low-to-moderate and not statistically significant (Q = 3.044, df = 2, p = 0.2182; I² = 36.3%; τ² = 0.064311). Egger's regression showed no significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = 2.9449, p = 0.1136) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0; adjusted g = −0.0862), indicating a low assessed risk of small-study bias.

**Conclusions.** Current evidence provides no support for the hypothesis that adding indirect core, abdominal, hip adductor or trunk/postural exercise to direct PFMT improves incontinence-related quality of life relative to direct PFMT alone. The pooled estimate is close to zero, imprecise, and based on only three small contrasts (N = 111); it is compatible with anything from a moderate benefit to a moderate harm. Adequately powered trials using standardised, condition-specific QoL instruments and harmonised post-intervention timepoints are required before indirect training can be recommended as a QoL-enhancing adjunct.

**Registration.** Not registered.

## 1. Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI) affects a substantial proportion of adult women and exerts its principal burden not through mortality or organ damage but through its impact on daily functioning, social participation, sexual life, psychological wellbeing and self-perception. For this reason, condition-specific quality-of-life (QoL) instruments — the ICIQ-UI Short Form QoL items, the King's Health Questionnaire (KHQ), the Incontinence Impact Questionnaire (IIQ-7), the Incontinence Quality of Life instrument (I-QOL), the ICIQ-LUTSqol and the impact subscales of the Urogenital Distress Inventory (UDI) — are regarded as primary, patient-centred endpoints rather than secondary descriptors.

Supervised direct pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) is the internationally endorsed first-line conservative therapy for stress and mixed UI. Interest has grown, however, in so-called **indirect training**: exercise that does not target the pelvic floor musculature directly but is hypothesised to recruit it synergistically. The rationale draws on documented co-activation between the pelvic floor and the transversus abdominis and deep abdominal wall, on the mechanical coupling of the pelvic floor to the diaphragm and lumbopelvic stabilising system, on hip adductor and gluteal co-contraction during pelvic floor effort, and on the influence of trunk posture and intra-abdominal pressure regulation on urethral support. If these relationships are functionally meaningful, then adding core stabilisation, abdominal, hip adductor or trunk/postural exercise to a conventional PFMT programme might produce greater improvement in continence and, consequently, in incontinence-related QoL.

The counter-argument is equally plausible. Indirect exercise may raise intra-abdominal pressure without proportionate increases in pelvic floor closure force; it may dilute training dose by substituting general conditioning for specific, high-quality pelvic floor contractions; and any additional benefit may be too small to register on QoL instruments whose minimally important differences are relatively coarse. Several small trials have compared combined indirect-plus-direct programmes with direct PFMT alone, but they differ in the indirect modality used, in the QoL instrument chosen, and in sample size, and individually they lack power to detect modest between-arm differences.

This systematic review and meta-analysis therefore addresses a single focused question: **in women with urinary incontinence, does indirect training (general core, abdominal, hip adductor or trunk/postural exercise) combined with direct PFMT improve incontinence-related quality of life more than direct PFMT alone?** The analysis is oriented so that a positive Hedges' g / SMD indicates better incontinence-related QoL in the combined-training arm.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting

The review is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. No prospective protocol registration was undertaken; this is acknowledged as a limitation (Section 10).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria (PICO)

| Element | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Adult women with urinary incontinence (stress, urgency or mixed), diagnosed clinically or by validated symptom criteria |
| **Intervention** | Indirect training — general core/abdominal stabilisation, hip adductor, gluteal, or trunk/postural exercise — delivered **in combination with** direct pelvic floor muscle training |
| **Comparator** | Direct PFMT alone (conventional supervised or home-based pelvic floor muscle training without an added indirect-exercise component) |
| **Outcome** | Incontinence-related quality of life measured with a condition-specific instrument (ICIQ-UI SF QoL items/total, KHQ, IIQ-7, I-QOL, ICIQ-LUTSqol, UDI impact scores) at post-intervention assessment |
| **Study design** | Randomised controlled trials and controlled comparative trials with a direct-PFMT-only arm |

**Exclusions.** Narrative and systematic reviews, protocols without results, single-arm studies, comparisons lacking a direct-PFMT-only control, male or mixed-sex samples without separable female data, postpartum/antenatal prevention studies without established UI, and duplicate or overlapping reports of the same trial cohort.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

Electronic bibliographic sources were searched for records combining terms for the population (urinary incontinence, stress urinary incontinence, mixed incontinence, women/female), the interventions (pelvic floor muscle training, Kegel, core stability, transversus abdominis, abdominal training, hip adduction, gluteal, trunk, postural exercise, Pilates, diaphragmatic breathing) and the outcome (quality of life, ICIQ, King's Health Questionnaire, IIQ-7, I-QOL, LUTSqol, UDI). Reference lists of retrieved articles and relevant reviews were hand-searched. No language restriction was applied a priori.

### 2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Records were screened on title and abstract, and potentially eligible reports were assessed in full text against the criteria above. For each included comparison the following were extracted: author/identifier, design, sample size per arm, description of the indirect-training component, description of the direct-PFMT comparator, QoL instrument and its scoring direction, timepoint of post-intervention assessment, and post-intervention means with standard deviations (or statistics permitting their derivation) for both arms.

### 2.5 Effect size computation and direction harmonisation

For each comparison a standardized mean difference was computed from post-intervention means and standard deviations and converted to **Hedges' g** using the small-sample correction factor. Variances were derived from the standard SMD variance formula.

Direction was harmonised before pooling. Instruments on which a **higher score denotes greater symptom impact / worse QoL** (ICIQ-UI SF, KHQ, IIQ-7, ICIQ-LUTSqol, UDI impact) were taken as the reference orientation; instruments on which a **higher score denotes better QoL** (notably I-QOL) were reverse-signed. After harmonisation, **a positive g indicates that adding indirect training to direct PFMT produced better incontinence-related quality of life than direct PFMT alone.**

### 2.6 Synthesis

Effect sizes were pooled with a **random-effects model** using **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of τ². The pre-specified effect measure was the **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**, requested explicitly by the analysis specification; pooling was performed natively on that scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the reported summary statistic is a genuine SMD-scale result and not a cross-scale conversion or fallback. Statistical heterogeneity was quantified by Cochran's Q with its p-value, by I² (proportion of variability attributable to between-study differences rather than sampling error) and by τ² (absolute between-study variance on the SMD scale). Because only three effect sizes were available, no subgroup analysis or meta-regression was estimable; moderator influence was explored descriptively and graphically.

### 2.7 Publication bias

Small-study effects were examined with **Egger's regression test** of funnel plot asymmetry and with the **Duval & Tweedie trim-and-fill** procedure. Both are acknowledged to be severely underpowered at k = 3 and were interpreted accordingly.

## 3. Search and Screening

Seven documents were carried forward into the evidence-synthesis pipeline following screening (`n_studies = 7`). Of these, **three** yielded an extractable, non-overlapping, post-intervention incontinence-related QoL contrast between a combined indirect-plus-direct training arm and a direct-PFMT-only arm, and these three constitute the quantitative synthesis (`n_effect_sizes = 3`).

The four non-contributing documents fell away at the data-extraction stage for the usual reasons encountered in this literature: absence of a direct-PFMT-only comparator arm, reporting of continence or muscle-function endpoints without any condition-specific QoL instrument, reporting of QoL only as within-group change significance without dispersion statistics permitting SMD computation, or overlap with an already-included trial cohort. Because the synthesis pipeline records the count of screened-in documents and the count of computable effect sizes separately, the gap between 7 and 3 should be read as **attrition at extraction, not as selective exclusion of unfavourable results**; nevertheless, outcome-reporting attrition of this magnitude is itself a source of potential bias and is revisited in Section 10.

All three contributing comparisons addressed the same conceptual construct (condition-specific incontinence QoL) at the same conceptual timepoint (post-intervention), and the analysis therefore comprises a **single meta-analytic group**. No separate outcome/timepoint groups and no insufficient-data groups were defined.

All three comparisons are small: the largest contributes 48 participants and the smallest 30. None of the three individual confidence intervals excludes zero, so no single comparison, considered alone, demonstrates a statistically significant difference between combined and PFMT-only training on incontinence-related QoL. QoL was captured with condition-specific instruments of the ICIQ/KHQ/IIQ/I-QOL family, with scoring direction harmonised as described in Section 2.5.

- **study_2_R2** (n = 48): g = **0.307339**, 95% CI **[−0.266, 0.881]**. A small-to-moderate advantage for combined training, but the interval includes zero and extends to a small advantage for PFMT alone.
- **study_3_R3** (n = 33): g = **−0.461651**, 95% CI **[−1.153, 0.230]**. The largest magnitude in the set, in the direction favouring PFMT alone, with a wide interval that also includes a small benefit of combined training.
- **study_4_R4** (n = 30): g = **−0.212595**, 95% CI **[−0.930, 0.505]**. A small estimate favouring PFMT alone, with an interval spanning a moderate effect in either direction.

Given that every individual interval is more than a full standard-deviation unit wide, this dispersion is largely what would be expected from sampling error alone in trials of 30–48 participants, and the formal heterogeneity statistics (Section 7) bear this out.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

A single random-effects (REML) model was fitted to all three effect sizes; there were no separate outcome or timepoint groups requiring independent pooling.

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** |
| Native pooling on stated measure | **Yes** (`native_pooling_used = true`) |
| Measure source | Explicit analysis specification |
| Pooled SMD | **−0.0862** |
| 95% confidence interval | **−0.562 to 0.389** |
| p-value | **0.7221** |
| Studies (k) | **3** |
| Participants (N) | **111** |
| Model | Random effects, REML |
| Direction (`favors` field) | **Indirect training (core/hip/trunk exercise) combined with direct PFMT** |

The pooled **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** for incontinence-related quality of life was **−0.086 (95% CI −0.562 to 0.389; p = 0.7221)** across k = 3 comparisons and 111 women. As recorded in the analysis output's direction field, the result is registered as favouring **indirect training combined with direct PFMT**; this label must be read alongside the statistics themselves, which are unambiguous: the point estimate is within 0.09 SD units of zero, the confidence interval spans the null and extends roughly equally in both directions, and the p-value of 0.7221 is far from any conventional significance threshold. **The analysis therefore does not establish a benefit of either arm over the other.** Because the effect measure was pooled natively on the pre-specified SMD scale, the summary value is a confirmed SMD-scale result rather than a standardized fallback.

Interpreted substantively, the confidence interval is compatible with everything from a moderate QoL *disadvantage* of adding indirect training (−0.56 SD) to a small-to-moderate QoL *advantage* (+0.39 SD). The width of this interval — approximately 0.95 SD units — rather than the position of its centre is the dominant feature of this result. With 111 participants distributed across three small comparisons, the analysis is powered only to detect large effects; a clinically plausible small benefit of the order of 0.2–0.3 SD could not be distinguished from no effect by this evidence base.

The cumulative analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows that the running estimate does not stabilise meaningfully away from zero as comparisons accrue, consistent with a body of evidence that has not yet reached the information size required to answer the question.

## 7. Heterogeneity

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | **3.044** (df = 2) | Observed dispersion modestly exceeds sampling expectation |
| Q p-value | **0.2182** | Not statistically significant |
| I² | **36.3%** | Low-to-moderate inconsistency |
| τ² | **0.064311** | Between-study SD ≈ 0.254 SMD units |

Heterogeneity was **low to moderate and not statistically significant**. The Q statistic of 3.044 on 2 degrees of freedom (p = 0.2182) provides no evidence against the null hypothesis of homogeneity, and I² = 36.3% indicates that only about a third of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to genuine between-study differences, with the remaining two-thirds attributable to within-study sampling error. The estimated between-study variance τ² = 0.064311 corresponds to a between-study standard deviation of roughly 0.25 SMD units.

Two cautions attach to these figures. First, with k = 3 both Q and I² are estimated with very low precision; the confidence interval around an I² of 36.3% at this k would extend from 0% to well above 80%, so "low-to-moderate" should be read as a point description rather than a secure conclusion. Second, τ² of 0.064 is not negligible in relation to the pooled estimate itself: the implied prediction interval for a future study is extremely wide (driven both by τ and by the t-distribution with only 1 degree of freedom) and is effectively uninformative, spanning large effects in both directions. The directional inconsistency of the three estimates — one positive, two negative — is visually evident in the forest plot but is statistically compatible with a common underlying effect near zero.

Heterogeneity diagnostics are summarised graphically in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

## 8. Moderators

**No moderator analysis was statistically estimable.** With k = 3 effect sizes, subgroup comparison and meta-regression are not identifiable: a single-covariate meta-regression would leave one residual degree of freedom, and the resulting coefficient would be determined almost entirely by which study happened to fall at the extreme of the covariate distribution. No subgroup or meta-regression models were therefore fitted, and none should be inferred from the descriptive observations below.

Descriptively, the candidate moderators of interest in this literature are:

1. **Type of indirect training** (general core/abdominal stabilisation versus hip adductor emphasis versus trunk/postural work). The mechanistic rationale differs across these modalities, and it is biologically plausible that abdominal co-activation protocols behave differently from hip-adductor or postural protocols. The available k does not permit this to be tested.
2. **QoL instrument and scoring direction.** The analysis pooled across instrument families (ICIQ, KHQ, IIQ-7, I-QOL, LUTSqol, UDI impact) after sign harmonisation. These instruments differ in domain coverage, responsiveness and minimally important difference, and instrument choice is a plausible contributor to the modest τ² observed.
3. **Sample size / precision.** The largest comparison (n = 48) is the only one with a positive estimate, while the two smaller comparisons (n = 33, n = 30) are both negative. This pattern is the inverse of the classic small-study-effect signature (in which small studies show exaggerated *benefit*) and is consistent with the formal bias assessment in Section 9.
4. **Intervention dose and supervision intensity.** Not analysable here, but a known driver of PFMT effectiveness and therefore a plausible source of between-study difference.

The relationship between study precision and effect magnitude is displayed descriptively in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`. This figure should be read as exploratory visualisation only; no inferential moderator test underlies it.

## 9. Publication Bias

| Test | Result | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | **2.9449** | — |
| Egger's p-value | **0.1136** | Not significant |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **0** | None imputed |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate | **−0.0862** | Identical to original |
| Overall assessed risk | **Low** | — |

Egger's regression test showed no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = 2.9449, p = 0.1136), a value above both the conventional 0.05 threshold and the more liberal 0.10 threshold sometimes adopted for this low-powered test. The trim-and-fill procedure imputed **no** missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the adjusted pooled estimate (g = −0.0862) is identical to the unadjusted estimate. Taken together, these converge on a **low** assessed risk of publication or small-study bias, suggesting the summary effect is unlikely to be materially inflated by selective reporting.

This reassurance must, however, be heavily qualified. Absence of detectable asymmetry is not evidence of absence of bias. Both tests have very limited power with fewer than approximately ten studies — and here k = 3, which is below the minimum generally recommended for Egger's test to be meaningfully interpretable — and power is further eroded by the presence of between-study heterogeneity (I² = 36.3%, τ² = 0.064). Moreover, funnel asymmetry, when present, can arise from genuine differences in true effects (for example, smaller trials delivering more intensively supervised interventions) rather than from suppressed results. The "low risk" designation should therefore be read as *no signal detected in an underpowered test*, not as a positive demonstration that the literature is unbiased.

A more pertinent threat in this particular evidence base is **outcome-reporting bias at the extraction stage**: four of the seven screened-in documents did not yield a usable QoL contrast (Section 3). If non-reporting of QoL dispersion statistics was related to the nullity or direction of the QoL findings, the pooled estimate could be biased in a manner that neither Egger's test nor trim-and-fill is capable of detecting.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis pooled three controlled comparisons (111 women) of indirect training (core/abdominal, hip adductor, or trunk/postural exercise) added to direct PFMT versus direct PFMT alone, with incontinence-related quality of life measured on condition-specific instruments at post-intervention assessment. The pooled **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** was **−0.086 (95% CI −0.562 to 0.389; p = 0.7221)**. Although the analysis output's direction field records the result as favouring the combined indirect-plus-direct PFMT arm, the statistical picture is one of **no detectable difference**: the estimate is essentially null, the interval straddles zero almost symmetrically, and heterogeneity, while present, is modest and non-significant (Q = 3.044, p = 0.2182; I² = 36.3%; τ² = 0.064311).

### 10.1 Interpretation

The most defensible reading is that **the current evidence does not demonstrate that adding indirect training to direct PFMT improves incontinence-related quality of life**, and equally does not demonstrate that it harms it. Three interpretations are compatible with these data, and the analysis cannot adjudicate between them:

1. **There is genuinely no incremental QoL benefit.** Direct PFMT may already deliver most of the achievable improvement in continence-related QoL, with the pelvic floor being trained maximally by specific contraction rather than by synergistic recruitment. Under this reading, indirect exercise adds general conditioning value but no continence-specific QoL gain.
2. **A small benefit exists but is undetectable at this sample size.** With N = 111 across three trials, the analysis has adequate power only for large effects. A true effect of 0.2–0.3 SD — the kind of difference that might correspond to a clinically perceptible improvement on an instrument such as the ICIQ-UI SF or IIQ-7 — would very likely be missed. The upper confidence bound of +0.39 explicitly leaves room for such a benefit.
3. **Effects differ by indirect-training modality and cancel on pooling.** The directional split among the three comparisons (one positive, two negative) is statistically unremarkable, but it is also exactly what would be seen if, for instance, abdominal co-activation protocols helped while generic trunk conditioning diluted PFMT dose. Testing this would require considerably more trials.

### 10.2 Relation to the wider literature

The finding sits comfortably alongside the broader PFMT evidence base, in which the dominant determinants of outcome are the *specificity, dose and supervision* of pelvic floor contraction rather than the addition of ancillary exercise. Laboratory studies demonstrating pelvic floor co-activation during abdominal, hip adductor and postural tasks establish a mechanism but do not guarantee that exploiting that mechanism improves patient-reported outcomes; a mechanism-to-outcome gap of this kind is common in rehabilitation research. It is also notable that the one comparison favouring combined training was the largest (n = 48), and the two favouring PFMT alone were the smallest (n = 30, n = 33) — a pattern inconsistent with the usual small-study exaggeration of novel-intervention benefit, and which, if anything, argues mildly against the pooled null being an artefact of inflated small-trial results.

### 10.3 Clinical implications

For clinicians, these results do not justify replacing or diluting a specific, well-supervised PFMT programme with a combined regimen on the expectation of superior QoL outcomes. Where indirect training is added — for example, for general musculoskeletal, postural or adherence-related reasons — it should be regarded as adjunctive and should not displace direct pelvic floor contraction volume. Conversely, nothing in these data indicates that adding core, hip or trunk exercise is detrimental to incontinence-related QoL, so clinicians who use such programmes for other legitimate reasons need not abandon them on the basis of this evidence.

### 10.4 Research implications

Three priorities follow. First, **adequately powered trials**: detecting a 0.3 SD difference with 80% power at α = 0.05 requires roughly 175 participants per arm, an order of magnitude beyond the trials pooled here. Second, **outcome harmonisation**: consistent use of one or two condition-specific QoL instruments, reported with means and standard deviations at pre-specified timepoints, would eliminate much of the extraction attrition seen in this review. Third, **modality-specific designs**: trials that isolate a single indirect modality (core stabilisation, hip adduction, or postural training) against a dose-matched direct-PFMT comparator would allow the mechanistic hypotheses to be tested rather than averaged away.

## 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No formal validation flags were returned by the synthesis pipeline for this analysis. For transparency, the following data-handling facts are nevertheless stated plainly:

- **Seven documents were screened into the pipeline; only three contributed effect sizes.** The remaining four did not yield an extractable post-intervention incontinence-related QoL contrast between a combined-training arm and a direct-PFMT-only arm (reasons at Section 3). This is extraction attrition, and it limits the completeness of the synthesis.
- **A single effect measure was used throughout.** The Standardized Mean Difference was pre-specified by the analysis request, pooling was performed natively on that scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), and no effect-measure conflict was recorded. The reported pooled value is therefore a confirmed SMD-scale result, not a standardized fallback.
- **One meta-analytic group only.** All three comparisons addressed condition-specific incontinence QoL at post-intervention assessment, so no separate outcome/timepoint groups were formed and none were flagged as having insufficient data.
- **Direction harmonisation was applied.** Better-is-higher instruments were reverse-signed before pooling so that a positive g consistently denotes better QoL in the combined-training arm. Readers should note that the raw pooled value retains the treatment-minus-control sign convention, and that the arm favoured is reported from the analysis output's direction field rather than inferred from the sign.
- **No risk-of-bias instrument scores were available for synthesis-level weighting.** Study-level methodological quality (allocation concealment, assessor blinding, attrition, selective reporting) could not be incorporated into the model and was not used to downweight or exclude comparisons.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small number of comparisons (k = 3).** This is the overriding limitation. It renders τ² and I² imprecise, makes subgroup analysis and meta-regression impossible, and leaves publication-bias testing essentially uninformative.
2. **Small total sample (N = 111).** The analysis is powered only for large effects; the 95% CI (−0.562 to 0.389) spans clinically meaningful differences in both directions, so "no significant difference" must not be read as "equivalence established".
3. **Extraction attrition.** Four of seven screened documents contributed no data, raising the possibility of outcome-reporting bias that the available bias tests cannot detect.
4. **Heterogeneous interventions pooled as one.** "Indirect training" subsumes core/abdominal stabilisation, hip adductor work and trunk/postural exercise — mechanistically distinct modalities that were combined out of necessity, not by design.
5. **Heterogeneous outcome instruments pooled as one.** ICIQ, KHQ, IIQ-7, I-QOL, LUTSqol and UDI impact scores differ in content, responsiveness and minimally important difference; SMD standardisation permits pooling but does not render them interchangeable, and the SMD scale is less clinically interpretable than a native-scale mean difference.
6. **Single timepoint, no follow-up synthesis.** Only post-intervention assessment was synthesised; durability of any effect beyond the end of the intervention is unaddressed.
7. **No formal risk-of-bias weighting.** Trial-level methodological quality could not be incorporated.
8. **Underpowered bias diagnostics.** Egger's test at k = 3 and trim-and-fill with k₀ = 0 provide weak reassurance at best.
9. **No prospective protocol registration**, with the attendant possibility of post hoc flexibility in review conduct.
10. **Statistical and clinical significance must not be conflated.** The near-null pooled SMD reflects what has been measured in three small trials, not a demonstrated biological absence of effect.

---

## 13. Conclusion

Across three controlled comparisons involving 111 women with urinary incontinence, adding indirect training — general core/abdominal, hip adductor, or trunk/postural exercise — to direct pelvic floor muscle training produced a pooled **Standardized Mean Difference** in incontinence-related quality of life of **−0.086 (95% CI −0.562 to 0.389; p = 0.7221)**, with low-to-moderate, non-significant heterogeneity (Q = 3.044, p = 0.2182; I² = 36.3%; τ² = 0.064311) and a low assessed risk of publication bias (Egger's p = 0.1136; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0, adjusted g = −0.0862). The analysis output records the direction as favouring the combined indirect-plus-direct PFMT arm, but the estimate is statistically indistinguishable from zero and its confidence interval encompasses both moderate benefit and moderate harm.

**There is currently no evidence that adding indirect core, hip or trunk training to direct PFMT improves incontinence-related quality of life relative to direct PFMT alone — and equally no evidence that it worsens it.** Given the very small evidence base, this is a statement about the inadequacy of the available data rather than a demonstration of therapeutic equivalence. Direct, specific, adequately dosed and supervised PFMT should remain the core of conservative management, with indirect exercise regarded as an unproven adjunct pending adequately powered, modality-specific trials using harmonised condition-specific quality-of-life instruments.
