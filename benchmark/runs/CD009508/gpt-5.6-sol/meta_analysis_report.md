# Indirect Core, Hip, or Trunk Exercise Added to Pelvic Floor Muscle Training for Incontinence-Related Quality of Life in Women: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) is a conservative treatment for urinary incontinence. Whether adding indirect core, hip, or trunk exercise improves incontinence-related quality of life beyond PFMT alone remains uncertain.

### Objective
To quantify the effect of indirect core, hip, or trunk exercise combined with direct PFMT, compared with direct PFMT alone, on incontinence-related quality of life among women with urinary incontinence.

### Methods
Only quantitative incontinence-specific quality-of-life or life-impact outcomes were eligible; urinary-incontinence frequency, leakage, pad-test results, pelvic-floor strength, subjective improvement, satisfaction, and other outcomes were outside the target outcome. The supplied evidence pipeline contained seven studies, of which four contributed one effect size each to the quantitative synthesis. These four comparisons included 170 participants. Because the native effect measure could not be confirmed, outcomes were combined using a standardized fallback, Hedges' g, under a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). A positive effect was defined as favoring the combined intervention. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran's Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were explored with Egger's test and trim-and-fill.

### Results
The pooled Hedges' g was 0.031601 (95% CI −0.320 to 0.384; p=0.860362; k=4; N=170), favoring indirect core, hip, or trunk exercise combined with direct PFMT according to the prespecified direction field, but the estimate was very close to the null and the confidence interval included effects in both directions. Heterogeneity was low to modest (I²=24.29%; τ²=0.03153; Q=4.031, p=0.2581). Egger's intercept was 2.0217 (p=0.0767). Trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study and produced an adjusted Hedges' g of 0.1559. Publication bias was classified as a moderate concern.

### Conclusions
Based on four comparisons and 170 participants, adding indirect core, hip, or trunk exercise to direct PFMT did not demonstrate a statistically significant improvement in incontinence-related quality of life over direct PFMT alone. The estimate was imprecise, the native outcome metric was unresolved, and the small number of studies limits heterogeneity, moderator, and publication-bias assessments.

## Introduction

Urinary incontinence can adversely affect physical activity, social participation, emotional well-being, and daily functioning. Direct pelvic floor muscle training (PFMT) is a central conservative intervention. Programs sometimes supplement PFMT with exercises that do not themselves consist of pelvic floor contractions, including general core, hip, and trunk training. Such indirect exercise could plausibly improve lumbopelvic control or facilitate effective exercise participation, but its incremental effect on patient-important, incontinence-related quality of life must be distinguished from effects on pelvic-floor strength, leakage, or symptom frequency.

This review addressed the following question: among women with urinary incontinence, what is the effect on incontinence-related quality of life of adding indirect core, hip, or trunk exercise to direct PFMT, compared with direct PFMT alone without an added indirect-training component?

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

The intervention of interest was a program combining indirect core, hip, or trunk exercise that was not itself a pelvic floor muscle contraction with direct PFMT. The comparator was direct PFMT alone, without an added indirect-training component. Participants were women with urinary incontinence.

The single target outcome was incontinence-related quality of life measured quantitatively with a validated or study-defined incontinence-specific quality-of-life or life-impact instrument. Urinary-incontinence episode frequency, leakage amount, pad-test outcomes, pelvic-floor strength, subjective cure or improvement, satisfaction, and other outcomes were not eligible for pooling as the target outcome.

Where multiple eligible measures or time points were available, the intended dependency rule was to select the primary validated incontinence-specific measure at the primary or end-of-treatment assessment, thereby retaining no more than one effect per independent comparison. The supplied quantitative dataset contains one effect size for each of the four pooled studies, so no participant group was counted more than once in the model.

### Information sources and selection process

The supplied results report that seven studies entered the evidence pipeline and that four study-level effect sizes were available for meta-analysis. Search databases, search dates, complete strategies, numbers of records before study-level consolidation, reasons for exclusion at each screening stage, and the disposition of the other three studies were not supplied. Consequently, those PRISMA flow details cannot be reconstructed without inventing information. The numerical synthesis is therefore limited to the four studies for which eligible, computable quality-of-life effects were provided.

## Effect Sizes

The supplied analysis represented each study using Hedges' g. Under the specified orientation, a positive Hedges' g indicates better incontinence-related quality of life with indirect core, hip, or trunk exercise combined with direct PFMT than with direct PFMT alone.

The individual estimates ranged from −0.461651 to 0.307339. Two estimates were positive (`study_1_R1`, 0.307339; `study_5_R5`, 0.259547), and two were negative (`study_3_R3`, −0.461651; `study_4_R4`, −0.212595). Every individual 95% confidence interval crossed zero. Thus, none of the four study-level estimates independently demonstrated a statistically clear between-group difference.

Importantly, the source effect measure was coded as `UNKNOWN`, and native-scale pooling was not used. Hedges' g was an unresolved standardized fallback rather than a confirmed native-scale effect. This allowed differently scaled quality-of-life results to be represented in a common standardized metric, but it reduces clinical interpretability and prevents translation into points on a particular quality-of-life instrument or comparison with an instrument-specific minimally important difference.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects meta-analysis using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) included four independent effects and 170 participants. The pooled Hedges' g was **0.031601146037016366** (95% CI **−0.320 to 0.384**; p=**0.8603622339973489**). According to the supplied direction field, the point estimate favors **indirect core, hip, or trunk exercise combined with direct PFMT**. Nevertheless, its magnitude was nearly null, and the confidence interval was compatible with a modest effect favoring either intervention.

Accordingly, the available evidence does not demonstrate that adding indirect exercise improves incontinence-related quality of life compared with direct PFMT alone. Failure to reject the null hypothesis should not be interpreted as proof of equivalence or absence of any clinically relevant effect, because the confidence interval remains comparatively wide and only four studies were available.

## Heterogeneity

Observed heterogeneity was low to modest:

- Cochran's Q=**4.031** with p=**0.2581**;
- I²=**24.29%**;
- τ²=**0.03153**.

The Q test did not detect statistically significant heterogeneity, although it has limited power when only four studies are included. I² suggests that approximately one quarter of the observed variance may reflect between-study differences rather than sampling error under the assumptions of the model. The positive τ² estimate likewise indicates some estimated between-study dispersion. Therefore, the non-significant Q result should not be taken as evidence that the interventions, populations, outcome instruments, and study methods were identical.

## Moderators

No moderator analysis was supported by the supplied data. Potentially relevant modifiers—such as incontinence subtype, baseline severity, age, exercise type, intervention dose and duration, adherence, quality-of-life instrument, and risk of bias—were not available at study level. Moreover, with k=4, meta-regression or extensive subgroup analysis would be statistically unstable and prone to spurious findings. ## Publication Bias

Egger's regression yielded an intercept of **2.0217** with p=**0.0767**. This result was not statistically significant at the conventional 0.05 threshold, but it suggests possible asymmetry. The trim-and-fill procedure estimated **one** potentially missing study and returned an adjusted Hedges' g of **0.1559**. The supplied assessment classified publication bias as a **moderate** concern that could slightly affect the pooled estimate.

These diagnostics warrant caution. With only four observed effects, Egger's test has low power and unstable type I error, while trim-and-fill can perform poorly when asymmetry is caused by heterogeneity or chance rather than selective publication. The adjusted value should consequently be treated as a sensitivity estimate, not as a corrected or definitive treatment effect.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This synthesis found little evidence that adding indirect core, hip, or trunk exercise to direct PFMT improves incontinence-related quality of life beyond direct PFMT alone. The random-effects point estimate was extremely small (Hedges' g=0.031601146037016366), statistically non-significant (p=0.8603622339973489), and accompanied by a 95% confidence interval spanning −0.320 to 0.384. Although the supplied direction field identifies the point estimate as favoring the combined program, its proximity to zero makes the average observed incremental benefit negligible on the standardized scale.

The individual results were mixed: two effects favored the combined intervention under the prespecified sign convention and two favored PFMT alone. All four confidence intervals included zero. Low-to-modest heterogeneity suggests that the studies did not differ dramatically in their estimated effects, but uncertainty is substantial because k was small.

### Clinical interpretation

The findings concern only incontinence-related quality of life. They do not establish whether the combined intervention affects leakage frequency, leakage volume, pad-test performance, pelvic-floor strength, perceived cure or improvement, adherence, satisfaction, or safety. Those outcomes were deliberately outside the target synthesis and should not be used to reinterpret the present estimate.

The standardized pooled result cannot be mapped directly onto a named questionnaire because the contributing instruments and native metrics were not supplied and the effect measure was unresolved. Consequently, the clinical importance of the confidence limits cannot be judged against a scale-specific minimally important difference. At present, the evidence does not support claiming superior quality-of-life benefit from routinely adding indirect training to PFMT, but it also does not establish equivalence between the programs.

### Implications for research

Future trials should prespecify one primary, validated incontinence-specific quality-of-life instrument and report group sample sizes, means, and standard deviations at baseline and end of treatment, as well as change-score correlations where change scores are analyzed. Trialists should describe the direct PFMT and indirect exercise components separately, report adherence and co-interventions, and retain sufficient follow-up to determine whether any quality-of-life effect persists. Larger trials and prospectively registered syntheses would improve precision and reduce concerns about selective reporting. Availability of native-scale data would permit clinically interpretable mean differences when the same instrument is used.

## Data Quality and Validation Considerations

No formal `validation_flags` field was supplied. Nevertheless, the results themselves identify an important measurement limitation: the original effect measure was unresolved (`effect_measure: UNKNOWN`), `native_pooling_used` was false, and Hedges' g was used as a standardized fallback. Seven studies were present in the pipeline, but only four effect sizes were synthesized; the reasons the other three studies did not contribute were not provided. Bibliographic details and instrument names were also unavailable, preventing independent confirmation of eligibility, outcome classification, and duplicate-report handling.

## Limitations

1. **Small evidence base:** Only four studies and 170 participants contributed to the pooled estimate, limiting precision and the reliability of heterogeneity and small-study-effect tests.
2. **Unresolved effect metric:** Hedges' g was a standardized fallback rather than a confirmed native-scale measure. The pooled estimate is therefore less clinically interpretable.
3. **Incomplete study information:** Study identities, designs, settings, participant characteristics, intervention details, outcome instruments, and follow-up times were not supplied.
4. **Incomplete search and screening documentation:** Databases, search dates, search strategies, screening counts, and exclusion reasons were unavailable. Full PRISMA flow reporting and assessment of search completeness were not possible.
5. **Unexplained attrition from the evidence pipeline:** Seven studies were reported in the pipeline, whereas four contributed effects. The status and exclusion reasons for the remaining three were not provided.
6. **Risk of bias unavailable:** No domain-level or overall risk-of-bias assessments were supplied, so the extent to which randomization, deviations from intervention, missing data, outcome measurement, or selective reporting may have influenced the result is unknown.
7. **No certainty assessment:** The evidence could not be formally graded because key information needed to evaluate risk of bias, indirectness, inconsistency, imprecision, and publication bias was incomplete.
8. **Publication-bias diagnostics are underpowered:** Egger's test and trim-and-fill are not dependable with only four observed effects; the moderate-concern rating should be interpreted cautiously.
9. **No moderator analysis:** Potential clinical and methodological explanations for differing effects could not be evaluated.
10. **Time-point detail unavailable:** Although one effect per study prevented double-counting in the supplied synthesis, the exact assessment times and whether each represented the primary or end-of-treatment assessment could not be independently verified.

## Conclusion

Across four studies with 170 participants, indirect core, hip, or trunk exercise combined with direct PFMT did not produce a statistically significant improvement in incontinence-related quality of life compared with direct PFMT alone. The REML pooled Hedges' g was 0.031601146037016366 (95% CI −0.320 to 0.384; p=0.8603622339973489), with low-to-modest heterogeneity (I²=24.29%; τ²=0.03153). The point estimate was designated as favoring the combined program, but it was nearly null and imprecise. The unresolved native effect measure, incomplete study-level reporting, small evidence base, and moderate concern about publication bias preclude strong conclusions. Better reported and adequately powered trials using prespecified, validated incontinence-specific quality-of-life outcomes are needed.
