# Higher versus Lower Blood Pressure Treatment Targets and All-Cause Mortality in Older Adults with Hypertension: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Randomised Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Guideline recommendations for blood pressure (BP) targets in older adults remain contested. Whether a less intensive target (approximately 150–160/95–100 mmHg) confers a mortality advantage or disadvantage relative to a conventional target of approximately 140/90 mmHg is a question of direct clinical and public-health relevance.

**Objective.** To quantify, by meta-analysis of randomised controlled trials, the effect of randomisation to a higher (less intensive) BP treatment target compared with a lower (more intensive) target of approximately 140/90 mmHg on all-cause mortality in older adults with hypertension.

**Methods.** A systematic search for randomised trials comparing explicitly defined higher versus lower BP treatment targets in older hypertensive populations was conducted and records were screened against pre-specified eligibility criteria. The outcome was death from any cause during trial follow-up, extracted as the number of deaths and the number randomised in each target arm. Cardiovascular mortality, non-cardiovascular mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure and adverse events were outside the scope of this analysis. Effects were pooled natively on the risk-ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** Four trials contributing four effect estimates and 16,732 randomised participants were included. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) for all-cause mortality with the higher BP target versus the lower target was 1.130 (95% CI 0.790 to 1.616; p = 0.504). The direction of this result favours neither arm — the estimate is, in effect, a statistical tie, and for a ratio measure of this kind favourability additionally depends on which event was counted. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant (Q = 11.21, df = 3, p = 0.0106; I² = 71.09%; τ² = 0.093197), with individual trial estimates pointing in opposing directions. Egger's test indicated significant funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept = −0.2592, p = 0.0086) and the overall publication-bias risk was rated high, although trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), leaving the adjusted estimate identical to the original.

**Conclusions.** In this pooled analysis of four randomised trials, a higher BP treatment target of approximately 150–160/95–100 mmHg was not associated with a statistically detectable difference in all-cause mortality compared with a target of approximately 140/90 mmHg. The confidence interval is wide and compatible with both a clinically meaningful ~21% relative reduction and a ~62% relative increase in deaths, and substantial heterogeneity together with evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry means the pooled estimate should be regarded as imprecise and potentially inflated. The evidence base is too small and too heterogeneous to support a confident equivalence claim; it neither establishes nor excludes a mortality effect of relaxing BP targets in older adults.

## 1. Introduction

Hypertension is the leading modifiable contributor to global cardiovascular morbidity and premature death, and its prevalence rises steeply with age. While the benefit of treating markedly elevated blood pressure (BP) in older adults is not in dispute, the question of *how far* to lower BP in this group has been debated for three decades. Older patients are more likely to have isolated systolic hypertension, arterial stiffness, orthostatic hypotension, polypharmacy, frailty and impaired cerebral and renal autoregulation — all mechanisms through which more aggressive BP lowering could, in principle, cause harm. The theoretical possibility of a J- or U-shaped relationship between achieved BP and mortality has repeatedly motivated the proposal that older adults should be treated to a deliberately relaxed target.

National and international guidance has oscillated accordingly. Some guideline committees have endorsed a systolic target of <150 mmHg for adults aged 60 or 80 years and over, while others have retained <140/90 mmHg or moved towards still lower goals. These divergent recommendations rest substantially on the same small body of randomised evidence, interpreted differently. Crucially, much of the evidence base compares *active treatment versus placebo* rather than one *target* against another; trials that randomise patients to explicitly defined BP goals are far scarcer, and those conducted specifically in older populations scarcer still.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses a narrowly framed and deliberately pragmatic question: among older adults with hypertension, what is the effect of randomisation to a **higher** BP treatment target (approximately 150–160/95–100 mmHg; the "less intensively treated" arm as labelled by each trial) compared with a **lower** target (approximately 140/90 mmHg; the "more intensively treated" arm as labelled by each trial) on **all-cause mortality**?

All-cause mortality was selected as the sole outcome for three reasons. First, it is the outcome least vulnerable to ascertainment bias and adjudication disagreement. Second, it is the single outcome that integrates both the cardiovascular benefits and the potential non-cardiovascular harms of intensive BP lowering, and therefore the outcome most relevant to a net-benefit decision in an older, multimorbid population. Third, restricting to a single, unambiguously defined outcome avoids the multiplicity and selective-reporting problems that arise when many endpoints are pooled from a small number of trials. Cardiovascular mortality, non-cardiovascular mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure and adverse events are explicitly **not** part of this analysis and no inference about them should be drawn from it.

The effect direction is pre-specified as follows: a result indicating that the higher BP target arm experienced **fewer** deaths from any cause than the lower-target arm would mean that the less intensive target was associated with lower all-cause mortality.

## 2. Methods: Search and Screening

### 2.1 Protocol and reporting standards

This review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) statement. The review question was specified in PICO form prior to synthesis:

- **Population (P):** Older adults with hypertension (trial populations defined by each study's own age-entry criterion, e.g. ≥60, ≥65, ≥70 or ≥80 years).
- **Intervention (I):** Randomisation to a higher BP treatment target, approximately 150–160 mmHg systolic and/or 95–100 mmHg diastolic — the arm labelled by the trial investigators as the less intensively treated or "usual"/"standard"/"relaxed" target group.
- **Comparator (C):** Randomisation to a lower BP treatment target, approximately 140/90 mmHg — the arm labelled by the trial investigators as the more intensively treated or "tight"/"intensive" target group.
- **Outcome (O):** All-cause mortality (death from any cause) during trial follow-up, extracted as the number of deaths and the number randomised in each target arm.
- **Study design (S):** Randomised controlled trials with allocation to pre-specified BP targets. Trials of active drug versus placebo, drug-class comparison trials without distinct BP goals, observational cohorts, case series, narrative reviews, prior systematic reviews and editorials were ineligible.

### 2.2 Information sources and search strategy

Bibliographic databases were searched for randomised trials comparing defined blood pressure treatment targets in older adults. The search combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms across three conceptual blocks — (i) hypertension / high blood pressure / blood pressure lowering; (ii) target / goal / intensive versus standard / tight versus usual control / "<140/90" / "<150" / "<160"; and (iii) older adults / elderly / aged / octogenarian / geriatric — intersected with a randomised-trial design filter. Reference lists of retrieved trials, guideline documents and previous systematic reviews on BP targets in older adults were hand-searched for additional eligible trials, and included-study citations were snowballed forward.

### 2.3 Study selection

Records retrieved by the search were de-duplicated and screened at title and abstract level against the eligibility criteria above, after which potentially eligible reports were retrieved in full text and assessed in detail. Reports were excluded at full text if they did not randomise participants to distinct BP *targets*, if the population was not restricted to or clearly dominated by older adults, if the comparison did not correspond to the pre-specified higher (~150–160/95–100 mmHg) versus lower (~140/90 mmHg) contrast, or if all-cause mortality could not be extracted as counts by randomised arm. Where multiple publications reported the same trial (primary report, extended follow-up, subgroup papers), a single report per trial was retained as the index record so that no trial contributed more than one effect estimate, thereby avoiding double-counting of participants.

### 2.4 PRISMA flow

The screening pipeline yielded a final set of **4 included trials contributing 4 effect estimates** and **16,732 randomised participants**. Stage-by-stage screening counts (records identified, de-duplicated, title/abstract excluded, full texts assessed and full texts excluded with reasons) were not retained in the exported analytic record for this synthesis, and are therefore not reported here as numeric values; readers should treat the PRISMA flow as incompletely documented for this review (see §11, Limitations). What is documented, and verifiable from the analytic dataset, is the endpoint of that flow: k = 4 trials, 4 effect sizes, N = 16,732.

### 2.5 Data extraction

For each included trial, the number of deaths from any cause and the number randomised were extracted separately for the higher-target arm and the lower-target arm. Extraction followed each trial's own labelling of which arm was the "less intensively treated" (higher target) and which the "more intensively treated" (lower target), so that the direction of the comparison was harmonised across studies before pooling.

### 2.6 Statistical analysis

Because the outcome is a binary event (death / survival) with arm-level counts, the **Risk Ratio (RR)** was pre-specified as the effect measure, with the higher-target arm in the numerator. This measure was selected by explicit analytic specification rather than being inferred post hoc, and **pooling was carried out natively on the risk-ratio scale** — that is, the reported pooled value is a genuine native-scale risk ratio and not a standardized surrogate back-converted from another metric.

Trial-specific estimates were combined using a **random-effects model** with **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of the between-study variance τ². A random-effects specification was chosen a priori because the included trials differ in their age-entry criteria, exact numeric targets, achieved BP separation, background therapy and follow-up duration, making a common-effect assumption implausible.

Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's **Q** statistic with its associated p-value, the **I²** statistic (proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error), and **τ²** (absolute between-study variance on the analysis scale). Heterogeneity was further characterised descriptively through a cumulative meta-analysis and an effect-distribution plot.

Small-study effects and potential publication bias were assessed using **Egger's regression test** of funnel-plot asymmetry and the **Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill** procedure. Given k = 4, both procedures were interpreted as exploratory and underpowered; this caveat is applied throughout §9.

Figures generated in support of the synthesis are referenced by path in the relevant sections below.

Several features of this evidence base warrant emphasis.

**Small number of trials.** Only four trials randomised older hypertensive adults to the specific higher-versus-lower target contrast of interest and reported extractable all-cause mortality counts by arm. This is a small k for a question of this clinical importance, and it constrains every downstream inference — the precision of the pooled estimate, the reliability of the heterogeneity variance estimate, and the power of the small-study-effect diagnostics.

**Marked imbalance in trial size.** Trial sizes span more than an order of magnitude, from 724 to 8,511 randomised participants. A single trial (study_3_R3) contributes 50.9% of all randomised participants, and the two largest trials together contribute 77.3%. Under a random-effects model the influence of the largest trials is attenuated relative to a common-effect model, but the synthesis nonetheless rests disproportionately on a small number of large trials, with the smallest trial (study_2_R2, 4.3% of participants) exerting influence out of proportion to its sample size precisely because the between-study variance is large.

**Inconsistent direction across trials.** The study-level estimates do not agree in sign. Two trials (study_1_R1 and study_3_R3) have negative study-level values and two (study_2_R2 and study_4_R4) have positive values; moreover the confidence intervals of study_1_R1 and study_2_R2 do not overlap at all, which is a strong qualitative signal of real between-study inconsistency rather than chance scatter. Only study_3_R3 has a study-level interval that comfortably crosses the null. This pattern anticipates the substantial I² reported in §6.

**Clinical and design heterogeneity.** Although detailed trial-level covariate data (age-entry threshold, exact numeric target, achieved between-arm BP separation, baseline cardiovascular risk, follow-up duration, background antihypertensive regimen) were not carried forward into the analytic record for this synthesis, these dimensions are known to vary widely among trials of BP targets in older adults and provide a plausible substantive explanation for the statistical heterogeneity observed. The absence of these fields is the principal reason that formal moderator analysis could not be undertaken (see §7).

**A note on the study-level numbers in Table 1.** The per-study effect sizes carried in the analytic record are expressed as standardized mean-difference-equivalent (Hedges' g) values with their own confidence intervals, and are reproduced above exactly as supplied. These are display-level standardizations used for cross-study comparison on a common axis. The **pooled** result reported in §5, by contrast, was computed natively on the risk-ratio scale, as described in §2.6 and confirmed in the appended Effect Measure Summary table. The two sets of numbers are therefore on different scales and should not be arithmetically reconciled with one another; the native-scale risk ratio is the inferential result of record, and the standardized study-level values should be read only as a visual aid to judging consistency of direction and relative precision.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure and its interpretation

The effect measure for this synthesis is the **Risk Ratio (RR)** for all-cause mortality, computed with the **higher BP target arm (~150–160/95–100 mmHg) as the index arm** and the **lower BP target arm (~140/90 mmHg) as the reference arm**. On this scale:

- **RR < 1** indicates *fewer* deaths in the higher-target (less intensive) arm — i.e. the less intensive target associated with lower all-cause mortality;
- **RR > 1** indicates *more* deaths in the higher-target arm — i.e. the less intensive target associated with higher all-cause mortality;
- **RR = 1** indicates no difference between targets.

The effect measure was set by explicit analytic specification (`effect_measure_source: explicit_user_request`), no conflict between the requested and detected measure was recorded, and **native risk-ratio pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used: true`). The pooled value reported below is therefore a confirmed native-scale risk ratio; it is **not** a standardized fallback, and it is **not** a Hedges' g.

### 4.2 Distribution of effect sizes

The four study-level estimates and their confidence intervals are displayed in the forest plot at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`, which shows each trial's estimate, its weight in the random-effects model, and the pooled summary with its confidence interval. The dispersion of the estimates is shown in the effect-distribution plot at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`, and the stability of the running summary as trials accumulate is shown in the cumulative meta-analysis plot at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`.

The salient feature of the effect-size distribution is its **bidirectionality**. The estimates straddle the null, with non-overlapping confidence intervals at the extremes. There is no single coherent signal that the trials are collectively estimating with random noise around it; rather, the trials appear to be estimating genuinely different quantities, or to differ in ways (population age, achieved BP separation, follow-up length, competing risks) that materially change the target-mortality relationship. This is the qualitative basis for the random-effects specification and for the cautious framing of the pooled result in §5.

### 5.1 Interpretation of the pooled estimate

The pooled Risk Ratio for all-cause mortality associated with a higher BP treatment target, relative to a target of approximately 140/90 mmHg, is **1.130 (95% CI 0.790 to 1.616; p = 0.504)**.

The direction of this result **favours neither arm**. Per the pre-specified direction field, the estimate represents essentially a statistical tie, and because this is a ratio measure, favourability additionally depends on which event was counted. The point estimate is not statistically distinguishable from the null at any conventional threshold (p = 0.504), and the confidence interval comfortably includes RR = 1.

The width of that interval is the most important single feature of the result. The data are compatible with a **21% relative reduction** in all-cause mortality under the higher target (RR = 0.790) at the lower bound and a **62% relative increase** (RR = 1.616) at the upper bound. Both extremes would be clinically consequential — the first would support relaxing targets in older adults, the second would argue strongly against it — and the evidence as it stands cannot discriminate between them. This is therefore a finding of **inconclusiveness**, not of established equivalence. The common rhetorical slide from "no statistically significant difference" to "no difference" is not warranted here: with k = 4, substantial heterogeneity, and a confidence interval spanning a factor of roughly two in relative risk, the analysis is simply underpowered to settle the question.

### 5.2 Stability of the pooled estimate

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows how the running pooled estimate evolves as trials are added. With only four trials and τ² = 0.093197, the cumulative summary does not converge to a stable value, and the running interval remains wide throughout. The practical implication is that the addition of even one further moderately sized trial with a discordant result could shift the pooled point estimate materially and change the sign of the apparent effect. No single-trial-removal (leave-one-out) sensitivity analysis was recorded in the analytic output; given the size imbalance documented in §3 and the non-overlapping intervals of study_1_R1 and study_2_R2, such an analysis would be expected to show considerable movement in the summary estimate, and its absence is noted as a limitation.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity in this synthesis is **substantial and statistically significant**:

- **Cochran's Q = 11.21** on 3 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.0106**. The null hypothesis of homogeneity is rejected at the conventional 5% level. This is notable because Q is typically underpowered when k is small; detecting significant heterogeneity with only four trials implies that the inconsistency is pronounced.
- **I² = 71.09%**. Approximately 71% of the observed variability across trial estimates is attributable to real between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. By conventional benchmarks this falls in the "substantial" range (50–90%), close to the boundary with "considerable".
- **τ² = 0.093197**, the estimated between-study variance on the analysis scale. Taking the square root gives a between-study standard deviation of approximately 0.305 on the log-risk-ratio scale, implying that the underlying true effects across trials plausibly range over roughly a fourfold span in relative risk. This dispersion is of the same order as, or larger than, any plausible average effect — which is precisely why the pooled point estimate carries so little information on its own.

The heterogeneity diagnostics are summarised graphically at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`, and the spread of individual estimates relative to the pooled value is visible in both the forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) and the effect-distribution plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`).

### 6.1 What the heterogeneity means substantively

Heterogeneity of this magnitude in a four-trial synthesis has three practical consequences.

First, **the pooled estimate should be read as a description of the average of a dispersed set of effects, not as an estimate of a single common effect**. A 95% *prediction* interval — the interval within which the true effect of a new, exchangeable trial would be expected to fall — would be considerably wider than the 0.790–1.616 confidence interval reported above, and with k = 4 would be so wide as to be essentially uninformative. Prediction intervals were not computed in the analytic record; this is a recognised gap.

Second, **the heterogeneity is a finding in its own right**. Trials of BP targets in older adults differ systematically in age-entry threshold, baseline risk, frailty burden, achieved between-arm BP separation (which is often far smaller than the nominal target difference), concomitant therapy, and follow-up duration. Any of these could plausibly modify the target–mortality relationship, and the physiological rationale for a J-curve implies that effect modification by baseline risk and vascular status should be *expected* rather than treated as nuisance variation.

Third, **heterogeneity interacts with the publication-bias assessment** (§9). Funnel-plot asymmetry can be generated by genuine between-study effect differences that correlate with study size — for instance, if smaller trials were conducted in more selected, higher-risk, or older populations — as readily as by selective publication. With I² = 71.09%, this alternative explanation for the asymmetry detected by Egger's test cannot be dismissed.

## 7. Moderators

**No moderator or subgroup analyses were performed, and none could be performed with the available data.**

The analytic record for this synthesis carries, for each trial, only an identifier, a sample size, and an effect estimate with its confidence interval. It does not carry trial-level covariates — such as mean or threshold age, the exact numeric systolic and diastolic targets in each arm, the achieved between-arm BP separation, baseline cardiovascular risk or frailty status, proportion with isolated systolic hypertension, background drug class, follow-up duration, or risk-of-bias domain judgements — and without such covariates neither subgroup analysis nor meta-regression is possible.

Even had these covariates been available, formal moderator analysis would have been inadvisable. With **k = 4**, a meta-regression on a single covariate would have 2 residual degrees of freedom; conventional methodological guidance recommends a minimum of roughly ten studies per covariate. Any such analysis would be severely overfitted, would have negligible power to detect a true moderator, and would have a high probability of producing a spuriously "significant" association by chance. Reporting such an analysis would convey false precision.

A bubble chart is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, displaying effect estimates against study size with marker area proportional to weight. This figure should be read as a **descriptive visualisation of the size–effect relationship only**. It is not the output of a fitted meta-regression model, and no slope, coefficient, R² or p-value should be inferred from it. Its one interpretable feature is qualitative and relates to §9: the visual tendency for the estimates to shift with study size is the same pattern that Egger's test formalises statistically.

The identification of effect modifiers in this clinical question — in particular whether the mortality consequences of a relaxed target differ by chronological age, frailty, baseline BP level, or achieved BP separation — remains an open and important research priority, and cannot be addressed by the present dataset.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Small-study effects were assessed by two complementary methods, both of which must be understood as **severely underpowered at k = 4**. Standard methodological guidance recommends a minimum of ten studies before funnel-plot asymmetry tests are applied; with four studies, the tests have very low power to detect real asymmetry and a correspondingly elevated chance of misleading results in either direction. The findings below are reported for completeness and interpreted as exploratory.

**Table 3. Small-study-effect diagnostics**

| Diagnostic | Value |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | −0.2592 |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.0086** |
| Trim-and-fill: studies imputed (k₀) | **0** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted estimate | 0.0741 (identical to unadjusted; no studies imputed) |
| Overall publication-bias risk rating | **High** |

### 8.1 Interpretation

**Egger's test indicates statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (intercept = −0.2592, p = 0.0086).** This is consistent with small-study effects and supports the overall "high" risk rating assigned to publication bias in this synthesis. The negative intercept indicates that smaller trials tended to produce estimates systematically displaced relative to larger trials — a pattern visible qualitatively in the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) and in the relative positions of study_2_R2 (the smallest trial, n = 724, with the most extreme estimate) and study_3_R3 (the largest trial, n = 8,511, with a near-null estimate).

**However, the trim-and-fill procedure imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), so the adjusted pooled estimate is identical to the original.** The two diagnostics are therefore discordant, and that discordance is informative rather than reassuring. It means the asymmetry detected by Egger's test was not of a form that the trim-and-fill algorithm could correct — trim-and-fill assumes a specific pattern of suppression at one edge of the funnel, and a mixed, bidirectional scatter such as that observed here will not trigger imputation even when asymmetry is statistically present.

Three substantive explanations for the asymmetry are plausible, and the available data cannot distinguish among them:

1. **Genuine selective publication or reporting** — smaller trials with null or unfavourable mortality findings not published, or all-cause mortality not reported in a subset of eligible trials.
2. **Substantive heterogeneity correlated with study size** — smaller trials conducted in more selected, older, frailer, or higher-risk populations in which the true target–mortality relationship genuinely differs. Given I² = 71.09%, this is a strong competing explanation.
3. **Methodological differences between small and large trials** — differences in outcome ascertainment completeness, duration of follow-up, allocation concealment, loss to follow-up, or intensity of achieved BP separation.

**Bottom line.** Given this discordance, the pooled effect should be interpreted cautiously and treated as **potentially inflated**. The null trim-and-fill result should be taken as **weak reassurance at best, and not as evidence against bias**. Combined with the wide confidence interval and substantial heterogeneity, this diagnostic pattern means the pooled RR of 1.130 should not be quoted as a stable or trustworthy estimate of the average effect of relaxing BP targets in older adults.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Principal findings

Across four randomised trials and 16,732 older adults with hypertension, randomisation to a higher BP treatment target (~150–160/95–100 mmHg) was associated with a pooled Risk Ratio for all-cause mortality of **1.130 (95% CI 0.790 to 1.616; p = 0.504)** relative to a lower target of approximately 140/90 mmHg. The result **favours neither arm**: it is, on the pre-specified direction criterion, essentially a tie, with the additional caveat that for a ratio measure favourability depends on which event was counted. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 71.09%; τ² = 0.093197; Q = 11.21, p = 0.0106), with individual trial estimates pointing in opposing directions and, at the extremes, with non-overlapping confidence intervals. Egger's test indicated significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.0086) and the overall publication-bias risk was rated high, although trim-and-fill imputed no studies.

The headline message is therefore **not** that higher and lower BP targets are equivalent with respect to mortality. It is that the randomised evidence addressing this specific contrast in older adults is **too sparse, too inconsistent, and too imprecise to answer the question**.

### 9.2 Relation to the wider literature and to guidelines

The debate over BP targets in older adults has been shaped disproportionately by a small number of landmark trials, and this review's finding of substantial heterogeneity reflects a real feature of that literature rather than an artefact of pooling. Trials differ in whether their older participants were comparatively fit and community-dwelling or frail and multimorbid; in whether the nominal target difference between arms translated into a meaningful achieved BP separation; and in follow-up duration, which determines the balance between early haemodynamic harms and later cardiovascular benefit. A pooled average across such trials describes a population that does not correspond to any actual patient.

Importantly, this analysis is restricted to **target-versus-target** randomised comparisons. It therefore speaks to a different question from the large body of **treatment-versus-placebo** trials in older hypertensives, which consistently show benefit from treating elevated BP. Nothing in this review should be read as questioning the value of treating hypertension in older adults; the question addressed is narrower — given that treatment is given, does the numerical goal matter for survival?

The analysis also speaks only to **all-cause mortality**. Cardiovascular mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure and adverse events — including the falls, syncope, acute kidney injury and electrolyte disturbance that are central to the clinical argument for relaxing targets in frail older people — were explicitly excluded from scope. A complete net-benefit assessment would require all of these, and the present result cannot substitute for one. It is entirely possible for targets to differ materially in stroke or heart-failure incidence, or in treatment-related harm, while producing no detectable difference in deaths from any cause over the follow-up durations studied.

### 9.3 Clinical implications

For clinicians, the practical implication is that the randomised evidence does **not** currently provide a mortality-based justification either for routinely relaxing BP targets in older adults or for insisting on a target of 140/90 mmHg on survival grounds alone. The confidence interval spans effects that would, at one extreme, support relaxation and, at the other, caution firmly against it.

In that evidentiary vacuum, decisions should be individualised on grounds that this analysis does not address: biological rather than chronological age, frailty and life expectancy, orthostatic symptoms and fall risk, polypharmacy and drug tolerance, competing comorbidity, renal function, and — not least — patient preference regarding the trade-off between a modest and uncertain prognostic gain and the burden of additional medication. The appropriate reading of this meta-analysis is as licence for individualised, shared decision-making rather than as endorsement of any single numeric goal.

### 9.4 Research implications

Three priorities follow. First, **adequately powered trials randomising specifically older and frailer adults to clearly separated BP targets, with mortality as a pre-specified outcome and with verified achieved BP separation, remain necessary**; the present k = 4 is insufficient. Second, **individual participant data (IPD) meta-analysis** would be far more informative than aggregate pooling here, because it would permit examination of effect modification by age, frailty, baseline BP and achieved separation — exactly the moderators that aggregate data rendered inaccessible (§7). Third, **consistent reporting of all-cause mortality as arm-level counts** in all BP-target trials would strengthen future syntheses and reduce the selective-availability pressures that may underlie the funnel asymmetry detected here.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No formal `validation_flags` were recorded in the analytic record for this synthesis. The following data-quality observations are nonetheless material to interpretation and are stated plainly rather than left implicit:

- **Effect measure provenance is clean.** The Risk Ratio was set by explicit analytic specification, no conflict between requested and detected measure was recorded, and **native risk-ratio pooling was used**. The pooled value of 1.130 is a confirmed native-scale risk ratio, not a standardized fallback and not a Hedges' g.
- **Single outcome group.** The record contains one meta-analysis entry and no `meta_analysis_groups`; all four trials address the same outcome at the same conceptual timepoint, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data for pooling. No separate subgroup results are therefore reported.
- **Double-counting was guarded against at the study-selection stage**, with a single index report retained per trial so that no trial contributes more than one effect estimate; the counts (k = 4 trials, 4 effect sizes) are consistent with this.
- **PRISMA stage counts were not retained** in the exported analytic record. Numbers of records identified, de-duplicated, screened, excluded at title/abstract, assessed at full text, and excluded at full text with reasons are not available, so the PRISMA flow is reported here as incomplete. This limits independent verification of the search and screening process.
- **Trial-level covariates and risk-of-bias judgements were not carried forward**, precluding moderator analysis (§7) and preventing any formal certainty-of-evidence (e.g. GRADE) rating by domain.
- **Study-level estimates are carried on a standardized (Hedges' g equivalent) display scale** while pooling was native risk ratio; these two scales are not arithmetically interconvertible and should not be reconciled. The native-scale pooled risk ratio is the result of record.
- **Leave-one-out and prediction-interval analyses were not computed**, despite being particularly informative given the size imbalance and the magnitude of τ².

---

## 11. Limitations

1. **Very small number of trials (k = 4).** This is the overriding limitation. It constrains the precision of the pooled estimate, renders τ² imprecisely estimated, makes the heterogeneity statistics unstable, and leaves the funnel-asymmetry diagnostics substantially underpowered.

2. **Substantial unexplained heterogeneity.** I² = 71.09% with Q significant at p = 0.0106 and estimates in opposing directions means the trials are not estimating a common effect. The pooled value is an average over a dispersed distribution of true effects; a prediction interval, had one been computed, would be wider still and probably uninformative.

3. **Wide confidence interval and inconclusiveness.** The interval 0.790 to 1.616 includes both a 21% relative mortality reduction and a 62% relative increase. The non-significant p-value (0.504) indicates inconclusiveness, not equivalence; no claim of "no difference" is supported.

4. **Evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry with high publication-bias risk.** Egger's p = 0.0086, while trim-and-fill imputed nothing (k₀ = 0). The discordance means the asymmetry could not be corrected by that method, and the pooled estimate should be treated as potentially inflated. The null trim-and-fill is weak reassurance at best.

5. **Dominance of a single large trial.** One trial contributes 50.9% of randomised participants and the two largest contribute 77.3%, so the synthesis rests heavily on a small number of studies; the absence of leave-one-out sensitivity analysis means the robustness of the pooled estimate to removal of any one trial is undocumented.

6. **No moderator analysis possible.** Trial-level covariates (age threshold, exact targets, achieved BP separation, frailty, follow-up duration, background therapy) were unavailable, so clinically plausible sources of heterogeneity could not be investigated, and with k = 4 meta-regression would have been uninterpretable in any case.

7. **No risk-of-bias assessment or certainty-of-evidence rating.** Domain-level bias judgements were not carried in the analytic record, so the contribution of trial quality to the heterogeneity and asymmetry observed cannot be evaluated, and no GRADE rating is offered.

8. **Incomplete PRISMA documentation.** Stage-by-stage screening counts and reasons for full-text exclusion are not available, limiting transparency and reproducibility of study selection.

9. **Single-outcome scope.** By design, cardiovascular mortality, non-cardiovascular mortality, stroke, myocardial infarction, heart failure and adverse events were excluded. The analysis therefore cannot support any net-benefit or net-harm conclusion; a mortality-neutral result is fully compatible with meaningful differences in non-fatal events or treatment-related harms.

10. **Heterogeneity in the definition of "older" and of the targets themselves.** Trials' age-entry criteria and numeric targets vary within the specified ranges (~150–160/95–100 vs ~140/90 mmHg), and achieved BP separation is typically narrower than nominal targets imply, which biases target-versus-target comparisons towards the null to an unquantified degree.

---

## 12. Conclusion

In a random-effects meta-analysis of four randomised trials enrolling 16,732 older adults with hypertension, randomisation to a higher blood pressure treatment target (~150–160/95–100 mmHg) versus a lower target (~140/90 mmHg) yielded a pooled Risk Ratio for all-cause mortality of **1.130 (95% CI 0.790 to 1.616; p = 0.504)**, computed natively on the risk-ratio scale. The result **favours neither arm** — it is essentially a statistical tie, and for a ratio measure favourability additionally depends on which event was counted.

This should be read as an **inconclusive** finding rather than a demonstration of equivalence. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant (I² = 71.09%; τ² = 0.093197; Q = 11.21, df = 3, p = 0.0106), with trial estimates in opposing directions; Egger's test indicated significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.0086) and the overall publication-bias risk was rated high, with trim-and-fill providing no correction (k₀ = 0) and hence no reassurance. The confidence interval remains compatible with both a clinically important mortality benefit and a clinically important mortality harm from relaxing targets.

Accordingly, the present evidence base does not justify a mortality-based recommendation either for or against a higher blood pressure target in older adults. Decisions should be individualised with reference to frailty, comorbidity, treatment tolerance, competing risks and patient preference, and should take account of outcomes — stroke, heart failure, and treatment-related harms — that lie outside the scope of this analysis. Adequately powered trials in older and frailer populations, and individual participant data meta-analysis capable of examining effect modification, are required before this question can be considered settled.
