# Higher Versus Lower Blood Pressure Targets and All-Cause Mortality in Older Adults With Hypertension: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
The optimal blood pressure target for older adults with hypertension remains uncertain because more intensive treatment may reduce vascular risk while increasing treatment burden and adverse effects. This meta-analysis evaluated all-cause mortality after assignment to a higher blood pressure target (approximately 150–160/95–100 mmHg; less intensive treatment) versus a lower target (approximately 140/90 mmHg; more intensive treatment).

### Methods
The eligible outcome was death from any cause, analyzed using deaths and total randomized in each trial arm. Cardiovascular mortality, non-cardiovascular mortality, stroke, and other outcomes were excluded. Four studies (16,732 participants) contributed four independent effects. Risk ratios (RRs) were pooled with a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were examined using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

### Results
The pooled risk ratio was 1.1298669598327635 (95% CI 0.790–1.616; p=0.503990037239755; k=4; n=16,732). The supplied favorability classification was “neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).” The confidence interval crossed the null value of 1, providing no statistically significant evidence that assignment to either target reduced all-cause mortality. Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=71.09%; τ²=0.093197), and Cochran’s Q was statistically significant (Q=11.21; p=0.0106). Egger’s intercept was −0.2869 (p=0.0757). Trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀=0), although publication-bias risk was classified as moderate.

### Conclusions
Across four studies, assignment to a higher rather than lower blood pressure target was not associated with a statistically significant difference in all-cause mortality. The estimate was imprecise and heterogeneity was substantial. The small evidence base, incomplete study-level reporting in the supplied dataset, and limited power of publication-bias tests warrant cautious interpretation.

## Introduction

Hypertension treatment in older adults requires balancing the potential cardiovascular benefits of lower blood pressure against treatment burden, orthostatic symptoms, falls, renal effects, and other complications that may accompany more intensive therapy. All-cause mortality is a clinically definitive outcome that captures fatal benefits and harms without requiring adjudication of cause-specific death.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among older adults with hypertension, what is the effect of assignment to a higher blood pressure target—approximately systolic/diastolic 150–160/95–100 mmHg and corresponding to less intensive treatment—versus a lower target of approximately 140/90 mmHg and corresponding to more intensive treatment, on all-cause mortality?

The prespecified comparison was:

- **Treatment:** the higher-target, less intensively treated arm in each trial.
- **Control:** the lower-target, more intensively treated arm in each trial.
- **Outcome:** death from any cause.
- **Effect measure:** risk ratio (RR), for which the null value is 1.

Cardiovascular mortality, non-cardiovascular mortality, stroke, and all nonmortality outcomes were outside the scope of this analysis.

## Meta-Analytic Result

Across four studies and 16,732 participants, the REML random-effects **pooled risk ratio** was **1.1298669598327635** with a **95% CI of 0.790 to 1.616** and **p=0.503990037239755**.

The confidence interval crossed the null value of 1, and the hypothesis test was not statistically significant. Thus, the available evidence did not demonstrate a difference in all-cause mortality between assignment to the higher, less intensive blood pressure target and assignment to the lower, more intensive target. The supplied classification indicated that the result favored neither arm.

The confidence interval was clinically imprecise: it remained compatible with appreciable relative mortality differences in either direction. Accordingly, absence of statistical significance should not be interpreted as proof of equivalence or no effect.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 11.21
- **Q-test p-value:** 0.0106
- **I²:** 71.09%
- **τ²:** 0.093197

The statistically significant Q test indicates that the dispersion of effects exceeded that expected from sampling error alone. An I² of 71.09% suggests that a substantial proportion of observed variation was attributable to between-study differences rather than chance. ## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−0.2869** with **p=0.0757**. This did not meet the conventional two-sided 0.05 threshold for statistical significance. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀=0** missing studies. The supplied trim-and-fill output also included a standardized summary value of **0.0299**; because this value was not identified as a native RR and differs in scale from the pooled mortality RR, it should not be interpreted as an adjusted pooled RR.

Taken together, these analyses provided no strong statistical evidence of small-study effects or missing studies. Nevertheless, publication-bias risk was classified as **moderate** because the Egger p-value was borderline and both Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill have limited sensitivity and reliability when only four heterogeneous studies are available. Failure to detect asymmetry in such a small set does not establish the absence of reporting bias.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This random-effects meta-analysis of four studies involving 16,732 older adults with hypertension found no statistically significant difference in all-cause mortality between assignment to a higher blood pressure target and assignment to a lower target. The pooled RR was 1.1298669598327635 (95% CI 0.790–1.616; p=0.503990037239755), and the supplied favorability classification was neither arm.

The central estimate alone is insufficient for clinical interpretation. Its broad confidence interval includes potentially meaningful effects in either direction, while substantial heterogeneity (I²=71.09%) indicates that the average pooled estimate may not represent every trial setting. The result should therefore be interpreted as inconclusive rather than as demonstrating equivalence between target strategies.

### Clinical interpretation

All-cause mortality integrates fatal benefits and harms and avoids ambiguities in cause-of-death classification. However, mortality alone does not capture nonfatal cardiovascular events, functional outcomes, quality of life, treatment burden, falls, syncope, electrolyte abnormalities, or kidney outcomes. Because these outcomes were intentionally outside the scope of this review, this analysis cannot determine the overall benefit–harm balance of higher versus lower targets.

Clinical decisions for older adults should account for baseline cardiovascular risk, frailty, multimorbidity, orthostatic symptoms, treatment tolerability, life expectancy, and patient preferences. The present analysis does not establish that either target universally minimizes mortality.

### Statistical interpretation

The random-effects model estimates an average effect while allowing true effects to vary. The significant Q statistic and I² of 71.09% support the presence of important variation. With only four studies, however, estimates of τ² and the random-effects confidence interval can be unstable. Moreover, conventional normal-approximation inference may understate uncertainty when the number of studies is very small. No prediction interval or small-sample adjustment was supplied, so neither can be reported.

The contrast between study-level standardized values in the supplied dataset and the native pooled RR also limits auditability. The pooled RR was explicitly requested and native pooling was reported as used, but the absence of arm-level mortality counts prevents independent verification of the study-specific RRs and weights.

## Data Quality and Validation Notes

Although no separate `validation_flags` object was supplied, the results contain several data-quality issues that require explicit documentation:

1. **Effect-measure conflict:** the user-requested effect was RR, but the source papers were reported to explicitly present hazard ratios. The RR request was retained as authoritative. RRs and hazard ratios are not generally interchangeable because hazard ratios incorporate event timing and proportional-hazards assumptions, whereas RRs compare cumulative risks over a defined period.
2. **Study-level scale mismatch:** the four study-level outputs were labeled Hedges’ g, while the pooled analysis was labeled a native-scale RR analysis. Those standardized values cannot be read as study-specific RRs.
3. **Unavailable arm-level data:** the numbers of deaths and randomized participants by arm were not supplied, although the intended analysis was defined in terms of those quantities. This prevents independent reconstruction of effects.
4. **Incomplete study identification:** trial names and bibliographic citations were not supplied, so individual reports cannot be linked to study characteristics or independently checked for overlapping populations.
5. **Incomplete PRISMA information:** search, deduplication, screening, exclusion, extraction, and risk-of-bias details were absent.

These issues do not alter the exact numerical pooled result provided, but they materially limit its reproducibility and interpretation.

## Limitations

This review has several important limitations.

First, only four studies were available. This restricts precision, makes random-effects variance estimation unstable, and provides little power to detect small-study effects or investigate heterogeneity.

Second, heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant. The average pooled RR may conceal meaningful differences among populations and trial protocols, but no adequately powered moderator analyses were available.

Third, the supplied dataset lacked trial names, full citations, arm-specific death counts, randomized denominators by arm, follow-up durations, and study-specific native RRs. The pooled result could therefore not be independently reconstructed or checked.

Fourth, the source reports were said to present hazard ratios, whereas this analysis retained the explicitly requested RR. Differences in estimands and follow-up can be consequential, particularly if event timing or censoring differs across arms.

Fifth, no risk-of-bias assessment was supplied. Potential bias from randomization, allocation concealment, missing outcome data, outcome ascertainment, selective reporting, or deviations from intended interventions could not be evaluated.

Sixth, publication-bias methods are unreliable with four studies. The nonsignificant Egger test and k₀=0 should not be considered evidence that selective publication or selective outcome reporting was absent.

Finally, this review considered only all-cause mortality. It does not provide a complete assessment of clinical benefits and harms associated with different blood pressure targets.

## Conclusion

In four studies comprising 16,732 older adults with hypertension, assignment to a higher, less intensive blood pressure target rather than a lower, more intensive target yielded a pooled risk ratio for all-cause mortality of 1.1298669598327635 (95% CI 0.790–1.616; p=0.503990037239755). The supplied favorability classification was neither arm. The evidence therefore did not show a statistically significant mortality difference, but substantial heterogeneity and wide uncertainty preclude a conclusion of equivalence.

More complete reporting of arm-level events and trial characteristics, consistent use of a prespecified effect measure, rigorous risk-of-bias assessment, and additional randomized evidence would be needed to obtain a more precise and clinically transportable estimate.
