# Effect of Structured Exercise Training on Five-Repetition Sit-to-Stand Performance in Adults Receiving Maintenance Dialysis: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Reduced lower-limb functional strength is a defining feature of the dialysis phenotype and predicts falls, hospitalisation and mortality. The timed five-repetition sit-to-stand test (STS-5) is a brief, dialysis-unit-feasible measure of this construct, in which a *shorter* time indicates better performance. Whether structured exercise training improves STS-5 time in people receiving maintenance dialysis has not been quantified on the native (seconds) scale.

**Objective.** To estimate the effect of structured exercise training (aerobic, resistance, combined or other structured modalities, delivered intradialytically or interdialytically) versus no exercise/usual care or placebo (sham/stretching) exercise on timed five-repetition sit-to-stand performance (seconds) in adults on maintenance haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis.

**Methods.** A systematic search and screening pipeline identified controlled trials reporting end-of-intervention (or, where only these were available, change-from-baseline) STS-5 time as mean, SD and n per arm. Standard errors were converted to SDs (SD = SE × √n). Repetition-count variants (30-s/60-s sit-to-stand), timed up-and-go and six-minute walk test were not eligible for this outcome. Eight records were carried into analysis and seven contributed extractable arm-level data. Data were pooled on the native mean-difference (MD, seconds) scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, τ² and I²; small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Seven comparisons comprising 451 participants were pooled. The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was **−2.47 seconds (95% CI −4.15 to −0.78; p = 0.0041)**, a result that **favours structured exercise training** (i.e. exercise participants completed five sit-to-stand repetitions approximately 2.5 s faster than controls at end of intervention). Between-study heterogeneity was substantial: Q = 20.47 (df = 6, p = 0.0023), I² = 68.55%, τ² = 3.190 s² (τ ≈ 1.79 s). Study-level standardised effects ranged from g = 0.00 (two trials showing no between-arm difference) to g = −1.63 (favouring exercise), with four of seven confidence intervals excluding the null in the direction of benefit. Egger's test showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept = −0.1693, p = 0.5955) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), consistent with a low assessed risk of publication bias.

**Conclusions.** Structured exercise training during or between dialysis sessions produces a statistically significant and clinically meaningful reduction of roughly 2.5 seconds in five-repetition sit-to-stand time compared with usual care or sham/stretching control. Confidence in the magnitude is tempered by substantial heterogeneity, a small number of pooled trials, and an approximate 95% prediction interval that crosses the null, indicating that benefit is probable on average but not guaranteed in every setting or programme.

## 1. Introduction

Adults receiving maintenance dialysis experience an accelerated loss of muscle mass and contractile function — a phenotype driven by uraemic myopathy, chronic inflammation, metabolic acidosis, protein-energy wasting, dialysis-related catabolism and, critically, profound physical inactivity. The functional consequence is most apparent in the lower limbs: difficulty rising from a chair, toilet or car seat is among the earliest and most disabling manifestations, and impaired sit-to-stand performance is associated prospectively with falls, fracture, loss of independence, hospitalisation and mortality in this population.

The timed five-repetition sit-to-stand test (STS-5) quantifies this construct directly. The participant rises from and returns to a seated position five times as quickly as safely possible, with arms folded, and the elapsed time in seconds is recorded. Because the outcome is a *duration*, **a lower value denotes better performance**, and an effective intervention therefore produces a *negative* mean difference (exercise minus control). The test requires only a standard chair and a stopwatch, takes under a minute, and can be administered in a dialysis unit before or after treatment — properties that have made it one of the more frequently reported functional endpoints in nephrology exercise trials. It should be distinguished carefully from repetition-count variants (the 30-second or 60-second sit-to-stand, in which *higher* is better), from the timed up-and-go (which incorporates gait and turning), and from aerobic endurance measures such as the six-minute walk test; none of these were eligible for the present synthesis.

Structured exercise training — supervised aerobic cycling, progressive resistance training, combined protocols, or other structured modalities such as neuromuscular electrical stimulation-augmented or functional circuit programmes — has been evaluated in dialysis populations both *intradialytically* (performed during the haemodialysis session, typically in the first two hours) and *interdialytically* (on non-dialysis days, in-centre, home-based or supervised in a gym). Individual trials have reported effects on STS-5 ranging from none at all to improvements exceeding one and a half standard deviations. Previous syntheses have frequently pooled heterogeneous "lower-limb function" constructs together, or converted everything to standardised units, which obscures whether the benefit is large enough to matter at the bedside.

This review therefore addresses a single, tightly specified question on the native scale of the measurement:

> In adults receiving maintenance haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis, what is the effect of structured exercise training (aerobic, resistance, combined or other; intradialytic or interdialytic), compared with no exercise/usual care or placebo (sham or stretching) exercise, on time in seconds to complete the timed five-repetition sit-to-stand test at the end of the intervention?

## 2. Methods: Search and Screening

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they met all of the following (PICO framing):

- **Population (P):** Adults (≥18 years) receiving maintenance haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis. Pre-dialysis chronic kidney disease and kidney transplant recipients were not eligible.
- **Intervention (I):** Structured exercise training of any principal modality — aerobic, resistance, combined aerobic-plus-resistance, or other structured exercise — delivered either during dialysis sessions (intradialytic) or between sessions (interdialytic). Programmes had to be structured and prescribed, as opposed to generic advice to be more active.
- **Comparator (C):** No exercise, usual care, or placebo exercise (sham exercise or stretching-only control).
- **Outcome (O):** Time in seconds to complete the five-repetition (or five-to-ten repetition) timed sit-to-stand test at the end of the exercise intervention, extractable as mean, standard deviation and number of participants per arm.
- **Design:** Controlled comparison of an exercise arm against an eligible control arm.

### 2.2 Outcome definition and data handling rules

End-of-intervention (post-intervention) values were preferred. Where a study reported only change-from-baseline data, the mean change and the SD of the change were used, applied consistently to *both* arms. Standard errors or standard errors of the mean were converted to standard deviations using SD = SE × √n. Where dispersion was reported only as a confidence interval, the SD was back-calculated from the interval half-width and the arm sample size.

Explicitly **ineligible** for this outcome, even when reported in otherwise relevant trials, were: repetition-count sit-to-stand tests (30-second and 60-second variants), the timed up-and-go test, and the six-minute walk test. These measure related but non-interchangeable constructs and, in the case of the repetition-count tests, run in the opposite direction.

### 2.3 Screening and selection

Records retrieved by the search were screened against the criteria above in a staged pipeline: title/abstract screening for population and intervention relevance, followed by full-text assessment for the presence of an eligible STS-5 outcome with extractable arm-level summary statistics. Reviews, protocols without results, and duplicate reports of the same trial cohort were removed so that each participant sample contributed only once.

**Screening yield.** The screening and extraction pipeline carried **8 study records** into the analysis stage. Of these, **7 comparisons provided complete arm-level STS-5 data (mean, SD, n for both arms) and were included in the quantitative synthesis**, contributing **451 participants** in total. One record retained at the study level did not yield an extractable effect size for this outcome — because the five-repetition sit-to-stand result was not reported in a form permitting computation of a between-arm mean difference and its variance — and therefore contributes to the narrative review but not to the pooled estimate. No eligible study was excluded from pooling for any reason other than non-extractable data for this specific outcome.

### 2.4 Statistical analysis

The prespecified effect measure was the **Mean Difference (MD) in seconds**, computed as exercise mean minus control mean, so that negative values favour exercise. This measure was applied at the explicit request of the review question (`effect_measure_source: explicit_user_request`) and **native-scale pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used: true`); the reported pooled value is therefore a confirmed native-scale mean difference in seconds and not a standardised fallback.

Pooling used a random-effects model with between-study variance (τ²) estimated by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**, chosen a priori because clinical and methodological diversity in exercise modality, dose, duration, supervision and dialysis modality makes a common-effect assumption untenable. Heterogeneity was characterised by Cochran's Q with its p-value, τ² (absolute between-study variance, in s²) and I² (proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity). For descriptive and moderator purposes, study-level standardised mean differences (Hedges' g, with small-sample correction) were also computed, because g places trials with different measurement variability on a comparable footing for visual inspection and for small-study-effect diagnostics.

Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed by Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and by the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. Cumulative meta-analysis (ordered by precision) and a leave-one-out style inspection of the forest plot were used to probe the influence of individual trials.

Several structural features of this evidence base are worth stating explicitly before the pooled result is interpreted.

1. **Sample size is extremely unevenly distributed.** Study 1 alone contributes 227 of the 451 participants (50.3%), while the remaining six trials range from 24 to 65 participants. Five of the seven trials enrolled fewer than 45 participants in total. Small trials of this size are vulnerable to baseline imbalance and to chance-driven extremes in both directions.
2. **Four of seven comparisons report a statistically significant benefit** (Studies 1, 2, 5 and 8, whose g confidence intervals lie entirely below zero). One further comparison (Study 6) points in the direction of benefit with a confidence interval crossing the null.
3. **Two comparisons (Studies 3 and 7) returned exactly g = 0.000**, meaning the extracted end-of-intervention means were identical, or effectively identical, between the exercise and control arms. Exact null point estimates are unusual and may reflect rounding in the source report, a genuinely inert intervention dose, or a control arm that was itself active (stretching/sham protocols can elicit modest functional gains in a severely deconditioned population, attenuating the contrast).
4. **The largest trial reports the smallest significant effect.** Study 1 (n = 227) yields g = −0.327, whereas the trials reporting the largest effects (Study 5, g = −1.635; Study 8, g = −0.985) are among the smallest (n = 41 and n = 27). This precision–magnitude gradient is the classic signature of either genuine clinical heterogeneity (more intensive, better-supervised programmes in smaller single-centre trials) or small-study effects, and it is examined formally in Sections 7 and 9.

Risk-of-bias assessment at the domain level is constrained by the extraction available here. Two generic limitations apply to essentially all exercise trials in dialysis and should be assumed to apply to this set: **participants cannot be blinded** to allocation, and the STS-5 is an effort-dependent, stopwatch-timed performance test, so performance bias and (where outcome assessors were unblinded) detection bias cannot be excluded. The use of sham/stretching placebo controls in some trials partially mitigates the first of these; usual-care controls do not.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Scale and sign convention

All effects are expressed as exercise minus control. Because the STS-5 outcome is a *time*, **negative values indicate that the exercise group was faster, i.e. better**. This is the inverse of the convention that applies to repetition-count sit-to-stand tests, and is the reason the raw sign of the pooled estimate must not be read naively as "harm".

### 4.2 Distribution of study-level effects

Study-level standardised effects (Table 1) span from 0.000 to −1.635, a range of 1.63 standard-deviation units. Using conventional benchmarks, the set contains two null effects, one small effect (Study 1, g = −0.327), one small-to-moderate effect (Study 6, g = −0.403), one moderate effect (Study 2, g = −0.522), one large effect (Study 8, g = −0.985) and one very large effect (Study 5, g = −1.635). No comparison favoured the control arm: the distribution is bounded at zero on the unfavourable side, with all departures from the null in the direction of exercise benefit.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

### 5.1 Primary pooled estimate

Pooling the **7 comparisons (451 participants)** in a random-effects model with REML estimation gave:

> **Pooled Mean Difference (MD) = −2.467 seconds (95% CI −4.152 to −0.781), p = 0.0041, k = 7, N = 451.**

The effect measure is the **Mean Difference (MD)** in seconds; `native_pooling_used` is **true**, so this is a confirmed native-scale result rather than a standardised fallback converted back onto a nominal scale.

Per the `favors` field, this result **favours the structured exercise training arm** (aerobic, resistance, combined or other; intradialytic or interdialytic). In plain terms: at the end of the intervention period, participants who had undertaken structured exercise completed five sit-to-stand repetitions, on average, **about 2.5 seconds faster** than participants receiving no exercise, usual care, or placebo (sham/stretching) exercise. The 95% confidence interval is compatible with a benefit as small as 0.78 seconds and as large as 4.15 seconds, and excludes the null (p = 0.0041).

### 5.2 Clinical interpretation of the magnitude

A 2.5-second reduction in STS-5 time is not a trivial statistical artefact. In older and clinically vulnerable populations, STS-5 times typically range from roughly 10 seconds (functionally robust) to beyond 15–20 seconds (markedly impaired, with substantially elevated fall and dependency risk), and thresholds around 12–15 seconds are commonly used to flag impaired lower-limb function. Against that backdrop, a mean improvement of ~2.5 seconds corresponds to a meaningful shift along the functional gradient — plausibly enough, for a patient near a clinical cut-point, to move from the impaired to the non-impaired side of it. The lower bound of the confidence interval (0.78 s) would be of marginal clinical consequence, whereas the upper bound (4.15 s) would represent a substantial functional gain, so the data are consistent with anything from a modest to a large practical benefit.

### 5.3 Precision and prediction

The standard error of the pooled mean difference, implied by the confidence interval, is approximately 0.86 seconds. Combining this with the estimated between-study variance (τ² = 3.190 s²) yields an **approximate 95% prediction interval of roughly −7.6 to +2.6 seconds**. This is the single most important nuance in the entire analysis: while the *average* effect across this literature is clearly favourable, the prediction interval indicates that in a new, exchangeable trial the effect could plausibly be a large benefit or no benefit at all (or a trivial disadvantage). The mean effect is robust; its universality is not.

### 5.4 Stability

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`), which adds studies in order of precision, allows inspection of whether the pooled estimate stabilises or drifts as less precise trials enter. Given that the single largest trial (Study 1, n = 227, g = −0.327) carries the smallest effect of the significant studies while the two most extreme effects come from trials of 41 and 27 participants, the pooled mean difference is expected to be sensitive to the smaller, high-effect trials — in particular Study 5, whose effect (g = −1.635) lies well outside the range of the other six. Readers should treat the point estimate of −2.47 seconds as the centre of a genuinely dispersed distribution rather than as a precise universal constant.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **substantial and statistically significant**:

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 20.47 (df = 6) | Observed dispersion exceeds that expected from sampling error alone |
| p-value for Q | 0.0023 | Null of homogeneity rejected |
| I² | 68.55% | Substantial heterogeneity; ~69% of observed variability is between-study rather than within-study |
| τ² | 3.190 s² | Between-study SD of true effects τ ≈ 1.79 seconds |
| Estimator | REML | — |

An I² of 68.55% falls in the range conventionally described as *substantial* heterogeneity, and the significant Q-test (p = 0.0023) confirms that this is unlikely to be a chance pattern in seven studies. The absolute measure is arguably more informative: **τ ≈ 1.79 seconds** means that the true effect of structured exercise on STS-5 time plausibly varies from one trial context to another by roughly ±1.8 seconds around the −2.47-second average — a dispersion of similar order to the mean effect itself. A graphical summary of the heterogeneity statistics is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

Candidate sources of this heterogeneity, all clinically plausible and none testable with precision in seven studies, include:

- **Exercise modality and target.** Resistance and combined programmes load the quadriceps and hip extensors directly, which are the prime movers of the sit-to-stand task; purely aerobic intradialytic cycling may improve cardiorespiratory fitness with less transfer to a maximal-effort strength task. Modality mismatch is a strong a priori explanation for the gap between the near-null and the very large effects.
- **Dose, intensity and progression.** Programme length, session frequency, and above all whether resistance load was genuinely progressed, differ widely across dialysis exercise trials and plausibly drive effect magnitude.
- **Supervision and adherence.** Supervised, in-centre intradialytic programmes typically achieve higher adherence than home-based interdialytic prescriptions; low adherence biases effects towards the null and would be consistent with the two exactly-null comparisons.
- **Comparator activity.** Trials using stretching or sham placebo controls generate a smaller contrast than trials using inert usual care, because the placebo arm itself may improve.
- **Baseline functional status and floor/ceiling effects.** Participants who are already relatively functional have less room to improve on a timed task; cohorts with severe baseline impairment have more.
- **Dialysis modality and timing of testing.** Haemodialysis versus peritoneal dialysis populations differ in volume status, symptom burden and daily routine; testing immediately post-dialysis versus on a non-dialysis day can shift STS-5 times materially.
- **Measurement protocol.** Permitting versus prohibiting arm use, chair height, and whether a practice trial was allowed all affect absolute times and their variability.

## 7. Moderators

A formal moderator (meta-regression or subgroup) analysis was **not conducted**, and this should be stated plainly rather than substituted with speculative subgroup estimates. With k = 7 comparisons, there is approximately one study per candidate covariate at best; conventional guidance requires on the order of ten studies per covariate before meta-regression estimates are interpretable, and subgroup analyses in sets of this size are notoriously unstable and prone to spurious significance.

What *can* be observed descriptively is the relationship between study precision and effect magnitude, visualised in the bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`, where bubble area is proportional to study weight. The pattern is clear and consistent: the single large trial (n = 227) reports g = −0.327, the mid-sized trial (n = 65) reports g = −0.522, and the extreme effects (g = −1.635 and g = −0.985) come from trials of 41 and 27 participants, while the two exactly-null results come from trials of 40 and 24 participants. In other words, the small trials are dispersed widely in both directions around the large trial's modest estimate. This is the expected appearance of genuine heterogeneity in intervention delivery combined with the imprecision of small samples; notably, the *formal* test for funnel asymmetry (Section 8) did not detect a systematic directional small-study effect, because the small trials scatter on both sides rather than clustering on the favourable side alone.

The clinically important moderator questions — which modality, what dose, intradialytic versus interdialytic, and which patients benefit most — therefore remain **unresolved by this dataset** and should be a priority for individual-participant-data synthesis or for adequately powered head-to-head trials.

## 8. Publication Bias

Two complementary diagnostics were applied, both operating on the standardised (Hedges' g) scale on which small-study-effect tests are conventionally defined:

| Diagnostic | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | −0.1693 |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.5955 |
| Trim-and-fill studies imputed (k₀) | 0 |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted standardised estimate | −0.531 (identical to observed, since k₀ = 0) |
| Overall assessed risk | **Low** |

**Interpretation.** Egger's regression test shows no statistically significant funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.5955, well above the conventional 0.10 threshold used for this test), and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), meaning the adjusted estimate is identical to the observed pooled effect. Together these converge on a low risk of publication (small-study) bias, so the meta-analytic estimate is unlikely to be materially inflated by selective reporting or unpublished negative findings. The near-zero Egger intercept (−0.1693) indicates essentially no relationship between a study's precision and the size of its standardised effect once the model's expected relationship is accounted for.

**Caveat worth noting:** these tests have low statistical power, particularly when fewer than ~10 studies are pooled or when between-study heterogeneity is high, so a null result is reassuring but not definitive evidence that bias is absent. With k = 7 and I² = 68.55%, both conditions that degrade power are present simultaneously here. Egger's test in particular can be unreliable — and can even be mis-signed — in the presence of substantial true heterogeneity, and trim-and-fill performs poorly when the funnel asymmetry arises from heterogeneity rather than from suppression. The conclusion should therefore be read as "no evidence of publication bias" rather than "evidence of no publication bias."

A further consideration specific to this field: exercise trials in dialysis are frequently small, single-centre, investigator-initiated studies, a literature structure in which null findings are plausibly under-reported even when formal asymmetry tests are negative. The presence in this set of two exactly-null comparisons is, in that light, mildly reassuring, since it demonstrates that null STS-5 results in this field do get published.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Principal findings

Across 7 controlled comparisons and 451 adults receiving maintenance dialysis, structured exercise training reduced timed five-repetition sit-to-stand time by a pooled **mean difference of 2.467 seconds (95% CI 0.781 to 4.152 seconds faster; p = 0.0041)** relative to no exercise, usual care, or placebo (sham/stretching) exercise. The direction of this result **favours structured exercise training**. The magnitude is clinically as well as statistically meaningful: a ~2.5-second improvement on a task that typically takes impaired dialysis patients 12–20 seconds represents a tangible gain in the ability to rise from a chair unaided, the mechanical foundation of toileting, transferring and independent mobility.

The finding is coherent with the broader physiology. The sit-to-stand task is a concentric, near-maximal loading of the knee and hip extensors, precisely the muscle groups most affected by uraemic myopathy and disuse, and precisely those targeted by resistance and combined training. That the benefit appears on a *timed* functional task — rather than only on isolated strength dynamometry — indicates transfer to a movement pattern of direct daily relevance.

### 9.2 Consistency and inconsistency

Importantly, **no included comparison favoured the control arm.** The dispersion in this literature is between "substantial benefit" and "no detectable benefit", not between benefit and harm. This asymmetry in the direction of dispersion strengthens the qualitative conclusion that structured exercise is, at worst, functionally neutral for STS-5 performance and frequently beneficial.

At the same time, the heterogeneity is real and not reducible to noise (I² = 68.55%, Q p = 0.0023, τ ≈ 1.79 s), and the approximate prediction interval spans −7.6 to +2.6 seconds. The practical reading is that *whether* a dialysis exercise programme improves sit-to-stand performance depends materially on how it is designed and delivered. The two exactly-null comparisons are the most informative outliers in this respect: they indicate that it is entirely possible to run a structured exercise programme in this population and achieve nothing measurable on this endpoint — most plausibly through insufficient resistance loading, inadequate progression, poor adherence, too short a duration, or an active comparator.

### 9.3 Comparison with the wider literature

This result sits comfortably alongside prior syntheses reporting improvements in physical function in dialysis populations, but adds specificity in two respects. First, by restricting to a single, precisely defined outcome (STS-5 in seconds) and excluding related-but-different measures (30-s/60-s repetition counts, timed up-and-go, six-minute walk), it avoids the construct-blending that has complicated interpretation of earlier pooled "physical function" estimates. Second, by pooling on the native second scale rather than in standardised units, it yields a number that clinicians and patients can interpret directly, rather than a standardised effect requiring back-translation.

### 9.4 Implications for practice

For dialysis services, the evidence supports offering structured exercise — most pragmatically, supervised intradialytic programmes that exploit otherwise sedentary treatment time — with the expectation of meaningful improvement in lower-limb functional strength. The heterogeneity analysis implies that programme *content* matters: given the mechanics of the sit-to-stand task, programmes incorporating progressive lower-limb resistance loading are the most physiologically defensible choice when improving chair-rise performance is the goal, and adherence support should be regarded as part of the intervention rather than an implementation detail.

### 9.5 Implications for research

Priorities are: (i) adequately powered trials, since five of seven comparisons here enrolled fewer than 45 participants; (ii) consistent, protocol-standardised STS-5 reporting (chair height, arm position, practice trials, timing relative to dialysis) with full mean/SD/n reporting for both arms at the end of intervention; (iii) direct comparisons of modality and dose rather than further exercise-versus-usual-care trials; and (iv) individual-participant-data pooling, which is the only realistic route to resolving the moderator questions that k = 7 cannot answer.

## 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were raised for this analysis. Two data-handling points nonetheless warrant plain statement:

- **One record of eight did not contribute to the pooled estimate.** The screening pipeline carried 8 study records into analysis, of which 7 yielded extractable arm-level STS-5 data (mean, SD, n for both arms) and were pooled. The remaining record did not provide this outcome in a form permitting computation of a mean difference and its variance, and therefore contributed no effect size. Its omission means the pooled estimate rests on 451 participants rather than the full screened sample.
- **Two comparisons returned an exactly null point estimate (g = 0.000, Studies 3 and 7).** Exact zeros are compatible with genuinely identical arm means but can also arise from rounded reporting in the source publication. These comparisons were retained at face value; they pull the pooled estimate towards the null and widen the heterogeneity estimate.
- **Effect measure and pooling scale.** The pooled result is reported as a Mean Difference (MD) in seconds, specified by explicit request, with native-scale pooling confirmed (`native_pooling_used: true`). Study-level values in Table 1 are standardised (Hedges' g) and are provided for cross-study comparison and for the small-study-effect diagnostics only; they are not the primary effect measure and should not be conflated with the pooled MD. The trim-and-fill adjusted value (−0.531) is likewise on the standardised scale.
- **No double-counting.** Each participant sample contributes a single comparison; no trial contributed more than one arm-pair to the pooled estimate, and no review, protocol or duplicate report entered the quantitative synthesis.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Small number of pooled studies (k = 7).** This limits the precision of τ², renders meta-regression and subgroup analysis uninformative, and substantially weakens publication-bias diagnostics.
2. **Substantial unexplained heterogeneity (I² = 68.55%; τ ≈ 1.79 s).** The pooled mean difference describes an average across clinically diverse programmes; the approximate prediction interval (−7.6 to +2.6 s) crosses the null, so the average effect should not be presumed to transfer to every programme or population.
3. **Dominance of a single trial.** One study supplies 50.3% of participants (227/451) and reports one of the smallest effects; conversely, the most extreme effects come from the smallest trials. The pooled estimate is therefore sensitive to how the random-effects model distributes weight between a single large modest-effect trial and several small large-effect trials, and may lie above the effect that would be observed in a large, well-conducted trial.
4. **No blinding of participants is possible** in exercise trials, and the STS-5 is an effort-dependent, stopwatch-timed measure. Performance and (where assessors were unblinded) detection bias cannot be excluded, and both would tend to exaggerate apparent benefit. Trials using sham/stretching controls mitigate this partially; usual-care comparators do not.
5. **Mixed comparators.** Pooling usual-care and placebo (sham/stretching) controls together conflates two different magnitudes of contrast and contributes to heterogeneity, but k was too small to separate them.
6. **Mixed outcome timing conventions.** End-of-intervention values were preferred, but change-from-baseline data were accepted where only those were reported. Although applied consistently within each study, mixing post-intervention and change scores introduces minor additional variability.
7. **Derived dispersion statistics.** Where SDs were reconstructed from standard errors (SD = SE × √n) or from confidence intervals, the resulting variances inherit any imprecision or rounding in the source reporting.
8. **Outcome-specific scope.** The findings apply to timed five-repetition sit-to-stand performance only. They do not license inference about repetition-count sit-to-stand tests, timed up-and-go, walking endurance, quality of life, hospitalisation or survival, none of which were analysed here.
9. **Generalisability.** The relative contribution of haemodialysis versus peritoneal dialysis participants, and of intradialytic versus interdialytic delivery, could not be quantified; the evidence base in dialysis exercise research is dominated by in-centre haemodialysis cohorts, and applicability to peritoneal dialysis populations is correspondingly uncertain.
10. **Longer-term durability unknown.** All estimates refer to the end of the intervention period. Whether functional gains persist after supervised programmes cease is not addressed.

## 12. Conclusion

Structured exercise training delivered during or between dialysis sessions significantly improves lower-limb functional strength as measured by the timed five-repetition sit-to-stand test. Pooling 7 controlled comparisons and 451 adults on maintenance dialysis, the **pooled Mean Difference was −2.467 seconds (95% CI −4.152 to −0.781; p = 0.0041)**, a result that **favours structured exercise training** — participants completed five chair rises roughly two and a half seconds faster than those receiving no exercise, usual care, or sham/stretching placebo. No included comparison favoured the control arm, and publication-bias diagnostics were reassuring (Egger's p = 0.5955; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0; low assessed risk), albeit underpowered.

Confidence in the *existence* and *direction* of benefit is reasonably high; confidence in its *exact magnitude and universality* is moderate at best, given substantial heterogeneity (I² = 68.55%, τ² = 3.190 s², Q p = 0.0023), a small number of trials, the dominance of one large study reporting a modest effect, and an approximate prediction interval that includes the null. The practical message for nephrology services is that structured exercise should be offered as a means of preserving chair-rise capacity and independent mobility, with programme design — in particular progressive lower-limb resistance loading, adequate duration and genuine adherence support — treated as the decisive variable. The key research need is not further exercise-versus-usual-care trials but adequately powered, protocol-standardised comparisons of modality and dose, ideally synthesised at the individual-participant level.
