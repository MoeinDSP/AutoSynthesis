# Structured Exercise Training and Timed Sit-to-Stand Performance in Adults Receiving Maintenance Dialysis: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Lower-limb functional impairment is common among adults receiving maintenance dialysis. This review evaluated whether structured exercise training improves performance on a timed five-repetition, or otherwise eligible five-to-ten-repetition, sit-to-stand test compared with usual care, no exercise, or placebo/sham exercise.

**Methods:** Eligible interventions comprised structured aerobic, resistance, combined, or other exercise delivered during or between haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis sessions. The outcome was completion time in seconds at the end of the intervention; lower times indicate better performance. Eight studies were included in the review, and five comparisons (184 participants) contributed to the quantitative synthesis. A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled the raw exercise-minus-control mean difference (MD). Individual standardized effects supplied by the analytic dataset are also reported descriptively.

**Results:** The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was −3.00999087993375 seconds (95% CI −5.283 to −0.737; p=0.009437603851188588), favoring structured exercise. Heterogeneity was substantial (I²=73.46%; τ²=4.579584; Q=18.888, p=0.0008). The five supplied individual standardized effects ranged from 0.000000 to −1.634863. Egger’s intercept was −0.7841 (p=0.9694), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀=0); publication-bias risk was classified as low. These analyses have limited power with only five comparisons.

**Conclusions:** Structured exercise training was associated with a statistically significant reduction of approximately 3.01 seconds in timed sit-to-stand completion time at intervention end, favoring exercise. Substantial between-study heterogeneity, incomplete study-level reporting in the supplied dataset, and the small number of pooled comparisons limit certainty and generalizability.

# Introduction

Adults receiving maintenance haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis frequently experience muscle wasting, impaired lower-limb strength, reduced mobility, and difficulty performing activities of daily living. The timed sit-to-stand test integrates lower-extremity strength, balance, coordination, and functional transfer capacity. Unlike fixed-duration sit-to-stand tests, which count repetitions completed in a specified interval, the outcome considered here was the time required to complete a specified number of repetitions. A shorter completion time represents better performance.

This review addressed the following question: among adults receiving maintenance haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis, what is the effect of structured exercise training—whether aerobic, resistance, combined, or another structured programme delivered during or between dialysis sessions—compared with usual care, no exercise, or placebo/sham exercise, on timed sit-to-stand completion time at the end of the intervention?

# Search and Screening

## Eligibility criteria

The prespecified eligibility framework was:

- **Population:** Adults receiving maintenance haemodialysis or peritoneal dialysis.
- **Intervention:** Structured aerobic, resistance, combined, or other exercise training delivered during or between dialysis sessions.
- **Comparator:** No exercise, usual care, or placebo/sham exercise such as stretching.
- **Outcome:** Time in seconds required to complete five sit-to-stand repetitions, or an otherwise eligible timed five-to-ten-repetition version, measured at the end of the exercise programme.
- **Eligible data:** Mean, standard deviation, and number analysed or randomized in each arm. End-of-intervention values were preferred; change scores were eligible only when reported for both arms and were not to be mixed with endpoint values.
- **Excluded outcomes:** Fixed-duration repetition counts, including 30- and 60-second tests; repetitions-per-time outcomes; post-programme follow-up assessments; timed up-and-go; six-minute walk distance; and all other noneligible outcomes.

Where needed, standard errors were to be converted to standard deviations using \(SD=SE\times\sqrt{n}\), table footnotes defining “±” were to be checked, and all completion times were to be expressed in seconds.

## Search and selection information available

The supplied results identify **8 included studies**, of which **5 comparisons** supplied effect sizes for quantitative synthesis. No database names, platform details, search strings, search dates, language restrictions, deduplication counts, title/abstract screening counts, full-text exclusion reasons, or protocol registration information were provided. For this outcome, an MD below zero represents a shorter completion time in the exercise arm and therefore better performance. # Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model fitted using restricted maximum likelihood produced a pooled **Mean Difference (MD) of −3.00999087993375 seconds** (95% CI **−5.283 to −0.737**; **p=0.009437603851188588**; **k=5**; **n=184**). The result favored structured exercise training.

Thus, at the end of the intervention, participants assigned to structured exercise completed the eligible timed sit-to-stand test approximately **3.01 seconds faster**, on average, than participants assigned to usual care, no exercise, or placebo/sham exercise. The confidence interval excluded the null value of 0 seconds, indicating statistical significance at the conventional two-sided 0.05 level.

# Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was substantial:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 18.888
- **Degrees of freedom:** 4
- **Q-test p value:** 0.0008
- **I²:** 73.46%
- **τ²:** 4.579584 seconds²

The significant Q test and I² of 73.46% indicate that a considerable proportion of observed variability was attributable to between-study differences rather than sampling error alone. The estimated between-study variance, τ²=4.579584 seconds², corresponds to an estimated between-study standard deviation of approximately 2.140 seconds on the raw MD scale.

Potential sources include differences in exercise modality, programme dose, intradialytic versus interdialytic delivery, participant frailty, dialysis modality, comparator activity, test protocol, repetition count, and the use of endpoint versus change scores. These explanations remain hypothetical because study-level characteristics were not supplied.

# Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−0.7841** with **p=0.9694**, providing no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **0 missing studies (k₀=0)**, and the supplied standardized trim-and-fill estimate was **−0.7116**. Because no studies were imputed, trim-and-fill did not indicate detectable suppression on the assessed side of the funnel. The overall publication-bias risk was classified as **low**.

These findings require caution. With only five comparisons, Egger’s test has very low power, funnel-plot patterns are difficult to distinguish from chance, and trim-and-fill can perform poorly in the presence of substantial heterogeneity. The non-significant test does not establish the absence of reporting bias. In addition, the supplied trim-and-fill value is on a standardized scale and should not be compared numerically with the pooled raw-second MD.

# Discussion

## Principal findings

This meta-analysis found that structured exercise training significantly improved timed sit-to-stand performance among adults receiving maintenance dialysis. Across five comparisons and 184 participants, exercise reduced completion time by **3.00999087993375 seconds** relative to control, with a 95% confidence interval from **0.737 to 5.283 seconds faster**. This direction is consistent with improved lower-limb functional performance because a shorter time is preferable.

The result is statistically significant, but its clinical importance cannot be established confidently from the supplied evidence. No minimally important difference for the specific dialysis population and eligible sit-to-stand protocols was provided. Interpretation is further complicated if studies used different numbers of repetitions within the allowed five-to-ten-repetition range, because raw completion times and the meaning of a given absolute time difference may vary by protocol.

## Interpretation of heterogeneity

The pooled result represents an average across heterogeneous effects. With I²=73.46% and a significant Q test, the magnitude of benefit likely varied materially among studies. Such variation is plausible because “structured exercise” encompasses diverse interventions, from aerobic cycling to progressive resistance or combined training, and because intervention frequency, intensity, supervision, adherence, and timing relative to dialysis may differ.

The anonymous dataset does not permit these explanations to be tested. The absence of moderator results means the pooled average should not be assumed to apply equally to every exercise programme or dialysis population.

## Consistency of available effects

The supplied standardized study-level effects varied considerably, from no observed standardized difference in study_6_R6 to a large negative estimate in study_4_R4. Three standardized confidence intervals excluded zero, while two did not. This variability is qualitatively compatible with the substantial heterogeneity detected in the raw MD synthesis, although standardized effects and raw mean differences are different metrics and should not be compared numerically.

## Implications for practice

The pooled evidence supports structured exercise as a potentially effective strategy for improving lower-limb functional performance during the intervention period. Exercise prescription should nevertheless account for cardiovascular stability, musculoskeletal limitations, vascular access, fall risk, comorbidity, and dialysis-related symptoms. The results do not identify a superior exercise modality, dose, or delivery setting.

## Implications for research

Future trials should:

1. Prespecify a single timed sit-to-stand protocol and report the exact number of repetitions.
2. Report arm-level endpoint means, standard deviations, and numbers analysed, as well as change scores where relevant.
3. Distinguish SD from SE/SEM explicitly in tables and footnotes.
4. Describe intervention intensity, progression, adherence, fidelity, supervision, and adverse events.
5. Report dialysis modality and whether exercise occurred during or between dialysis sessions.
6. Use concealed allocation, blinded outcome assessment where feasible, and intention-to-treat analysis.
7. Establish or validate a minimally important difference for timed sit-to-stand completion time in the maintenance-dialysis population.
8. Provide sufficiently detailed results to permit subgroup analyses without double-counting participants.

# Limitations

Several limitations constrain interpretation:

1. **Small evidence base:** Only five comparisons and 184 participants contributed to the pooled estimate.
2. **Substantial heterogeneity:** I² was 73.46%, and the Q test was significant (p=0.0008).
3. **Incomplete study descriptions:** The supplied results lacked bibliographic details, population characteristics, intervention protocols, comparator descriptions, and dialysis modality by study.
4. **Incomplete screening information:** Search sources, dates, search strategies, screening counts, and full-text exclusion reasons were unavailable, preventing full verification of PRISMA search and selection procedures.
5. **Unexplained noncontribution:** Eight studies were included, but only five supplied pooled effects; reasons for the other three studies’ absence from the synthesis were not reported.
6. **Risk of bias unavailable:** No domain-level or overall risk-of-bias assessments were supplied.
7. **Endpoint versus change status unclear:** It was not possible to verify whether each comparison used endpoint data or change-from-baseline data, although these should not be mixed across arms.
8. **Protocol comparability uncertain:** The exact repetition count and testing procedures were not reported for each study.
9. **Metric inconsistency in supplied outputs:** Study-level effects were supplied as standardized Hedges’ g values, whereas the confirmed primary synthesis used a native-scale raw MD in seconds. The reported pooled MD was retained, but the underlying arm-level means and SDs were unavailable for independent verification.
10. **Direction-label inconsistency:** The negative study-level standardized values do not conform to the stated positive-is-better sign-harmonization convention; they were therefore reported without directional reinterpretation.
11. **Publication-bias analyses are underpowered:** Egger’s test and trim-and-fill are unreliable with as few as five comparisons, particularly when heterogeneity is substantial.
12. **Clinical importance uncertain:** No validated minimally important difference was supplied for this outcome and population.
13. **Safety and durability not assessed:** Adverse events and post-programme follow-up were outside the available outcome synthesis, so persistence of benefit and comparative safety cannot be determined.

# Conclusion

Among adults receiving maintenance dialysis, structured exercise training produced a statistically significant improvement in end-of-intervention timed sit-to-stand performance compared with usual care, no exercise, or placebo/sham exercise. The REML random-effects pooled **Mean Difference was −3.00999087993375 seconds** (95% CI **−5.283 to −0.737**; p=**0.009437603851188588**), favoring structured exercise.

The finding suggests an average reduction of approximately 3.01 seconds in completion time, but substantial heterogeneity, limited sample size, incomplete study-level information, and unavailable risk-of-bias assessments reduce confidence in the precision and generalizability of the estimated benefit. Larger, rigorously reported trials using standardized timed sit-to-stand protocols are needed to determine which exercise programmes are most effective and whether the observed improvement is clinically important and sustained.
