# Fibrin-Based Haemostatic Agents Versus Non-Fibrin Haemostatic Agents or Techniques and Perioperative All-Cause Mortality in Adults Undergoing Liver Resection: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Randomised Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Diffuse bleeding from the transected hepatic parenchyma remains a principal determinant of morbidity after liver resection, and a wide range of topical haemostatic products is marketed for this indication. Fibrin-based agents (fibrin sealants, fibrin glues, fibrin patches and fibrin-coated collagen carriers) are among the most widely used, but whether their adoption translates into any change in the hardest and least biased outcome — death — has not been established.

**Objective.** To determine whether, in adults undergoing hepatic resection, fibrin-based haemostatic agents compared with non-fibrin haemostatic agents or techniques (oxidised cellulose, collagen sponge, argon beam coagulation, electrocautery, or standard surgical haemostasis) change perioperative all-cause mortality.

**Methods.** A systematic review of randomised controlled trials was undertaken and analysed according to PRISMA 2020 reporting standards. Eligible trials randomised adults undergoing liver resection to a fibrin-based topical haemostatic agent versus any non-fibrin comparator and reported the number of deaths from any cause in hospital or within approximately 30–90 days of surgery, with intention-to-treat denominators. Arms with zero deaths were retained as 0 events with their randomised arm size; trials not reporting mortality were excluded. In multi-arm trials the fibrin arm was compared with a single non-fibrin comparator, pooling two non-fibrin arms where present. No outcome other than perioperative all-cause mortality was pooled. The pre-specified effect measure was the risk ratio (RR); trials were pooled on the native risk-ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, I² and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression test and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

**Results.** Ten randomised trials contributing 10 independent effect estimates and 1,993 randomised participants were included. The pooled risk ratio for perioperative all-cause mortality was **RR = 1.127 (95% CI 0.634 to 2.004; p = 0.6828; k = 10; N = 1,993)**. By the direction field carried with the analysis, this result **favours neither arm** — it is essentially a statistical tie, with a confidence interval that is compatible with both a clinically meaningful reduction (approximately a 37% relative decrease) and a clinically meaningful increase (approximately a doubling) in perioperative death. Between-study heterogeneity was low and not statistically significant (Q = 9.899, df = 9, p = 0.3587; I² = 13.74%; τ² = 0.117626). Risk of publication/small-study bias was judged low: Egger's intercept was 0.3024 (p = 0.7145) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0, adjusted standardized estimate 0.1035). No moderator or meta-regression analysis was pre-specified or executed, and the available number of trials would not have supported reliable subgroup inference.

**Conclusions.** In adults undergoing liver resection, fibrin-based haemostatic agents neither reduce nor increase perioperative all-cause mortality to any detectable degree; the evidence is consistent with no effect, but the confidence interval remains wide and the analysis is underpowered for a rare event. Mortality should not be used as a justification for — or against — the routine use of fibrin-based topical haemostats in hepatic surgery, and decisions should instead rest on process outcomes, safety, and cost, none of which were pooled here.


## 1. Introduction

Hepatic resection is performed for primary and secondary liver malignancy, benign tumours, living-donor transplantation and trauma. Unlike most solid-organ surgery, the liver presents a large, vascularised, biliary-bearing raw surface after transection, from which low-pressure parenchymal ooze and occult bile leakage may continue despite meticulous suture and energy-device haemostasis. Persistent raw-surface bleeding is associated with transfusion, coagulopathy, longer operating time, and — by extension — postoperative morbidity and, potentially, death.

A substantial industry of topical haemostatic agents has grown around this problem. Fibrin-based products reconstitute the terminal step of the coagulation cascade at the cut surface by delivering concentrated fibrinogen and thrombin, either as a sprayed or dripped liquid sealant ("fibrin glue"), as a pre-formed patch, or as a fibrinogen/thrombin-coated collagen carrier. Their competitors include oxidised regenerated cellulose, gelatin and collagen sponges with or without thrombin, polyethylene-glycol and albumin–glutaraldehyde polymers, and purely physical or energy-based techniques such as argon beam coagulation, electrocautery, saline-linked radiofrequency devices, and conventional suture/clip haemostasis.

Randomised trials in this field have very largely been powered for surrogate or process endpoints: intraoperative blood loss, time to haemostasis, proportion of patients achieving haemostasis at 3, 5 or 10 minutes, drain volume, transfusion requirement, bile leak, and length of stay. Mortality, when reported at all, is typically reported as a safety descriptor rather than an endpoint. This creates a genuine evidence gap: topical haemostats are expensive, their routine use is often defended on the grounds of patient safety, and yet the question of whether they change the probability of dying around the time of liver resection has rarely been synthesised in isolation.

The present systematic review and meta-analysis addresses exactly one question, deliberately and narrowly:

> In adults undergoing liver (hepatic) resection, do fibrin-based haemostatic agents (fibrin sealant, fibrin glue, fibrin patches, fibrin-coated collagen), compared with non-fibrin haemostatic agents or techniques, change perioperative all-cause mortality?

No other outcome — blood loss, time to haemostasis, transfusion, bile leak, reoperation, or infection — is pooled or reported as a pooled estimate here. This restriction is intentional: it preserves the interpretability of a single, hard, objectively ascertained endpoint and avoids the multiplicity that characterises much of the existing literature on topical haemostats.

**Direction of effect convention.** Throughout this report, an effect size favouring the fibrin-based agent (RR < 1) denotes *fewer* perioperative deaths with the fibrin-based agent than with the non-fibrin comparator; RR > 1 denotes *more* deaths with the fibrin-based agent.

## 2. Methods: Search and Screening

### 2.1 Reporting standard and protocol

The review was conducted and is reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement for systematic reviews and meta-analyses. The clinical question was framed in PICO terms before data extraction:

| Element | Specification |
|---|---|
| **Population (P)** | Adults (≥18 years) undergoing liver (hepatic) resection of any extent, for any indication, by open or minimally invasive approach |
| **Intervention (I)** | Fibrin-based haemostatic agent — fibrin sealant, fibrin glue, fibrin patch, or fibrinogen/thrombin-coated collagen carrier — applied to the resection surface |
| **Comparator (C)** | Any non-fibrin haemostatic agent or technique, including oxidised (regenerated) cellulose, collagen or gelatin sponge, argon beam coagulation, electrocautery, or standard/conventional surgical haemostasis |
| **Outcome (O)** | Perioperative all-cause mortality: number of randomised participants who died in hospital or within approximately 30–90 days of liver resection, as defined by each trial |
| **Study design (S)** | Randomised controlled trials (parallel-group; multi-arm trials eligible) |

### 2.2 Information sources and search strategy

The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms across three concept blocks — (i) liver/hepatic resection, hepatectomy, hepatic surgery; (ii) fibrin sealant, fibrin glue, fibrinogen–thrombin, fibrin patch, fibrin-coated collagen, and the principal proprietary product families; and (iii) randomised trial design filters — applied to the major biomedical bibliographic databases and trial registries. Reference lists of retrieved trials, prior systematic reviews and narrative reviews were hand-searched for additional eligible reports, and multiple publications of the same trial were linked to a single study record.

### 2.3 Eligibility criteria

**Inclusion.** (1) Randomised allocation; (2) adult participants undergoing hepatic resection; (3) a fibrin-based topical haemostatic agent in at least one arm; (4) a non-fibrin haemostatic agent or technique (including no topical agent / standard surgical haemostasis) in at least one comparator arm; (5) reporting of the number of deaths from any cause during the perioperative window (in-hospital, or approximately 30–90 days), with the number of participants in each arm available.

**Exclusion.** (1) Non-randomised designs, including cohort, case–control, registry and single-arm series; (2) narrative or systematic reviews, editorials, letters and conference abstracts without extractable arm-level mortality data; (3) trials in which both arms received a fibrin-based agent (dose, delivery-device or formulation comparisons), since these do not address the fibrin-versus-non-fibrin contrast; (4) trials in non-hepatic surgery or in paediatric populations; (5) **trials not reporting perioperative mortality at all**; (6) reports of treatment-related deaths only (rather than all-cause deaths) and reports of late mortality only (≥1 year), which do not satisfy the outcome definition; (7) duplicate reports of an already-included trial.

### 2.4 Outcome definition and data handling rules

Perioperative all-cause mortality was extracted as the number of events and the number of randomised participants in each arm. The following pre-specified handling rules were applied:

- **Intention-to-treat denominators** were used wherever available. Where only a safety or as-treated population was reported, that denominator was used and the deviation noted.
- **Zero-event arms** were retained in the dataset as 0 events with the full arm size, rather than being discarded; double-zero studies contribute no information to a risk-ratio pooling but are documented.
- **Deaths from any cause only** were counted. Trials reporting exclusively "treatment-related" or "device-related" deaths were not credited with a mortality outcome.
- **Multi-arm trials** contributed a single comparison: the fibrin arm versus one non-fibrin comparator. Where two eligible non-fibrin arms existed, events and participants were summed into one composite comparator arm, so that no participant and no event was double-counted and the unit-of-analysis assumption of independence was preserved.

### 2.5 Screening and selection

Records were screened in two stages (title/abstract, then full text) against the criteria above. Records identified as reviews, duplicate publications of the same trial, or trials silent on mortality were removed at full-text stage. The selection process ultimately yielded **10 randomised trials contributing 10 independent effect estimates and 1,993 randomised participants** to the quantitative synthesis.

A transparency note on study identifiers: the included-study identifiers carried through the analytic pipeline are non-contiguous (R1–R6 and R8–R11, with no R7 in the analysed set). This is consistent with at least one otherwise potentially relevant randomised report having been screened and then set aside at the data-extraction stage — the pre-specified reason for such removal being failure to report perioperative all-cause mortality, or removal of a duplicate report of an already-included trial. Because bibliographic metadata for the individual trials was not transmitted with the analytic dataset, this report refers to the included trials by their pipeline identifiers (Study 1/R1 through Study 11/R11) rather than by author and year; readers requiring the primary citations should consult the accompanying study-characteristics file (see §11, References).

### 2.6 Statistical analysis

The pre-specified and user-requested effect measure was the **risk ratio (RR)** for perioperative all-cause mortality, and pooling was performed **on the native risk-ratio scale** (`effect_measure = RR`; `computation_scale = RR`; `native_pooling_used = true`; measure source: explicit analytic specification; no effect-measure conflict was recorded). Accordingly, the headline pooled statistic in this report is a **pooled risk ratio**, not a standardized mean difference and not Hedges' *g*.

Trials were combined under a **random-effects model** with between-study variance estimated by **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)**, reflecting the clinical and methodological diversity of resection indications, extents, comparators and mortality ascertainment windows across trials. Heterogeneity was characterised by Cochran's Q with its p-value, the I² statistic, and τ² (the absolute between-study variance on the log-risk-ratio scale). Small-study and publication bias were assessed by Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and by the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure.

Analyses were performed within a standardised meta-analytic pipeline; figures generated are referenced by path in the relevant sections below.

### 3.1 Risk of bias

A formal domain-level risk-of-bias assessment (e.g. Cochrane RoB 2) was not transmitted with the analytic dataset and is therefore not reported as a quantitative input to the synthesis. Two generic considerations nonetheless apply to this literature and should temper interpretation. First, **blinding of surgeons** to a topical haemostatic agent applied under direct vision is effectively impossible, so performance bias cannot be excluded for process outcomes; all-cause mortality, however, is a relatively objective endpoint and is comparatively robust to unblinded assessment. Second, because mortality was in almost all of these trials a **secondary or safety endpoint rather than the primary endpoint**, selective-reporting risk for this specific outcome is non-trivial — the exclusion of trials that measured haemostasis but never reported deaths is itself an expression of that risk.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Primary (native-scale) effect measure

The analysis was executed on the **risk-ratio scale**, which is the appropriate and pre-specified measure for a binary, relatively rare event such as perioperative death, and which is directly interpretable as a relative risk of dying with a fibrin-based agent versus a non-fibrin comparator. Native-scale pooling was confirmed (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the pooled statistic reported in §5 is a genuine **pooled Risk Ratio (RR)** and not a standardized surrogate.

### 4.2 Per-study estimates as transmitted, and an important interpretive caveat

The study-level estimates transmitted with the dataset (Table 1) are expressed on the **standardized mean-difference (Hedges' *g*) scale**, which is the pipeline's internal common-currency representation used for display, for the funnel/asymmetry diagnostics, and for the cumulative and distributional plots. These per-study *g* values should **not** be read as risk differences or as relative risks of death, and their signs do **not** map onto the risk-ratio sign convention used for the pooled result. Specifically:

- The standardized values range from **−0.98161** (Study 1) to **+0.669833** (Study 9), a spread of approximately 1.65 standardized units.
- Four of ten estimates are negative (Studies 1, 2, 5, 8) and six are positive (Studies 3, 4, 6, 9, 10, 11).
- Seven of ten standardized confidence intervals exclude zero (Studies 1, 4, 5, 6, 8, 9, 10, 11 — eight, in fact), while two (Studies 2 and 3) straddle zero. The apparent precision of these intervals is a property of the standardized transformation and of the arm sizes, and it substantially overstates the precision actually available for a rare binary outcome. This is precisely why the native risk-ratio pooling, and not the standardized representation, is the basis of the primary inference.

Readers are therefore directed to the pooled risk ratio and its confidence interval (§5) as the substantive result, and to the per-study standardized values only as a descriptive and diagnostic layer.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

### 5.1 Primary pooled estimate

Pooling all ten trials on the native risk-ratio scale with a REML random-effects model yielded:

> **Pooled Risk Ratio (RR) = 1.127 (95% CI 0.634 to 2.004), p = 0.6828; k = 10 trials; N = 1,993 randomised participants; τ² = 0.117626; I² = 13.74%; method: REML.**

**Direction.** As recorded in the analysis, this result **favours neither arm** — it is essentially a tie. The point estimate sits 12.7% above unity, nominally in the direction of more perioperative deaths with fibrin-based agents, but the p-value of 0.6828 indicates that this displacement is wholly indistinguishable from chance, and the confidence interval comfortably contains the null value of 1.00. It is important to state the direction neutrally and not to over-read the sign of the point estimate: for a ratio measure such as this, favourability depends on which event was counted, and a point estimate of 1.127 with a confidence interval from 0.634 to 2.004 carries no directional information of clinical value.

**Precision and clinical compatibility.** The confidence interval is wide. Its lower bound (RR 0.634) is compatible with a **36.6% relative reduction** in perioperative death with fibrin-based agents; its upper bound (RR 2.004) is compatible with a **doubling** of perioperative death. Both of these boundary scenarios would, if true, be clinically decisive, and the data cannot discriminate between them. The honest summary is therefore: *no detectable difference, with the data unable to exclude moderate-to-large effects in either direction.*

**Approximate prediction interval.** Taking the pooled log-risk-ratio (ln 1.127 ≈ 0.120), its standard error implied by the reported confidence interval (≈ 0.294), and τ² = 0.117626, the approximate 95% prediction interval for the true effect in a new, exchangeable trial spans roughly **RR 0.40 to 3.19**. This derived quantity — which should be read as indicative rather than definitive, given that k = 10 — underlines that the synthesis constrains the plausible effect only loosely.

**Absolute terms.** Perioperative mortality after elective hepatic resection in contemporary high-volume practice is typically of the order of 1–3%. Against such a baseline, a pooled RR of 1.127 corresponds to an absolute risk difference on the order of a fraction of one percentage point, and the confidence limits translate to roughly one fewer death per 100 patients at the favourable extreme and one to three additional deaths per 100 at the unfavourable extreme. The event count available across 1,993 participants is simply too small to resolve differences of this magnitude.

### 5.2 Multiple outcome groups

The analytic dataset contained a **single** meta-analytic entry (one outcome, one timepoint definition: perioperative all-cause mortality as defined by each trial). There were no additional `meta_analysis_groups` entries requiring separate reporting, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data. Accordingly, one pooled estimate is reported, and no estimates have been merged across distinct outcomes or timepoints.

The forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) displays the ten study-level estimates and the pooled summary; the cumulative plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows that the running pooled estimate does not drift systematically as trials accumulate and that no single trial reverses the overall null conclusion.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was **low and not statistically significant**:

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 9.899 (df = 9) | Observed dispersion barely exceeds the degrees of freedom |
| Q p-value | 0.3587 | No significant between-study inconsistency |
| I² | 13.74% | Only ~14% of observed variability attributable to true between-study differences rather than sampling error; conventionally "low" |
| τ² | 0.117626 | Between-study variance on the log-risk-ratio scale; τ ≈ 0.343 |
| Model | REML random-effects | Retained despite low I², consistent with pre-specification |

The combination of Q = 9.899 (p = 0.3587) and I² = 13.74% indicates that the ten trials are statistically consistent with one another: the spread of their mortality risk ratios is close to what would be expected from sampling variation alone. This is a reassuring finding for a synthesis of trials that differed in comparator type, resection extent, indication and mortality ascertainment window — it suggests that no single trial is a gross outlier on the native scale, and that the near-null pooled estimate is not an artefact of averaging strongly opposed effects.

Two cautions attach to this reading. First, I² is a *relative* measure, and in a dataset dominated by sampling error (as rare-event mortality data inevitably are), a low I² can reflect large within-study variance rather than genuine agreement between trials. Second, τ² = 0.117626 (τ ≈ 0.343 on the log scale) is not negligible in absolute terms: it corresponds to meaningful dispersion of true effects around the pooled mean, and is the reason the prediction interval (§5.1) is so much wider than the confidence interval. Low I² should therefore not be mistaken for a demonstration of homogeneity of true effects.

## 7. Moderators

**No moderator, subgroup or meta-regression analysis was pre-specified or performed, and none could be performed with the data transmitted for synthesis.**

This is a substantive limitation rather than an omission of convenience, and the reasons are worth stating plainly:

1. **No trial-level covariates were available.** The analytic dataset carried only study identifier, sample size, and effect estimate with confidence interval. Variables that would be clinically informative as moderators — the *identity of the comparator* (oxidised cellulose versus collagen sponge versus argon beam versus plain surgical haemostasis), the *form of the fibrin agent* (liquid sealant versus patch versus fibrin-coated collagen), *resection extent* (major ≥3 segments versus minor), *indication* (malignant versus benign; cirrhotic versus non-cirrhotic liver), *surgical approach* (open versus laparoscopic), *publication year*, *risk-of-bias status*, and the precise *mortality ascertainment window* (in-hospital versus 30-day versus 90-day) — were not present and could not be reconstructed.
2. **Insufficient trials for reliable inference.** With k = 10, conventional guidance (a minimum of roughly 10 studies per covariate) would permit at most a single moderator, and even then with very low power and a high risk of spurious findings. Subgroup analyses by comparator type would have left two to four trials per subgroup.
3. **Low residual heterogeneity leaves little to explain.** With I² = 13.74% and Q p = 0.3587, there is minimal systematic between-study variance for a moderator to account for. Even a well-specified meta-regression would have had very little signal to work with.

The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) displays effect estimates against study size/precision and serves as an informal, descriptive check for a size–effect relationship; it is not a fitted meta-regression and no coefficient, slope, p-value or R² is claimed. Inspected alongside the formal small-study diagnostics in §8, it offers no indication that effect magnitude varies systematically with trial size.

Any future update of this review should prioritise extraction of the covariates listed above, because the clinically most pressing version of this question is not "fibrin versus everything else" but "fibrin versus *which* alternative, in *which* resection".

## 8. Publication Bias

Two complementary assessments of small-study effects were performed, and both were negative:

| Test | Result | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Egger's regression (intercept) | 0.3024 | Intercept close to zero |
| Egger's test p-value | 0.7145 | No statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry |
| Trim-and-fill: studies imputed (k₀) | 0 | No studies required to restore funnel symmetry |
| Trim-and-fill: adjusted estimate | 0.1035 (standardized scale) | Adjusted estimate essentially unchanged from the observed estimate |
| Overall judgement | **Low risk** | Symmetric distribution of effects around the pooled estimate |

Egger's regression test shows no statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.7145), and the Duval–Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0), indicating a symmetric distribution of effect sizes around the pooled estimate. Together these converge on a **low risk of publication (small-study) bias**, suggesting that the meta-analytic estimate is unlikely to be materially inflated by selective reporting. Note that the trim-and-fill adjusted value of 0.1035 is expressed on the standardized scale used for the asymmetry diagnostics, not as a risk ratio, and that because k₀ = 0 the adjustment is a formality: the observed and adjusted summaries coincide.

Three qualifications are essential. First, **these methods have limited statistical power when the number of included studies is small or between-study heterogeneity is high**, and k = 10 sits at the lower boundary of what is usually considered interpretable for Egger's test; the null result should therefore be read as an *absence of detectable asymmetry* rather than as proof that no bias exists. Second, funnel-based diagnostics perform poorly for **rare binary outcomes**, where the relationship between effect size and standard error is distorted by low event counts; in such settings, asymmetry tests can be both underpowered and biased. Third, and most importantly for this particular review, the most plausible reporting bias here is **not** classical publication bias but **outcome-reporting bias**: trials of topical haemostats are published whether or not they report deaths, and the trials excluded from this synthesis were excluded precisely because they were silent on mortality. Neither Egger's test nor trim-and-fill can detect that form of selective reporting, because the missing information sits inside published papers rather than in unpublished ones. The low-risk judgement should therefore be understood as applying to the trials that *did* report mortality, not to the mortality evidence base as a whole.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Principal finding

Across ten randomised trials and 1,993 adults undergoing hepatic resection, fibrin-based haemostatic agents produced **no detectable change in perioperative all-cause mortality** relative to non-fibrin haemostatic agents or techniques (pooled RR 1.127, 95% CI 0.634 to 2.004, p = 0.6828). The result favours neither arm. Between-study heterogeneity was low (I² = 13.74%; Q p = 0.3587), and there was no evidence of small-study bias (Egger's p = 0.7145; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0). The synthesis is thus internally consistent and statistically unremarkable — which is, in this instance, its main message.

### 9.2 Interpretation in context

This null finding is neither surprising nor uninformative. It is consistent with a straightforward causal account: topical haemostatic agents act on the *surface* of the transected liver and plausibly influence surface-related process outcomes (time to haemostasis, drain output, perhaps transfusion) without addressing the dominant drivers of perioperative death after hepatectomy — namely post-hepatectomy liver failure, major vascular injury and massive intraoperative haemorrhage, sepsis from biliary leakage or abscess, and decompensation of underlying cirrhosis or cardiopulmonary comorbidity. A product that shortens time to haemostasis of parenchymal ooze from, say, five minutes to two minutes is unlikely, on mechanistic grounds alone, to alter the probability of dying.

The finding also reflects a structural feature of this literature. Trials of topical haemostats are designed and powered for surrogate endpoints with event rates in the tens of percent; mortality in elective hepatic resection in the trial-eligible population is typically 1–3%. A pooled sample of 1,993 participants will contain only a few tens of deaths at most, which is an order of magnitude short of what would be required to detect or exclude a modest relative effect. In this sense the meta-analysis is best understood as establishing the **absence of evidence of a mortality signal**, rather than evidence of equivalence.

A pragmatic corollary follows. If fibrin-based agents are to be used, the case for them must be made on the outcomes they plausibly affect — speed and completeness of haemostasis, transfusion avoidance, bile-leak prevention — and weighed against their considerable acquisition cost and their theoretical risks (allergic reaction, transmission concerns with pooled plasma-derived products, rare thromboembolic or air-embolism events with pressurised spray application). None of those outcomes were pooled in this review, by design, and nothing here should be read as supporting or refuting any of them.

### 9.3 Implications for practice

For the clinician, the practical implication is narrow but clear: **perioperative mortality is not a valid basis for choosing between a fibrin-based haemostat and a non-fibrin alternative in adult liver resection.** The available randomised evidence does not show that fibrin sealants save lives, and equally does not show that they cost lives. Decisions should therefore be driven by intraoperative performance, local cost and availability, and patient-specific factors, with the recognition that the mortality question remains formally open rather than settled.

For health-technology assessment and formulary committees, the corollary is that cost-effectiveness models for these products should not assume a mortality benefit. Modelled survival gains, where they appear in manufacturer submissions, are not supported by the pooled randomised evidence synthesised here.

### 9.4 Implications for research

Three recommendations follow. First, future trials of topical haemostats in hepatic surgery should **report all-cause mortality as a standard, pre-specified safety outcome with an explicit window (90-day mortality is preferable to in-hospital or 30-day, since it captures late post-hepatectomy liver failure and readmission deaths)**, regardless of the primary endpoint. Second, **trial-level covariates should be reported in a form that permits comparator-specific and agent-specific synthesis**; the aggregate "fibrin versus non-fibrin" contrast conflates several quite different clinical comparisons. Third, because no feasible single trial will be powered for mortality in this setting, **individual-participant-data pooling or a large pragmatic registry-based randomised trial** is the only realistic route to resolving the question — and it is reasonable to ask whether that resolution is worth its cost, given the low prior probability of a true mortality effect.

## 10. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were attached to this analysis — the analytic record contained no `validation_flags` entries, no effect-measure conflict (`effect_measure_conflict = null`), and no outcome group marked as having insufficient data. The following data-quality observations are nonetheless material to interpretation and are stated plainly:

1. **Effect measure.** The pooled result is a **Risk Ratio (RR)**, pooled on its native scale (`native_pooling_used = true`), as explicitly specified for this analysis. The headline statistic is therefore a confirmed native-scale risk ratio and must be named as a *pooled risk ratio* — not as Hedges' *g* and not as a standardized mean difference.
2. **Mixed scales in the transmitted dataset.** The per-study estimates supplied for display and for bias diagnostics are expressed on the **standardized (Hedges' *g*) scale**, whereas the pooled estimate is on the risk-ratio scale. The two are not directly comparable, and the signs of the standardized per-study values do not map onto the RR sign convention. The trim-and-fill adjusted value (0.1035) likewise belongs to the standardized diagnostic scale, not the risk-ratio scale. Per-study standardized confidence intervals appear narrower than is plausible for a rare binary outcome and should be treated as a descriptive artefact of the transformation rather than as a precision claim.
3. **Non-contiguous study identifiers.** The analysed set comprises R1–R6 and R8–R11; identifier R7 is absent. Consistent with the pre-specified exclusion rules, this indicates that at least one screened randomised report was removed before synthesis — the applicable grounds being failure to report perioperative all-cause mortality, or being a duplicate publication of an already-included trial (removed to avoid double-counting). The exact reason was not transmitted with the dataset.
4. **Trials not reporting mortality were excluded by design.** This is an explicit eligibility rule, not a data loss, but it means the synthesis represents only the mortality-reporting subset of the randomised literature on fibrin haemostats in liver resection, and is exposed to outcome-reporting bias that funnel-based diagnostics cannot detect.
5. **Absent trial-level metadata.** No bibliographic details, population descriptors, comparator identities, blinding status, or risk-of-bias domain judgements accompanied the analytic dataset. Consequently, no formal risk-of-bias table, no GRADE certainty rating, and no moderator analysis could be produced, and included trials are cited by pipeline identifier only.
6. **Denominator convention.** Intention-to-treat denominators were specified as the primary convention, with safety/treated populations used and noted only where ITT figures were unavailable; arm-level data were not re-transmitted with the pooled result, so per-trial confirmation of which denominator applied is not possible from the analytic record.

## 11. Limitations

1. **Severe underpowering for a rare event.** With 1,993 participants and an expected perioperative mortality of roughly 1–3%, the absolute number of deaths available is small. The confidence interval (RR 0.634 to 2.004) accordingly spans effects from a one-third reduction to a doubling of mortality. This is the single most important limitation, and it means the review demonstrates *no detectable effect*, not *no effect*.
2. **Outcome-reporting bias.** Trials that did not report mortality were excluded by protocol. Because mortality was a secondary or incidental outcome in most trials in this field, the mortality-reporting subset may not be representative of the full randomised evidence base. Egger's test and trim-and-fill cannot detect this form of bias.
3. **Heterogeneous and unspecified comparators.** The control condition pools pharmacologically and mechanically distinct alternatives — oxidised cellulose, collagen and gelatin sponges, argon beam coagulation, electrocautery, and plain surgical haemostasis. Because comparator identity was not transmitted, the aggregate contrast cannot be decomposed, and the pooled RR should be read as an average across a clinically non-uniform comparator set.
4. **Heterogeneous mortality windows.** Trials defined the perioperative window variously as in-hospital, 30-day, or up to approximately 90-day mortality. Pooling across these definitions introduces a degree of outcome misalignment that was accepted by protocol but not quantified.
5. **No risk-of-bias or certainty assessment.** No domain-level RoB 2 judgements and no GRADE rating accompanied the dataset. Readers cannot therefore gauge how much of the observed consistency reflects genuine trial quality. On general grounds, the certainty of evidence for this outcome would most plausibly be rated **low** — downgraded principally for imprecision, with possible further downgrading for risk of bias and indirectness of the pooled comparator.
6. **No moderator analysis.** As detailed in §7, neither the available covariates nor the number of trials permitted subgroup or meta-regression analysis, so effect modification by agent form, comparator type, resection extent, cirrhosis status or approach remains entirely unexamined.
7. **Low I² may be misleading.** With rare events and large within-study variance, I² = 13.74% may understate true between-trial differences. The non-negligible τ² (0.117626) and the wide approximate prediction interval (≈ RR 0.40 to 3.19) are the more honest expressions of residual uncertainty.
8. **Mixed-scale analytic inputs.** The coexistence of native risk-ratio pooling with standardized per-study display values (§10) limits the extent to which the forest plot and the pooled summary can be visually reconciled, and readers should rely on the pooled RR for inference.
9. **Single outcome by design.** No process or morbidity outcome was pooled. This review cannot be used to assess whether fibrin-based agents achieve faster haemostasis, reduce transfusion, or affect bile leak, reoperation or infection, and no inference about overall clinical utility should be drawn from it.
10. **Trials unidentified at the bibliographic level.** The absence of primary citations limits independent verification of extraction and precludes assessment of overlap with prior reviews in this field.

## 12. Conclusion

In adults undergoing liver resection, fibrin-based haemostatic agents — fibrin sealants, glues, patches and fibrin-coated collagen — show **no detectable effect on perioperative all-cause mortality** compared with non-fibrin haemostatic agents or techniques. Pooling ten randomised trials and 1,993 participants on the native risk-ratio scale with a REML random-effects model gave a **pooled risk ratio of 1.127 (95% CI 0.634 to 2.004; p = 0.6828)**, a result that **favours neither arm**. Between-study heterogeneity was low and non-significant (I² = 13.74%; τ² = 0.117626; Q = 9.899, df = 9, p = 0.3587), and the risk of publication or small-study bias was low (Egger's intercept 0.3024, p = 0.7145; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0).

The clinically responsible reading of this evidence is one of *equipoise under imprecision*. The data are entirely compatible with no mortality effect, but they are also compatible with a one-third relative reduction or a doubling of perioperative death, and they cannot distinguish between these. Perioperative mortality therefore provides no basis for preferring, or avoiding, fibrin-based topical haemostats in hepatic surgery; such decisions should rest on intraoperative performance, safety profile and cost. Future randomised trials in this field should report all-cause mortality with an explicit 90-day window as a routine, pre-specified safety outcome, and should report comparator and agent characteristics in sufficient detail to permit the comparator-specific syntheses that this aggregate analysis could not deliver.
