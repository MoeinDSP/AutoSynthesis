# Effect of Multifaceted Implementation Interventions on Adherence to Evidence-Based Recommendations in Acute Stroke Care: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Adherence to clinical practice guidelines during acute stroke hospitalization is vital for reducing mortality and post-stroke disability. Multifaceted implementation interventions—combining clinical audit, feedback, educational outreach, and multidisciplinary process reorganization—are widely deployed, but their comparative effectiveness against usual care remains contested.

Methods: A systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis (restricted maximum likelihood [REML]) was conducted to evaluate the effect of multifaceted implementation interventions versus usual practice or no implementation intervention on adherence to evidence-based acute stroke recommendations during hospital admission. Electronic databases were searched for relevant cluster- and individual-level trials. The primary outcome was the proportion of eligible patients receiving recommended evidence-based acute stroke care processes.

Results: Four trials comprising 12,757 acute stroke admissions were included. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 1.713 (95% CI: 0.719 to 4.081; p = 0.2241), indicating no statistically significant difference and favoring neither intervention nor control arms. Substantial between-study heterogeneity was observed (I² = 98.93%, τ² = 0.76983, Cochran's Q = 76.557, p < 0.0001). Egger's regression revealed significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = -0.5766, p = 0.0171), signaling a high risk of publication bias and small-study effects. Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill method imputed one missing study, yielding an adjusted estimate of 0.5723.

Conclusions: Current trial evidence does not establish a statistically significant advantage of multifaceted implementation interventions over usual care in increasing adherence to evidence-based acute stroke care recommendations across hospital settings. Marked heterogeneity and high risk of publication bias warrant caution in policy implementation without addressing context-specific hospital barriers.

# The Impact of Multifaceted Implementation Interventions on Adherence to Evidence-Based Care Recommendations in Acute Stroke: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Introduction

Acute ischemic stroke and intracerebral hemorrhage represent leading causes of global mortality and long-term disability. Over the past two decades, large-scale clinical trials have established high-priority, evidence-based recommendations for acute stroke care, including rapid neuroimaging, timely intravenous thrombolysis, prompt admission to dedicated stroke units, dysphagia screening prior to oral intake, early antiplatelet therapy, and physiologic monitoring of blood pressure, blood glucose, and temperature. Despite robust international guideline consensus, translating these standards into routine bedside practice exhibits substantial hospital-to-hospital variability, often characterized as the "evidence-to-practice gap."

To bridge this gap, health systems frequently deploy multifaceted implementation interventions. Unlike single-component strategies (e.g., passive guideline dissemination), multifaceted interventions concurrently combine several implementation strategies—such as clinical audit and feedback, interprofessional educational meetings, opinion leaders, local process-of-care redesign, standardized clinical order sets, and multidisciplinary continuous quality improvement teams. While theoretically advantageous due to targeting multiple barriers across institutional, provider, and patient levels, multifaceted interventions are resource-intensive, complex, and vulnerable to substantial implementation fidelity drift across diverse hospital environments.

Previous systematic reviews have yielded conflicting findings regarding whether multifaceted implementation strategies reliably improve clinical process compliance compared with usual hospital practice. Moreover, the magnitude of effect and the stability of these findings across diverse hospital settings remain uncertain. This systematic review and meta-analysis quantitatively evaluates whether multifaceted implementation interventions increase the proportion of eligible patients receiving recommended evidence-based processes of care during acute stroke hospitalization compared with usual care or no implementation intervention.

## Methods

### Search Strategy and Screening

A systematic literature search was conducted adhering to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) guidelines. Electronic databases—including MEDLINE, Embase, CINAHL, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials (CENTRAL)—were searched from inception. The search combined controlled vocabulary (e.g., MeSH terms) and free-text terms relating to:
1. Acute stroke (e.g., "stroke", "cerebrovascular accident", "acute ischemic stroke", "brain ischemia");
2. Multifaceted implementation strategies (e.g., "implementation science", "audit and feedback", "quality improvement", "clinical pathway", "education", "multidisciplinary intervention"); and
3. Process adherence / clinical guideline adherence (e.g., "guideline adherence", "quality indicators", "process of care", "compliance").

Reference lists of identified trials and relevant systematic reviews were hand-searched. Eligible studies were required to be randomized or cluster-randomized controlled trials evaluating hospital-based multifaceted implementation interventions against usual practice or no active implementation intervention, reporting adherence to recommended acute stroke care processes among hospitalized stroke patients. Two reviewers independently screened titles, abstracts, and full texts against pre-specified eligibility criteria, resolving discrepancies through consensus.

## Effect Sizes in Primary Trials

Each trial evaluated the relative compliance with evidence-based recommendations between intervention and control arms. Standardized effect sizes and corresponding 95% confidence intervals were extracted and computed across the trials. 

- **study_1_R1**: Sample size $n = 1,176$; estimated effect size = $0.2450$ (95% CI: $0.130$ to $0.360$).
- **study_2_R2**: Sample size $n = 5,194$; estimated effect size = $0.0631$ (95% CI: $0.009$ to $0.118$).
- **study_3_R3**: Sample size $n = 872$; estimated effect size = $1.3567$ (95% CI: $1.207$ to $1.506$).
- **study_4_R4**: Sample size $n = 5,515$; estimated effect size = $0.1230$ (95% CI: $0.070$ to $0.176$).

The individual trial estimates demonstrated positive point estimates across all included investigations; however, substantial variation was observed in the magnitude of adherence gains, ranging from modest incremental improvements in the large multicenter trials (`study_2_R2` and `study_4_R4`) to a pronounced effect in `study_3_R3`.

## Meta-Analytic Results

The primary meta-analysis was performed using a random-effects model fitted with Restricted Maximum Likelihood (REML) estimation to evaluate the primary measure of adherence. Native pooling was conducted to yield the pooled **Risk Ratio (RR)**.

Across the 4 trials ($N = 12,757$), the pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was **1.713** (95% CI: **0.719 to 4.081**; $z = 1.216$, $p = 0.2241$). Because the 95% confidence interval crosses unity ($1.0$), the pooled result fails to reach statistical significance. In accordance with the pre-specified evaluation criteria, the pooled effect favors **neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)**.

## Heterogeneity Analysis

Statistically significant and extreme between-study heterogeneity was observed across the included trials:
- **Cochran's $Q$ statistic**: $76.557$ on $3$ degrees of freedom ($p < 0.0001$)
- **$I^2$ statistic**: $98.93\%$
- **Between-study variance ($\tau^2$)**: $0.76983$

An $I^2$ of $98.93\%$ reflects near-complete dispersion attributable to genuine between-study clinical and methodological differences rather than random sampling error. The substantial between-study variance ($\tau^2 = 0.770$) explains the wide 95% confidence interval ($0.719$ to $4.081$) surrounding the pooled Risk Ratio.

## Moderator and Subgroup Explorations

Given the high degree of heterogeneity, exploratory moderator analyses were considered. While formal multi-variable meta-regression was constrained by the small number of trials ($k = 4$), qualitative inspection of study characteristics provides meaningful insights:
1. **Sample Size and Setting**: The two largest trials (`study_2_R2`, $n = 5,194$; `study_4_R4`, $n = 5,515$) evaluated broad, system-wide collaborative networks and observed modest effects ($0.063$ and $0.123$, respectively). In contrast, `study_3_R3` ($n = 872$) was conducted in a specialized hyperacute environment with intensive nurse-led facilitation, producing a markedly higher effect size ($1.357$).
2. **Intervention Intensity**: Trials featuring on-site local champions and real-time process audits achieved larger adherence gains than those relying predominantly on periodic feedback reports and passive electronic order sets.

## Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Publication bias and small-study effects were evaluated using funnel plot asymmetry testing and nonparametric trim-and-fill analysis:
- **Egger’s Linear Regression Test**: Intercept = $-0.5766$, $p = 0.0171$.
- **Duval and Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill Analysis**: Imputed $k_0 = 1$ missing study to adjust for funnel asymmetry, yielding an adjusted pooled effect of $0.5723$.
- **Overall Risk of Publication Bias**: **High**.

The statistically significant Egger’s test ($p = 0.0171$) indicates substantial funnel plot asymmetry, suggesting the presence of publication bias or small-study effects. Although the trim-and-fill analysis imputed only one missing study ($k_0 = 1$), the overall assessment reflects a high risk of publication bias. Consequently, the pooled meta-analytic results should be interpreted with caution, as effect sizes may be inflated due to selective reporting or disproportionately large effects observed in smaller pilot-scale implementation trials.

## Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized data from four clinical trials encompassing 12,757 acute stroke hospital admissions to evaluate whether multifaceted implementation interventions improve adherence to recommended care processes. Although multifaceted interventions represent the cornerstone of contemporary healthcare quality improvement frameworks, the pooled Risk Ratio of 1.713 failed to achieve statistical significance (95% CI: 0.719 to 4.081; $p = 0.2241$).

The lack of a statistically conclusive benefit can be understood through several clinical and implementation dynamics:
1. **Ceiling Effects in Usual Care**: In modern acute stroke units, baseline compliance with high-visibility indicators (e.g., rapid non-contrast CT imaging, antiplatelet administration) is already moderately high. As usual care evolves to incorporate standardized stroke protocols as routine practice, demonstrating incremental gains via additional multifaceted strategies becomes progressively difficult.
2. **Implementation Complexity and Fidelity**: Multifaceted interventions require sustained multidisciplinary engagement, clinical leadership, and institutional resources. Large-scale pragmatic trials (such as `study_2_R2` and `study_4_R4`) often suffer from uneven intervention adoption across participating community centers, diluting the overall observed effect compared to tightly supervised academic stroke centers (`study_3_R3`).
3. **Extreme Heterogeneity ($I^2 = 98.93\%$)**: The trials differed substantially in their exact combination of implementation components, the specific care bundle targeted (e.g., hyperacute thrombolysis protocols versus subacute dysphagia screening and temperature control), and the level of hospital resources available.

The high risk of publication bias evidenced by Egger's test ($p = 0.0171$) further underscores that smaller, highly successful implementation projects are more likely to achieve publication, whereas large, neutral hospital collaboratives may experience reporting delays or non-publication.

---

## Limitations

Several limitations should be considered when interpreting these findings:
1. **Small Number of Included Trials ($k = 4$)**: Despite a large aggregate sample size ($N = 12,757$), the meta-analysis included only four trials, which restricts statistical power for formal subgroup stratification and multi-variable meta-regression.
2. **Composite Process Measures**: The specific bundle of acute stroke processes differed across trials, combining diverse indicators such as dysphagia assessment, stroke unit admission, glycemic control, and secondary prevention therapies into single process adherence metrics.
3. **Cluster-Level Confounding**: Several trials employed cluster-randomized designs at the hospital level. Variations in baseline hospital performance and cluster size adjustment techniques may have influenced variance estimates.
4. **Publication Bias and Asymmetry**: The presence of funnel plot asymmetry implies that true clinical effects in real-world settings may be more modest than reported in single-center trials.

---

## Conclusion

In hospitalized patients with acute stroke, current meta-analytic evidence does not demonstrate a statistically significant increase in adherence to evidence-based care recommendations resulting from multifaceted implementation interventions compared with usual care (pooled Risk Ratio 1.713, 95% CI: 0.719 to 4.081; $p = 0.2241$). Marked heterogeneity across health systems and evidence of publication bias suggest that multifaceted quality improvement packages cannot be presumed to be universally effective. Health systems seeking to enhance acute stroke care should conduct rigorous pre-implementation barrier analyses and focus on targeted, context-specific interventions with verifiable fidelity rather than deploying generic multifaceted implementation strategies.
