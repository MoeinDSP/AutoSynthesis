# Multifaceted Implementation Interventions to Improve Adherence to Evidence-Based Processes of Acute Stroke Care: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Gaps in delivery of evidence-based care during acute-stroke admission may be addressed through multifaceted implementation strategies. This review assessed whether interventions containing at least two implementation components improve adherence to recommended in-hospital processes of care relative to usual practice or no implementation intervention.

**Methods:** Eligible evaluations enrolled people with acute stroke during hospital admission and compared a multifaceted implementation intervention with usual practice or no implementation intervention. The intended outcome was the trial-primary adherence or quality-of-care process measure at the end of the intervention period, expressed as the post-intervention proportion receiving recommended care. Five effect estimates from four studies were supplied. Risk ratios (RRs) were pooled with a random-effects model using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Statistical heterogeneity and small-study effects were assessed using Q, I², τ², Egger’s regression, and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** The five effect estimates represented an aggregate analytical sample size of 13,933 across estimates; this cannot be assumed to be the number of unique participants because two estimates had the same reported sample size and the mapping of estimates to the four studies was not supplied. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 1.6362044671113425 (95% CI 0.838 to 3.194; p=0.1491830910487694). The supplied favourability classification was **neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)**; accordingly, the result is not described as favouring either intervention arm. Heterogeneity was considerable (Q=79.549, p=0.0; I²=98.72%; τ²=0.569895). Egger’s intercept was −0.5577 (p=0.0251), and trim-and-fill estimated one potentially missing study and an adjusted value of 0.4941. Publication-bias risk was classified as high.

**Conclusions:** The pooled estimate was imprecise, statistically non-significant, and extremely heterogeneous. Because the supplied event definition did not permit the direction of benefit to be established, the estimate cannot be interpreted as evidence that implementation improved adherence. Possible small-study effects, an effect-measure conflict, multiple estimates from four studies, and limited study-level information further reduce confidence in the result.

## Introduction

Evidence-based processes delivered during acute-stroke admission—including rapid assessment, timely investigations and treatments, coordinated stroke-unit practices, and prevention of in-hospital complications—can improve the reliability and quality of care. Translation of recommendations into routine clinical practice is nevertheless variable. Multifaceted implementation interventions combine at least two strategies, such as professional education, audit and feedback, local champions, protocols, clinical pathways, reminders, or changes in care delivery. Such combinations may address several barriers concurrently, but their effects may differ substantially according to intervention content, clinical setting, targeted process, and baseline performance.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among people with acute stroke during hospital admission, what is the effect of multifaceted implementation interventions, compared with no implementation intervention or usual practice, on adherence to evidence-based recommendations for processes of care?

The prespecified direction of interest was increased adherence to recommended care. The intended effect measure was the risk ratio (RR), calculated from the post-intervention proportions of eligible patients receiving the recommended process in intervention and control groups. However, the supplied dataset contained an unresolved conflict between the requested RR and odds ratios reportedly used in the source papers. This issue is addressed explicitly below.

## Search and Screening

### Eligibility criteria

Studies were eligible if they:

1. included people with acute stroke during hospital admission;
2. evaluated a multifaceted implementation intervention containing at least two implementation components, with or without changes to care delivery;
3. used no implementation intervention, usual care, or practice as normal as the comparator; and
4. reported an in-hospital adherence or quality-of-care process measure at the end of the intervention period.

The target outcome was the trial-designated primary adherence measure. If a study did not explicitly designate a primary process outcome, the intended approach was to select the measure most central to the implementation intervention using a prespecified hierarchy. Baseline measures, patient clinical outcomes, costs, staff knowledge or satisfaction, and adherence assessed after discharge were outside the outcome definition.

### Information sources and search strategy

The supplied results did not identify databases, registries, other information sources, search dates, search terms, language restrictions, protocol registration, or methods used to identify unpublished studies. Consequently, a reproducible search strategy cannot be reported or independently assessed.

### Study selection and data collection

No counts were supplied for records identified, deduplicated, screened, reviewed in full text, or excluded, and no reasons for full-text exclusion were available. ## Included Studies

Four studies contributed five effect estimates. The supplied data did not provide study authors, publication years, countries, clinical settings, study designs, intervention components, comparator details, patient characteristics, cluster structures, targeted processes of care, intervention duration, or risk-of-bias judgments. It was therefore not possible to describe clinical or methodological comparability in detail.

The five estimates had reported analytical sample sizes of 1,176, 1,176, 5,194, 872, and 5,515, respectively. Their sum was 13,933, matching the meta-analysis field `n_total`. This is an aggregate across effect estimates and should not be interpreted as 13,933 unique participants: five estimates arose from four studies, and the first two estimates had the same reported sample size. The available information did not identify which study contributed more than one estimate or whether the two estimates represented overlapping participants, different outcomes, or separate comparisons.

The report also did not identify the actual process-of-care outcome selected from each trial, whether it was explicitly trial-designated as primary, or—where no primary measure existed—how the hierarchy was applied. Thus, compliance of outcome selection with the intended rule could not be verified.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A REML random-effects model pooled five effect estimates. Native pooling was marked as used, and the requested statistic was the **Risk Ratio (RR)**. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was **1.6362044671113425** (95% CI **0.838 to 3.194**; p=**0.1491830910487694**; k=5 effect estimates; aggregate n across estimates=13,933).

The confidence interval included the null value of 1, and the pooled result was not statistically significant at a two-sided 0.05 threshold. Importantly, the supplied `favors` field classified the result as **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)”**. In accordance with that classification, the pooled estimate is not interpreted as favouring the multifaceted intervention or control. Although the research protocol defined an RR greater than 1 as beneficial when the counted event was adherence, the supplied information did not confirm that adherence—rather than non-adherence—was consistently counted in the analysis.

The cumulative meta-analysis plot is available at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`. It should be interpreted cautiously because the study ordering variable was not reported and five estimates arose from only four studies.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was extreme:

- Cochran’s Q=**79.549**;
- Q-test p=**0.0** as supplied;
- I²=**98.72%**; and
- τ²=**0.569895** under REML.

The Q-test indicates variation beyond that expected from sampling error, while I² suggests that nearly all observed dispersion was attributable to between-estimate heterogeneity rather than within-estimate uncertainty. The magnitude and interpretation of τ² depend on the RR computation scale used by the analysis. Given the unclear correspondence between the supplied study-level standardized estimates and the pooled RR, τ² should not be interpreted in isolation.

Potential sources of heterogeneity include differences in the process targeted, implementation components and intensity, intervention duration, hospital organization, baseline adherence, study design, and analytical handling of clustering. None could be examined from the supplied study characteristics. Dependence arising from multiple estimates contributed by one of the four studies is another possible source of distorted precision or heterogeneity if it was not modelled.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−0.5577** with p=**0.0251**, indicating statistically detectable funnel-plot asymmetry. The trim-and-fill analysis estimated **one** potentially missing study and returned an adjusted value of **0.4941**. The supplied assessment classified publication-bias risk as **high**.

These findings are consistent with possible small-study effects or publication bias, but they are not specific to publication bias. Extreme heterogeneity, outcome differences, methodological differences, and dependence among effect estimates can also cause asymmetry. Moreover, tests for funnel-plot asymmetry are generally unreliable with so few studies; the current evidence base contained only four studies and five estimates. The trim-and-fill adjusted value was supplied under the field `trim_fill_g`, and its scale was not explicitly confirmed as RR. It is therefore reported as an adjusted value rather than described as an adjusted Risk Ratio.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across five effect estimates from four studies, the REML random-effects model yielded a pooled Risk Ratio (RR) of 1.6362044671113425 (95% CI 0.838 to 3.194; p=0.1491830910487694). The estimate was imprecise and did not exclude the null. Its direction cannot be interpreted as beneficial or harmful because the supplied favourability classification was neither arm and the counted event was not confirmed. Accordingly, these results do not establish that multifaceted implementation interventions improve adherence to recommended acute-stroke processes of care.

The most prominent feature of the evidence was heterogeneity (I²=98.72%; τ²=0.569895). A single average effect may therefore inadequately represent the intervention effects in individual contexts. Multifaceted implementation interventions are intrinsically heterogeneous, and the targeted processes of care may differ in complexity, required resources, baseline uptake, and responsiveness to implementation support. Without descriptions of intervention components or outcomes, clinically meaningful explanations cannot be distinguished from analytical inconsistency.

### Interpretation and applicability

A multifaceted approach is plausible when barriers operate at clinician, team, workflow, and organizational levels. Nevertheless, adding components does not ensure effectiveness: poorly matched strategies may increase complexity without addressing the principal determinants of practice. Effects may also depend on whether the intervention includes active mechanisms such as repeated feedback, facilitation, accountable local leadership, and workflow redesign rather than passive dissemination alone.

Applicability to particular hospitals, health systems, stroke types, or evidence-based processes cannot be determined because these characteristics were not supplied. The analysis also does not address patient clinical outcomes, costs, staff knowledge or satisfaction, or adherence after discharge, all of which were outside the review outcome.

### Certainty of the evidence

A formal GRADE assessment was not provided and cannot be completed reliably without study designs and domain-level risk-of-bias information. Confidence should nonetheless be considered limited because of very serious inconsistency, imprecision around the pooled estimate, possible publication bias or small-study effects, unclear dependence among estimates, and unresolved outcome and effect-measure classification. These concerns preclude a firm causal conclusion.

### Implications for research

Future trials and reviews should prespecify a single primary process outcome, report arm-level event counts and eligible denominators, distinguish adherence from non-adherence events, account for clustered allocation and repeated measurements, and provide intervention descriptions using an implementation-reporting framework. Reviews should avoid double-counting participant samples, use multilevel or robust-variance methods when multiple dependent estimates are retained, and plan subgroup analyses only where sufficient studies exist. Registration of protocols and publication of null implementation evaluations would improve transparency.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

The supplied data generated several material validation concerns:

1. The pipeline reported **four studies but five effect estimates**; the study contributing multiple estimates was not identified, and statistical dependence could not be evaluated.
2. The summed analytical sample size was **13,933 across estimates**, not necessarily 13,933 unique participants.
3. Study-level effects were labelled **Hedges’ g**, whereas the pooled analysis was labelled a native-scale **Risk Ratio (RR)** analysis. The study-level and pooled scales could not be reconciled.
4. An explicit effect-measure conflict stated that the user requested RR while source papers reportedly presented odds ratios; the RR request was retained as authoritative. Without arm-level counts, conversion or verification was impossible.
5. The event definition was not supplied. Consistent with the provided `favors` field, the pooled result cannot be assigned to either arm.
6. The selected primary process measures were not named, so adherence to the prespecified outcome hierarchy could not be checked.
7. The trim-and-fill value (**0.4941**) was stored under a `g` field, and its native scale was not confirmed.
8. No searchable study citations, search history, exclusion log, risk-of-bias judgments, or intervention descriptions were available.

## Limitations

This report is limited primarily by incomplete aggregate input. It lacks a reproducible search strategy, PRISMA screening counts, trial citations, study characteristics, outcome definitions, arm-level event data, and risk-of-bias assessments. The absence of these elements prevents independent verification of eligibility, outcome selection, effect calculation, and study quality.

The meta-analysis used five estimates from four studies without information about within-study dependence. Treating correlated estimates as independent can overweight a study and produce confidence intervals that are too narrow. Conversely, selecting only one estimate without knowing the trial-primary outcome could introduce selective outcome bias.

Extreme heterogeneity limits the interpretability of the average effect. Egger’s regression and trim-and-fill are unstable with only four studies and may be misleading in the presence of heterogeneity. The conflict between study-level Hedges’ g values, reportedly published odds ratios, and the pooled RR is a major limitation. Finally, no sensitivity analyses were supplied using alternative estimators, Hartung–Knapp inference, leave-one-out exclusion, cluster adjustments, one estimate per study, or separate pooling by process outcome.

## Conclusion

The available analysis does not provide conclusive evidence that multifaceted implementation interventions improve adherence to evidence-based processes during acute-stroke admission. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 1.6362044671113425 (95% CI 0.838 to 3.194; p=0.1491830910487694), with extreme heterogeneity (I²=98.72%) and a supplied classification favouring neither arm. Funnel-plot asymmetry and trim-and-fill findings raised concern about small-study effects or publication bias, although these methods are unreliable with so few studies. Firm conclusions require verified event definitions, arm-level adherence data, correct handling of multiple estimates per study, detailed intervention and outcome descriptions, and a transparent systematic-search record.
