# Caregiver-Directed Oral Hygiene, Diet and Feeding-Practice Advice and the Prevention of Dental Caries in the Primary Dentition: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Early childhood caries remains the most prevalent chronic disease of childhood. Because the behaviours that drive it — tooth-brushing, sugar exposure, bottle and feeding practices — are controlled by parents, antenatal and postnatal counselling of mothers and other primary caregivers has been widely promoted as a primary-prevention strategy. Whether such advice actually lowers the proportion of children who go on to develop caries in their primary teeth is unresolved.

**Objective.** To determine whether oral hygiene, diet and feeding-practice advice delivered to pregnant women, new mothers or other primary caregivers (counselling, motivational interviewing, family-centred oral health promotion, brushing and diet counselling, photograph-based feedback), compared with standard care, usual dental/antenatal care or no advice, reduces the proportion of children with dental caries in any primary tooth at the longest reported follow-up.

**Methods.** A systematic search of controlled trials was screened against pre-specified criteria. The outcome was dichotomous: number of children with caries (trial's own caries definition, preferring any carious lesion in any primary tooth) out of the number of children assessed in each arm. Multi-arm trials had advice arms combined and the control arm used once, with fluoride/active-treatment arms excluded in favour of advice-only arms; cluster-randomised trials contributed their reported adjusted effect estimate. Mean dmft/dmfs severity, front-four-teeth-only caries, mutans streptococci counts, knowledge/behaviour outcomes and adverse events were excluded from this outcome. Five studies were retained after screening, of which three contributed extractable dichotomous caries data (k = 3; N = 732 children). Effects were pooled on the native risk-ratio scale using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of between-study variance. Heterogeneity was quantified with Q, I² and τ²; small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.428 (95% CI 0.135 to 1.356; p = 0.1492; k = 3; N = 732). Per the pre-specified direction field, this result favours **neither arm** — it is statistically indistinguishable from no effect, with a confidence interval spanning both a large reduction and a modest increase in the proportion of children developing caries. Heterogeneity was substantial and statistically significant: Q = 17.005, p = 0.0002, I² = 86.09%, τ² = 0.7158. All three individual study-level standardized estimates pointed in the same direction (g = −0.0829, 95% CI −0.336 to 0.171; g = −0.6366, 95% CI −1.179 to −0.095; g = −0.9721, 95% CI −1.171 to −0.773), but their magnitudes differed by more than an order of magnitude on the standardized scale, which is the source of the wide pooled interval. Egger's regression showed no funnel asymmetry (intercept = −0.5851, p = 0.9784) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0); risk of publication bias was rated low, with the caveat of very low power at k = 3.

**Conclusions.** Across three poolable trials, caregiver-directed oral hygiene, diet and feeding-practice advice did not produce a statistically reliable reduction in the proportion of children with caries in their primary teeth. The point estimate is compatible with a clinically important benefit, but the evidence base is too small and too heterogeneous (I² = 86.09%) to support a confident conclusion in either direction. The pooled estimate should be read as inconclusive rather than as evidence of no effect, and further adequately powered trials with harmonised caries case definitions are needed.

## 1. Introduction

Dental caries in the primary dentition — commonly described as early childhood caries (ECC) when it affects children under six — is the single most prevalent chronic disease of early childhood worldwide. Its consequences extend well beyond the affected teeth: pain, infection, disturbed sleep and feeding, impaired growth, general anaesthetic exposure for extractions, and a strongly elevated risk of caries in the permanent dentition. Because the disease is driven almost entirely by modifiable behaviours that are under adult rather than child control during the first years of life — the frequency and timing of tooth-brushing, the use of fluoride toothpaste, the frequency and form of free-sugar exposure, bottle use, nocturnal feeding, and the sharing of utensils — caregivers have long been the logical target of primary preventive effort.

A substantial body of health-promotion practice has therefore grown up around **caregiver-directed advice**: antenatal oral health counselling for pregnant women, postnatal brushing and diet instruction for new mothers, motivational interviewing delivered to parents in dental or paediatric settings, family-centred oral health promotion programmes, and more recent innovations such as photograph-based feedback in which caregivers are shown images of their own child's teeth or plaque to prompt behaviour change. These interventions are attractive because they are low-risk, relatively inexpensive, and can be grafted onto existing antenatal, well-child or dental-visit contacts.

The behavioural intermediate outcomes of such programmes — caregiver knowledge, self-reported brushing frequency, reported sugar exposure — frequently improve. What is far less clear is whether these improvements translate into the outcome that matters clinically: **fewer children actually developing caries in their primary teeth.** Individual trials have reported results ranging from essentially no difference to substantial relative reductions, and the field has been hampered by inconsistent caries case definitions (any decay vs. cavitated lesions vs. decay into dentine vs. inclusion of white-spot lesions), by outcomes restricted to the maxillary incisors only, and by reliance on surrogate endpoints such as mutans streptococci counts.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addresses the following question:

> Does oral hygiene, diet and feeding-practice advice delivered to pregnant women, new mothers or other primary caregivers, compared with standard care, usual dental/antenatal care or no advice, reduce the proportion of children who develop dental caries in their primary teeth?

The pre-specified effect direction is that a **risk ratio below 1.0 favours the caregiver advice intervention**, i.e. that proportionally fewer children in the advice arm have caries in their primary teeth at the longest reported follow-up.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Search and screening

The review followed PRISMA 2020 reporting conventions. Records identified by the search were screened in two stages — title/abstract, then full text — against the following pre-specified eligibility criteria.

**Population.** Pregnant women, new mothers, or other primary caregivers (fathers, grandparents, foster carers) of infants and young children, with the child's primary dentition as the unit of outcome assessment.

**Intervention.** Oral hygiene, diet and/or feeding-practice advice directed at the caregiver, delivered in any format: one-to-one counselling, motivational interviewing, family-centred oral health promotion, brushing and diet counselling, written or audiovisual education, or photograph-based feedback on the child's oral condition.

**Comparator.** Standard care, usual dental or antenatal care, or no advice.

**Outcome.** Presence of dental caries in **any** primary tooth of the child at the longest reported follow-up, expressed as the number of children with caries (events) out of the number of children assessed (total) in each arm.

**Design.** Controlled trials, including individually randomised and cluster-randomised designs.

### 2.2 Outcome definition and extraction rules

A deliberately permissive but hierarchically specified approach to the caries endpoint was adopted, with each trial's own caries definition used and recorded (e.g. any decay in primary teeth, dmft/dmfs > 0, cavitated lesions, decay into dentine, or white-spot lesions). Where a trial reported more than one definition, the definition counting **any carious lesion in any primary tooth** was preferred over a definition restricted to a subset of teeth (for example, maxillary incisors only).

The following extraction rules were applied:

- Percentages were converted to counts using the number of children **assessed** in that arm. Where only the number **randomised** was available for the outcome, that figure was used and the substitution noted.
- **Multi-arm trials:** advice arms were combined into a single intervention group and the control group used once, so that no control participant was counted twice. Arms adding fluoride varnish or another active clinical treatment on top of advice were excluded in favour of the advice-only arm, preserving the advice-vs-no-advice contrast.
- **Cluster-randomised trials** reporting an adjusted risk ratio or odds ratio contributed that adjusted estimate as reported, so that clustering was accounted for in the trial's own analysis rather than by post hoc inflation.

The following were explicitly **excluded** from this outcome: mean dmft/dmfs severity scores (a continuous severity rather than prevalence endpoint); caries limited to the front four teeth; bacterial (mutans streptococci) colonisation counts; caregiver knowledge, attitude and behaviour outcomes; and adverse events.

### 2.3 Statistical analysis

The effect measure was the **Risk Ratio (RR)**, specified a priori by the review question (effect measure source: explicit user request; no conflict with the data was detected). Pooling was performed on the native RR scale — that is, native-scale pooling was used (`native_pooling_used = true`), so the pooled value reported below is a genuine risk ratio and **not** a standardized mean difference or Hedges' *g* fallback.

A random-effects model was fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation of the between-study variance τ². Heterogeneity was quantified with Cochran's Q, the I² statistic and τ² itself. Publication bias and small-study effects were assessed using Egger's regression test of funnel-plot asymmetry and the Duval and Tweedie trim-and-fill procedure. Given the small number of poolable studies (k = 3), no formal meta-regression was attempted; moderator influence was examined descriptively.

## 3. Search and Screening Results

The screening process yielded **five studies** meeting the review's substantive eligibility criteria (population, intervention, comparator and design). Of these, **three studies** provided extractable dichotomous data on the proportion of children with caries in any primary tooth at the longest reported follow-up in a form compatible with risk-ratio pooling, and these three constitute the quantitative synthesis (k = 3).

The two remaining eligible studies were retained in the qualitative synthesis but could not contribute to the pooled risk ratio. In a review of this type, the usual reasons are that the trial reported only mean dmft/dmfs severity scores rather than the proportion of children affected, that the caries outcome was restricted to a subset of teeth, that the only available endpoint was a surrogate (bacterial counts) or a behavioural measure, or that no arm-level numerator and denominator could be reconstructed. The practical consequence is identical regardless of the reason: **40% of the eligible trial base (2 of 5 studies) is not represented in the pooled estimate**, and the pooled result therefore describes a subset of the available evidence rather than its totality. This is stated plainly here and revisited in the Data Quality and Limitations sections.

A PRISMA-style accounting of the synthesis is therefore: 5 studies eligible → 3 studies with poolable dichotomous caries data → 3 effect sizes entering the random-effects model → 732 children analysed.

## 4. Included Studies

Three trials contributed to the quantitative synthesis, together contributing **N = 732 children** assessed for caries in the primary dentition. Their characteristics, as carried through the analysis pipeline, are summarised below.

| Study ID | Effect-size label | Children analysed (n) | Standardized study-level estimate (g) | 95% CI | Relative weight (inverse-variance, random-effects) |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Study 1 | study_1_R1 | 241 | −0.0829 | −0.336 to 0.171 | Largest-precision contributor after Study 5 |
| Study 3 | study_3_R3 | 55 | −0.6366 | −1.179 to −0.095 | Smallest study; widest interval |
| Study 5 | study_5_R5 | 436 | −0.9721 | −1.171 to −0.773 | Largest study; narrowest interval |
| **Total** | — | **732** | — | — | — |

Several features of this set deserve emphasis before any pooled figure is interpreted.

First, the trials differ markedly in **size**, from 55 to 436 children — an eight-fold range. Under a fixed-effect model Study 5 would dominate; under the random-effects model actually fitted, the large τ² substantially flattens the weighting, so Study 3 (n = 55) exerts more influence on the pooled estimate than its sample size alone would suggest.

Second, the trials necessarily differ in **caries case definition**, since each trial's own definition was used as specified in the protocol. Definitions in this literature span cavitated lesions only, decay into dentine, dmft/dmfs > 0 including non-cavitated change, and definitions that count white-spot lesions as events. Because the event rate in a caries trial is extremely sensitive to whether non-cavitated lesions are counted, this definitional variation is a first-order source of between-study variability in the baseline risk and hence in the achievable relative risk.

Third, the **interventions** grouped under "caregiver-directed advice" are not interchangeable. Antenatal counselling delivered once to a pregnant woman, a structured motivational-interviewing programme with multiple contacts across the first years of life, a family-centred oral health promotion programme embedded in community services, and photograph-based feedback on the child's own teeth differ in intensity, timing relative to tooth eruption, theoretical basis and dose. Pooling them answers a deliberately broad pragmatic question ("does caregiver advice, broadly construed, prevent caries?") at the cost of mixing mechanisms.

Fourth, **follow-up duration** varies with the longest-reported-follow-up rule, and caries is a cumulative, time-dependent outcome: the same intervention will show a different relative risk at 2 years than at 5 years, because control-arm prevalence rises toward a ceiling over time and compresses the attainable relative difference.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Three effect sizes entered the model, one per study (no study contributed more than one estimate, so there is no within-study dependency to model).

At the study level, the standardized estimates reported in the pipeline were:

- **Study 1 (study_1_R1):** g = −0.0829, 95% CI −0.336 to 0.171 (n = 241). The confidence interval comfortably includes zero; this trial is individually null, consistent with little or no difference between advice and standard care.
- **Study 3 (study_3_R3):** g = −0.6366, 95% CI −1.179 to −0.095 (n = 55). A moderate-to-large effect in the direction of fewer caries in the advice arm; individually statistically significant, but from the smallest sample and hence the most fragile.
- **Study 5 (study_5_R5):** g = −0.9721, 95% CI −1.171 to −0.773 (n = 436). A large effect favouring advice, with the narrowest interval of the three.

All three study-level point estimates carry the same sign, which is why the pooled point estimate sits well below the null on the ratio scale. However, the magnitudes are strikingly discordant: the interval for Study 1 (−0.336 to 0.171) and the interval for Study 5 (−1.171 to −0.773) do not overlap at all. Two trials in the same synthesis producing non-overlapping confidence intervals is, by itself, direct visual evidence of heterogeneity that cannot be attributed to sampling error, and it anticipates the formal heterogeneity statistics reported in Section 7.

The **pooling itself was conducted on the native risk-ratio scale** (`computation_scale = RR`, `native_pooling_used = true`). The standardized *g* values above are reported because they are what the pipeline carries at study level and because they permit direct visual comparison of magnitude and precision across trials; they should not be confused with the pooled statistic, which is a risk ratio.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

The primary random-effects (REML) synthesis of caregiver-directed advice versus standard care or no advice, for the outcome *proportion of children with caries in any primary tooth at longest follow-up*, gave:

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Risk Ratio (RR)** — native-scale pooling confirmed |
| Pooled RR | **0.428** |
| 95% confidence interval | **0.135 to 1.356** |
| p-value | **0.1492** |
| Studies (k) | **3** |
| Children (N) | **732** |
| Between-study variance τ² | **0.7158** |
| I² | **86.09%** |
| Cochran's Q | **17.005** (p = **0.0002**) |
| Estimation method | REML |
| Direction (pre-specified `favors` field) | **Neither arm** — essentially a tie, or a ratio measure whose favourability depends on which event was counted |

### 6.1 How this result should be read

The pooled risk ratio of **0.428** would, taken at face value, correspond to a 57% relative reduction in the proportion of children developing caries — a large and clinically important effect. **It is not statistically significant.** The 95% confidence interval runs from 0.135 to 1.356, and because it crosses the null value of 1.0, the data are simultaneously compatible with an 86.5% relative reduction in caries prevalence and with a 35.6% relative *increase*. The p-value of 0.1492 does not meet conventional thresholds.

Consistent with this, the pre-specified direction field records that the result favours **neither arm**: it is essentially a tie, and for a ratio measure the apparent favourability also depends on which event was counted in the source trials. We follow that field rather than inferring direction from the sign of the point estimate, because the point estimate's distance from 1.0 is not supported by the interval around it.

The correct summary is therefore **inconclusive, not null**. An inconclusive result and a demonstration of no effect are different things: this analysis has not shown that caregiver advice fails to prevent caries; it has shown that three heterogeneous trials in 732 children cannot resolve whether it does. The width of the interval — spanning more than a ten-fold range on the ratio scale — is the honest headline of this review.

### 6.2 Why the interval is so wide

Two factors combine. First, **k = 3** is at the absolute lower bound for random-effects meta-analysis; with three studies the between-study variance is estimated from two degrees of freedom and is itself extremely imprecise. Second, **τ² = 0.7158** is very large on the log-RR scale (τ ≈ 0.846), meaning the model estimates that the *true* underlying log risk ratios in the source populations differ substantially from one another. When τ² is large and k is small, the standard error of the pooled estimate inflates sharply, and the confidence interval widens accordingly even though every individual study points the same way. This is not a defect of the analysis; it is the model correctly reporting that three discordant trials do not constitute a precise body of evidence.

A prediction interval — the range within which the true effect of a *future* comparable trial would be expected to fall — was not estimable with useful precision at k = 3, but given τ² = 0.7158 it would be very much wider still than the confidence interval reported above, and would unambiguously include both substantial benefit and substantial harm.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity in this synthesis is **substantial, statistically significant, and the dominant feature of the result.**

- **Cochran's Q = 17.005** on 2 degrees of freedom, **p = 0.0002**. The null hypothesis that all three trials share a common underlying effect is decisively rejected. Note that Q is notoriously underpowered when k is small, so rejecting the null at p = 0.0002 with only three studies indicates genuinely marked dispersion rather than a borderline signal.
- **I² = 86.09%**, which falls in the "considerable heterogeneity" band under conventional Cochrane thresholds (75–100%). Approximately 86% of the observed variability in effect estimates is attributable to real between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error.
- **τ² = 0.7158** (τ ≈ 0.846 on the log scale). To make this concrete: a between-study standard deviation of roughly 0.85 in log-RR means that trials whose true effects sit one standard deviation either side of the pooled estimate would have true risk ratios differing by a factor of about e^1.69 ≈ 5.4. That is an enormous spread for a single intervention category.

The qualitative evidence agrees with the statistics. As noted in Section 5, the confidence intervals for Study 1 and Study 5 do not overlap, and the three point estimates span from essentially no effect (g = −0.0829) to a large effect (g = −0.9721).

### 7.1 Plausible sources of the heterogeneity

Because formal statistical exploration is not feasible at k = 3, the candidate explanations must be set out on substantive grounds:

1. **Caries case definition.** This is likely the single largest contributor. Trials counting white-spot lesions or any dmft/dmfs > 0 will record far higher event rates in both arms than trials requiring cavitation or dentinal involvement. Since relative risks are constrained differently at high and low baseline prevalence, definitional variation alone can generate order-of-magnitude differences in RR for an identical underlying biological effect.
2. **Intervention intensity and dose.** A single antenatal counselling session and a multi-contact motivational-interviewing programme sustained across the first years of life are not the same exposure. Interventions with repeated reinforcement, a theoretical behaviour-change basis, and timing aligned with tooth eruption and the introduction of solids would be expected to outperform one-off information delivery.
3. **Follow-up duration.** Caries prevalence is cumulative. The longest-reported-follow-up rule means trials are compared at different points on a rising prevalence curve, and the achievable relative reduction shrinks as control-arm prevalence approaches its ceiling.
4. **Baseline caries risk and population context.** Trials conducted in high-risk, low-income or Indigenous communities with high sugar availability and limited fluoride exposure start from a very different absolute risk than trials in fluoridated, low-prevalence settings. Both the scope for benefit and the difficulty of achieving it differ.
5. **Unit of randomisation and analysis.** The protocol permitted cluster-randomised trials to contribute their own adjusted RR or OR. Mixing cluster-adjusted estimates with individually randomised estimates introduces variation in how precisely uncertainty is characterised, and an adjusted OR entering a risk-ratio synthesis will diverge from the corresponding RR whenever the event is common — as caries typically is.
6. **Control-arm contamination.** In settings where oral health advice is already part of routine antenatal or well-child care, the "standard care" arm may receive a meaningful dose of the active ingredient, attenuating the observed contrast — and the degree of attenuation will vary by health system.

Given I² = 86.09%, the random-effects pooled estimate should be understood as the mean of a genuinely dispersed distribution of effects, not as an estimate of a single common effect. In such circumstances many methodologists would argue that the dispersion itself, rather than its mean, is the primary finding.

## 8. Moderators

**No formal moderator analysis or meta-regression was performed, and none is defensible at k = 3.** With three studies and two residual degrees of freedom, a meta-regression with even a single covariate would be fitted essentially without error degrees of freedom, would have negligible power, and would be at acute risk of overfitting and of aggregation (ecological) bias. Reporting such a model would create a false impression of explanatory insight.

What can be said descriptively is the following. Inspection of the bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) and of the study-level table in Section 4 shows that in this particular set the **largest trial produced the largest effect** (Study 5: n = 436, g = −0.9721) while the **mid-sized trial produced the smallest effect** (Study 1: n = 241, g = −0.0829). This ordering is the reverse of the classic small-study-effect pattern, in which small trials show inflated benefits; it is consistent with the formal publication-bias tests reported in Section 9, which found no asymmetry.

Because effect magnitude here is clearly not tracking sample size, the heterogeneity must be driven by the substantive differences enumerated in Section 7.1 — caries definition, intervention dose, follow-up length, baseline risk, and unit of randomisation. Those remain hypotheses. Testing them would require either individual participant data or a considerably larger pool of trials, and we state plainly that this review cannot adjudicate between them.

## 9. Publication Bias

Small-study effects were assessed with two complementary methods.

**Egger's regression test.** The regression intercept was **−0.5851** with **p = 0.9784**. This is as far from significance as a test can practically be, and indicates no detectable relationship between effect size and study precision — the relationship that would be expected if small, non-significant trials were systematically absent from the literature.

**Trim-and-fill (Duval and Tweedie).** The procedure imputed **k₀ = 0** missing studies, with an adjusted estimate of **−0.5627** on the analysis scale. Imputing zero studies means the funnel plot is already symmetric under the method's assumptions and the pooled estimate requires no correction for suppressed findings.

**Risk rating: low.**

**Interpretation.** Egger's regression test shows no evidence of funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.9784, far from conventional significance), indicating that effect sizes are not systematically related to study precision — the pattern you would expect if small, non-significant studies were missing. This is corroborated by the trim-and-fill procedure, which imputed zero missing studies (k₀ = 0), meaning the funnel plot is already symmetric and the pooled estimate requires no adjustment for suppressed findings.

**Caveat.** Taken together these results support a low risk of publication bias, though both methods have limited statistical power when the number of included studies is small (typically <10) or between-study heterogeneity is high, so a null result should be read as an absence of detectable asymmetry rather than definitive proof that no bias exists. Both conditions apply here in the most extreme form available: **k = 3** is far below the minimum of ten studies conventionally recommended before Egger's test is even attempted, and **I² = 86.09%** is high enough that heterogeneity alone can distort funnel-plot geometry. These tests should therefore be regarded as uninformative rather than as reassurance. A further consideration specific to this literature is that caregiver oral-health-promotion trials are frequently funded by public health agencies and published irrespective of result, and some are registered cluster trials with mandatory reporting — factors that may reduce, though they do not eliminate, selective-publication pressure.

## 10. Discussion

### 10.1 Principal findings

Three trials in 732 children yielded a pooled risk ratio of 0.428 (95% CI 0.135 to 1.356; p = 0.1492) for the presence of caries in any primary tooth following caregiver-directed oral hygiene, diet and feeding-practice advice. The pre-specified direction field records this as favouring **neither arm**. Between-study heterogeneity was considerable (I² = 86.09%; τ² = 0.7158; Q = 17.005, p = 0.0002), and publication bias was not detectable, though the tests had essentially no power.

The honest reading is that **the question posed by this review remains open.** The point estimate is attractive and all three trials point the same way, but the confidence interval is so wide that it cannot exclude a null or even an adverse effect, and the heterogeneity is so marked that the pooled mean may not describe any real-world implementation of the intervention particularly well.

### 10.2 Reconciling an encouraging point estimate with an inconclusive interval

It is tempting to emphasise the RR of 0.428 and the consistent direction of all three studies, and to treat the non-significant p-value as an artefact of small k. There is something to this argument: with only three trials, even a true large effect can fail to reach significance once τ² is properly accounted for, and the consistency of sign across independent trials is not nothing.

But three countervailing considerations caution against that optimistic reading.

First, **consistency of sign is weak evidence when magnitudes are irreconcilable.** Study 1's interval (g: −0.336 to 0.171) and Study 5's (g: −1.171 to −0.773) do not overlap. These trials are not noisy measurements of one effect; they are measurements of different things. Averaging them produces a number that neither trial's population would recognise.

Second, **the behavioural-intervention literature has a well-documented pattern** in which early, small, enthusiastically delivered trials by programme developers show large effects that shrink markedly in larger, independently conducted, pragmatically implemented replications. The present set does not show the classic small-study pattern (the largest trial has the largest effect), which argues against this explanation here — but with three studies the pattern cannot be reliably assessed either way.

Third, **the caries case-definition problem is not cosmetic.** A trial that counts white-spot lesions as caries events measures early enamel demineralisation, a reversible condition highly responsive to short-term brushing change; a trial requiring dentinal cavitation measures established, irreversible disease. An intervention can plausibly produce a large relative effect on the former and little on the latter. Until trials converge on a common, cavitation-anchored definition, pooled relative risks in this field will remain difficult to interpret and difficult to translate into policy.

### 10.3 Comparison with the wider literature and biological plausibility

The mechanism underlying caregiver-directed advice is coherent: caries is a behaviourally driven disease, caregiver behaviours determine the child's exposures, and behaviours are modifiable by counselling. There is good evidence that such interventions change the *proximal* behaviours — brushing frequency, fluoride toothpaste use, reported sugar intake. The difficulty lies in the long causal chain between knowledge change and clinically detectable caries reduction, across which effect attenuates at every link: knowledge → intention → sustained behaviour → changed oral exposure → changed demineralisation balance → fewer detectable lesions. The attenuation across that chain is a plausible reason why trials with robust behavioural effects report modest or null caries effects, and is consistent with the present finding that the caries endpoint remains unresolved while behavioural endpoints (excluded from this analysis by protocol) more reliably favour intervention.

It is also worth noting what this review *excluded* and why that matters for interpretation. Mean dmft/dmfs severity scores were excluded in favour of the prevalence endpoint. An intervention could reduce the *number of affected teeth per child* — a genuine clinical benefit — while leaving the *proportion of children with any caries* largely unchanged. The present analysis is therefore specifically silent on severity, which is an important complementary outcome and may well be where the benefit of caregiver advice chiefly resides.

### 10.4 Implications for practice

The evidence synthesised here does not support strong claims in either direction, and practitioners should resist both over-reading the RR of 0.428 and dismissing caregiver advice as ineffective. Several considerations nonetheless bear on practice:

- Caregiver oral health advice is **low-cost, low-risk and has established benefits on behavioural and knowledge outcomes**, and it supports broader child-health goals (nutrition, bottle weaning). The case for continuing to deliver it does not rest solely on a demonstrated caries-prevalence reduction.
- However, **it should not be presented to funders or families as a proven caries-prevention measure** on the strength of this evidence. The pooled interval includes no benefit.
- Where caries prevention is the explicit objective, advice should be positioned as a **complement to, not a substitute for, interventions with stronger evidence** — notably fluoride toothpaste use and professionally applied fluoride. Note that this review deliberately excluded advice-plus-fluoride arms in favour of advice-only arms in order to isolate the advice component; the combined packages may well perform better than either element alone, and are not evaluated here.
- Given τ² = 0.7158, **the average effect is a poor guide to what any specific programme will achieve.** Local implementation should be locally evaluated.

### 10.5 Implications for research

The priorities that follow from this synthesis are specific:

1. **Adequately powered trials.** Three trials totalling 732 children is insufficient. Powering on caries *prevalence* in the primary dentition, with realistic assumptions about control-arm prevalence and attrition over multi-year follow-up, implies substantially larger samples than most trials in this field have recruited.
2. **Harmonised case definitions.** Adoption of a standard, cavitation-anchored caries definition for the primary outcome (with non-cavitated lesions reported separately as a secondary outcome) would remove what is probably the largest single source of the heterogeneity observed here.
3. **Reporting of both prevalence and severity.** Trials should report the proportion of children affected *and* mean dmft/dmfs, so that reviews are not forced to choose between a prevalence synthesis that misses severity benefit and a severity synthesis that misses prevalence benefit.
4. **Characterisation of intervention dose and content.** Reporting against a behaviour-change taxonomy would permit future reviews to test dose–response rather than speculate about it.
5. **Individual participant data pooling.** With so few trials, IPD meta-analysis is the most realistic route to exploring the moderators identified in Section 7.1 — baseline risk, follow-up duration, definition, intervention intensity — that aggregate meta-regression cannot address at k = 3.

## 11. Data Quality and Validation Notes

No automated validation flags were raised by the analysis pipeline for this synthesis. Nevertheless, several data-integrity features of the dataset should be recorded plainly so that readers can calibrate their confidence in the pooled figure:

- **Five studies were eligible; only three contributed effect sizes.** Two of five eligible trials (40%) are absent from the pooled risk ratio because they did not report extractable arm-level event counts for caries in any primary tooth at the longest follow-up. The pooled estimate therefore represents a subset of the eligible evidence, and the possibility that the two non-contributing trials would shift the estimate cannot be excluded.
- **The pooled statistic is a genuine Risk Ratio.** The effect measure was specified a priori by the review question, native-scale pooling was used (`native_pooling_used = true`), and no measure conflict was detected. The pooled value of 0.428 is therefore a confirmed native-scale risk ratio and not a standardized fallback. It must not be described as a Hedges' *g*.
- **Study-level estimates are reported on the standardized (g) scale while pooling was on the RR scale.** The per-study values in Sections 4 and 5 (g = −0.0829, −0.6366, −0.9721) are the standardized estimates carried at study level; they are presented for comparison of magnitude and precision and are not directly numerically comparable to the pooled RR. Readers should not attempt to recover the pooled RR arithmetically from them.
- **Protocol-driven data-handling decisions affect the composition of the comparison.** Multi-arm trials had advice arms combined and control arms used once (avoiding double-counting of controls); advice-plus-fluoride arms were excluded in favour of advice-only arms (isolating the advice component but excluding potentially more effective combined packages); cluster-randomised trials contributed their own adjusted RR or OR as reported (appropriately handling clustering, but introducing mixed-measure contributions into a risk-ratio synthesis, since an adjusted OR diverges from the corresponding RR when events are common).
- **Where only the number randomised was available for the caries outcome, that denominator was used** in place of the number assessed, and this substitution is noted as specified in the protocol. This can bias event proportions where attrition was differential between arms.
- **Caries case definitions were not harmonised.** Each trial's own definition was used, as pre-specified. Definitions ranging from white-spot lesions to dentinal cavitation were therefore pooled together, which is a known and substantial source of the heterogeneity reported in Section 7.
- **Publication-bias diagnostics are formally reported but substantively uninformative at k = 3.** Egger's test and trim-and-fill are reported for completeness and transparency; neither has meaningful power with three studies and I² = 86.09%, and neither should be cited as evidence that the literature is unbiased.

---

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small number of poolable studies (k = 3).** This is the overriding limitation. It constrains the precision of the pooled estimate, renders τ² itself poorly estimated, makes the confidence interval extremely wide, precludes meta-regression, and deprives the publication-bias diagnostics of power.
2. **Considerable unexplained heterogeneity (I² = 86.09%, τ² = 0.7158, Q p = 0.0002).** The three trials are statistically incompatible with a common underlying effect, and two of the three have non-overlapping confidence intervals. The pooled mean may not correspond to the true effect in any real population.
3. **Statistically non-significant result (p = 0.1492) with an interval spanning the null (0.135 to 1.356).** The analysis can neither confirm nor exclude a clinically important benefit. This is an inconclusive finding, and it should not be reported as evidence of no effect.
4. **Incomplete representation of the eligible evidence.** Two of five eligible trials could not be pooled, so the synthesis covers 60% of the identified trial base.
5. **Heterogeneous caries case definitions**, permitted by protocol, which plausibly constitute the dominant source of between-study variability and limit the clinical interpretability of a pooled relative risk.
6. **Heterogeneous interventions.** "Caregiver-directed advice" aggregates one-off antenatal counselling, multi-contact motivational interviewing, family-centred programmes and photograph-based feedback — interventions differing in intensity, timing, theoretical basis and delivery agent.
7. **Heterogeneous comparators.** "Standard care" ranges from genuinely no advice to routine antenatal care that already contains oral health content, producing variable contamination and variable attenuation of the observed contrast.
8. **Variable follow-up durations**, compared at the longest reported timepoint for each trial, against a cumulative outcome whose attainable relative reduction depends on where on the prevalence curve measurement occurs.
9. **Mixing of individually randomised and cluster-randomised evidence**, with the latter contributing trial-reported adjusted RRs or ORs; odds ratios overstate risk ratios when the outcome is common, as caries frequently is.
10. **No formal risk-of-bias assessment is reported in the available data.** Blinding of caregivers to a counselling intervention is impossible, outcome assessor blinding is variable, and multi-year follow-up in this field commonly involves substantial and potentially differential attrition. The certainty of this body of evidence would almost certainly be downgraded on risk-of-bias grounds in addition to the downgrades already implied by imprecision and inconsistency.
11. **Severity outcomes excluded by protocol.** Mean dmft/dmfs was excluded in favour of prevalence, so the review is silent on whether caregiver advice reduces the number of affected teeth among children who do develop caries.
12. **Advice-plus-fluoride arms excluded by protocol.** The review isolates the advice component and therefore does not evaluate the combined packages that are often what is actually delivered in practice.
13. **No prediction interval is reported** with useful precision; at k = 3 with τ² = 0.7158 it would be uninformatively wide, but its absence means the expected range of effects in future settings is not formally quantified.

Applying GRADE-style reasoning informally, the certainty of evidence for this outcome would be rated **very low**, downgraded for serious imprecision (interval spanning the null, k = 3), serious inconsistency (I² = 86.09%), and indirectness/risk-of-bias concerns arising from definitional and comparator heterogeneity and unblindable delivery.

---

## 13. Conclusion

Pooling three randomised trials in 732 children, caregiver-directed oral hygiene, diet and feeding-practice advice delivered to pregnant women, new mothers or other primary caregivers yielded a **pooled Risk Ratio of 0.428 (95% CI 0.135 to 1.356; p = 0.1492)** for the presence of dental caries in any primary tooth at the longest reported follow-up. In accordance with the pre-specified direction field, this result **favours neither arm**: although the point estimate lies well below the null and all three contributing trials pointed in the same direction, the confidence interval includes both substantial benefit and modest harm, and the result is not statistically significant.

Between-study heterogeneity was **considerable and statistically significant (I² = 86.09%; τ² = 0.7158; Q = 17.005, p = 0.0002)**, with two of the three trials producing non-overlapping confidence intervals. Formal publication-bias diagnostics were unremarkable (Egger's p = 0.9784; trim-and-fill k₀ = 0; risk rated low) but carry little weight at k = 3 with high heterogeneity, and should be read as an absence of detectable asymmetry rather than as evidence that no bias exists.

The appropriate conclusion is one of **genuine uncertainty rather than demonstrated ineffectiveness.** Caregiver oral health advice remains a low-cost, low-risk intervention with established effects on caregiver knowledge and behaviour and plausible biological rationale, and there is no reason arising from this review to withdraw it from antenatal and well-child care. But the available trial evidence — only three poolable studies, with incompatible effect magnitudes and non-harmonised caries case definitions — does not currently establish that it reduces the proportion of children who develop caries in their primary teeth. Resolving the question will require larger trials, a standardised cavitation-anchored primary caries outcome, parallel reporting of prevalence and severity, explicit characterisation of intervention dose, and ideally individual participant data pooling to test the moderators that this analysis could only enumerate.
