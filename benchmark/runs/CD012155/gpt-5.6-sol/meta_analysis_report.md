# Caregiver Oral-Hygiene, Diet, or Feeding-Practice Advice for Preventing Caries in the Primary Dentition: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Advice delivered to pregnant women, new mothers, or other primary caregivers may help prevent dental caries in young children, but its effect on child-level caries prevalence is uncertain.

**Objective:** To assess the effect of caregiver-directed oral-hygiene, diet, or feeding-practice advice, compared with standard care or no advice, on the risk that children have caries in any primary tooth at the longest reported follow-up.

**Methods:** Five studies were represented in the review pipeline, of which three contributed effect sizes to the quantitative synthesis. The meta-analysis included 732 participants and pooled Risk Ratios (RRs) using a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) model. The intended outcome was child-level presence of caries in any primary tooth. Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were explored with Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Across three effect sizes, the pooled Risk Ratio was 0.4281630436411562 (95% CI 0.135 to 1.356; p = 0.149240448665201). The supplied direction field classified the result as favoring “neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).” Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 86.09%; τ² = 0.715834), and Cochran’s Q was statistically significant (Q = 17.005, p = 0.0002). Egger’s intercept was −0.5851 (p = 0.9784), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0). These publication-bias analyses were severely underpowered because only three effects were available.

**Conclusions:** The available synthesis did not establish a statistically significant effect of caregiver advice on child-level primary-tooth caries at the longest follow-up. The wide confidence interval and substantial between-study heterogeneity permit both potentially important benefit and little or no benefit. Incomplete reporting of screening, intervention, outcome-definition, follow-up, and risk-of-bias information, together with the small number of pooled studies, limits confidence in the result.

# Introduction

Dental caries in the primary dentition is common, preventable, and associated with pain, infection, impaired eating and sleep, and subsequent oral-health inequalities. Because oral-hygiene routines, dietary exposures, and feeding practices are established early in life, advice directed to pregnant women, new mothers, and other primary caregivers is a plausible preventive strategy. Such advice may influence toothbrushing, free-sugar exposure, bottle use, breastfeeding or complementary-feeding practices, and timely engagement with dental services.

This review addressed the following question: among pregnant women, new mothers, or other primary caregivers, what is the effect of oral-hygiene, diet, or feeding-practice advice, compared with standard care or no advice, on the risk that their children have caries in any primary tooth at the longest reported follow-up?

The prespecified outcome was binary child-level presence of dental caries in any primary tooth. The intended effect measure was the Risk Ratio (RR), with the advice intervention in the numerator and standard care or no advice in the denominator. Although the clinical protocol defines an RR below 1 as fewer caries events in the advice group, reporting of the direction of the pooled result below follows the supplied meta-analysis `favors` field, as required.

# Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects REML model pooled three effects from 732 participants. The **pooled Risk Ratio (RR)** was **0.4281630436411562**, with a **95% confidence interval from 0.135 to 1.356** and **p = 0.149240448665201**.

The confidence interval crossed the null value of 1, and the pooled result was not statistically significant at the conventional two-sided 0.05 level. In accordance with the supplied `favors` field, the pooled finding was classified as favoring **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).”** This direction statement is reported directly from the provided result rather than inferred from the magnitude or sign of the pooled estimate.

The interval was wide, indicating considerable imprecision. It included a potentially large reduction in risk as well as a value compatible with a possible increase in risk. Thus, the analysis does not provide a precise estimate of the intervention’s effect.

# Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- **I² = 86.09%**;
- **τ² = 0.715834**;
- **Cochran’s Q = 17.005**;
- **Q-test p = 0.0002**;
- **k = 3**.

The Q test indicates that the dispersion of effects exceeded that expected from within-study sampling error alone. The I² value suggests that a large proportion of the observed variability was attributable to between-study differences rather than sampling variation. The τ² estimate likewise indicates appreciable between-study variance on the model’s analysis scale.

These measures require caution with only three effects: Q has unstable operating characteristics in small meta-analyses, while I² and τ² can be imprecisely estimated. Nevertheless, the convergence of a high I² and a significant Q test supports genuine inconsistency among the included effects. Plausible sources include differences in the timing, content, intensity, or personnel delivering advice; baseline caries risk; comparator services; adherence; follow-up duration; attrition; and caries definitions. The supplied study-level information was insufficient to determine which explanations applied.

# Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−0.5851** with **p = 0.9784**. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies, and the supplied trim-and-fill estimate was **−0.5627**. The pipeline classified publication-bias risk as **low**.

These results provide no detected evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. However, absence of detected asymmetry is not evidence that reporting bias is absent. With only three effect sizes, Egger’s test is severely underpowered and is generally not recommended as a dependable basis for inference. Trim-and-fill is likewise unstable when there are very few studies and when heterogeneity is substantial. The scale of the supplied trim-and-fill estimate was not explicitly identified, so it should not be interpreted as a Risk Ratio. The publication-bias conclusion should therefore be regarded as exploratory despite the pipeline’s low-risk classification.

# Discussion

## Principal findings

This random-effects synthesis of three effects and 732 participants did not establish a statistically significant effect of caregiver-directed oral-hygiene, diet, or feeding-practice advice on the risk of caries in any primary tooth at the longest reported follow-up. The pooled Risk Ratio was 0.4281630436411562 (95% CI 0.135 to 1.356; p = 0.149240448665201), while the required direction field classified the finding as favoring neither arm.

The most consequential feature of the analysis was the marked inconsistency across studies. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 86.09%; τ² = 0.715834), and Cochran’s Q was significant (Q = 17.005, p = 0.0002). Therefore, the pooled estimate should be interpreted as an average across potentially dissimilar interventions and study contexts rather than as a universally applicable intervention effect.

## Clinical and methodological interpretation

Caregiver advice is a heterogeneous intervention category. Brief information provision may have a different effect from repeated counseling, motivational interviewing, anticipatory guidance, practical toothbrushing instruction, or interventions integrated with maternal and child health services. Likewise, advice delivered prenatally may act through different pathways from advice initiated after tooth eruption. These differences could plausibly contribute to the observed statistical heterogeneity, but the available data did not permit testing.

Outcome-definition variation is another likely concern. A definition including non-cavitated white-spot lesions will generally produce a higher prevalence than one restricted to cavitated or dentinal lesions. Differences in examiner calibration, clinical versus photographic assessment, and child age at follow-up can also influence measured prevalence. Because trial-specific definitions and follow-up times were unavailable, clinical comparability could not be established.

The confidence interval was too wide to support a conclusion of equivalence or no clinically important effect. Rather, it reflects uncertainty created by the small evidence base, differing effects, and limited reporting. Accordingly, the nonsignificant p value should not be interpreted as proof that caregiver advice is ineffective.

## Implications for practice and research

The current synthesis alone is insufficient to support a precise estimate of expected benefit across settings. Caregiver counseling may still be appropriate as part of routine anticipatory guidance because it is generally low intensity and aligns with established preventive principles, but clinical or policy recommendations should integrate intervention burden, feasibility, equity, adherence, and evidence from outcomes not included in this analysis.

Future trials should prespecify child-level caries prevalence in the full primary dentition, report arm-specific numbers assessed and numbers with caries, and provide both non-cavitated and cavitated thresholds where feasible. Reports should distinguish advice-only interventions from packages adding fluoride varnish, supplements, or other active dental treatments. Cluster-randomized trials should report cluster-adjusted RRs or provide sufficient information for appropriate adjustment. Investigators should also document intervention fidelity, contamination, usual-care content, and adverse events. Individual-participant-data synthesis could help harmonize diagnostic thresholds and investigate effect modification by baseline risk.

# Limitations

This review has several important limitations:

1. **Incomplete search documentation:** Databases, search dates, search strategies, screening totals, and exclusion reasons were unavailable, preventing assessment of search completeness and full PRISMA flow reporting.
2. **Only three pooled effects:** Although five studies appeared in the pipeline, only three contributed quantitative data. The reasons for non-contribution of the other two studies were not supplied.
3. **Missing study characteristics:** Intervention components, comparator details, participant demographics, settings, and follow-up durations were unavailable, limiting judgments about applicability.
4. **Unreported caries definitions:** Trial-specific diagnostic criteria could not be recorded or compared, and adherence to the intended preference for any carious lesion in any primary tooth could not be independently verified.
5. **No arm-level event data:** Numbers with caries and numbers assessed in each arm were unavailable. Percentage-to-count conversions, denominator choices, shared-control handling, and multi-arm exclusions could not be audited.
6. **Study-level scale ambiguity:** The three study-level pipeline values were negative and therefore were not directly interpretable as untransformed RRs, although the meta-analysis record confirmed native RR pooling. Independent reconstruction was impossible.
7. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** The internal validity of the included studies could not be evaluated.
8. **Substantial heterogeneity:** I² was 86.09%, making the meaning and transportability of the average effect uncertain.
9. **Imprecision:** The 95% CI was wide and crossed the null.
10. **Publication-bias tests were underpowered:** Egger’s test and trim-and-fill are not reliable with only three effects, despite no detected asymmetry and k₀ = 0.
11. **No certainty-of-evidence assessment:** The information required for a formal GRADE assessment was not available. Given the imprecision, inconsistency, and missing risk-of-bias information, confidence in the pooled estimate should be cautious.

# Conclusion

In this random-effects meta-analysis of three effects involving 732 participants, caregiver-directed oral-hygiene, diet, or feeding-practice advice did not produce a statistically significant pooled effect on child-level primary-tooth caries at the longest follow-up. The pooled Risk Ratio was 0.4281630436411562 (95% CI 0.135 to 1.356; p = 0.149240448665201), and the supplied direction classification favored neither arm. Substantial heterogeneity (I² = 86.09%; τ² = 0.715834; Q = 17.005, p = 0.0002) and incomplete reporting materially limit interpretation. Better-reported, adequately powered trials using harmonized child-level caries outcomes are needed to determine the magnitude and consistency of any preventive effect.
