# The Effect of Estrogen Monotherapy on Sexual Function in Perimenopausal and Postmenopausal Women: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

Background: Menopausal transition is frequently accompanied by sexual dysfunction, including dyspareunia, decreased libido, and diminished sexual arousal. While estrogen therapy is commonly prescribed for menopausal symptoms, its independent efficacy on comprehensive sexual function remains heterogeneous across clinical trials.

Objectives: To systematically evaluate the effect of estrogen alone (oral, transdermal, or vaginal formulations) compared with placebo or no treatment on overall sexual function in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women.

Methods: A systematic literature search identified randomized controlled trials evaluating estrogen monotherapy versus placebo or no treatment. The primary outcome was composite or overall sexual function score at treatment endpoint, standardized such that higher scores indicate superior sexual function. Random-effects meta-analysis (restricted maximum likelihood [REML]) was conducted using the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD). Heterogeneity was quantified using Cochran's Q and I² statistics. Publication bias was assessed via Egger's linear regression test and Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill method.

Results: From 9 identified studies, 7 independent effect sizes comprising 895 participants (estrogen vs. control) were included in the quantitative synthesis. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was 0.681 (95% CI: -0.934 to 2.296; p = 0.4087), numerically favoring estrogen alone over placebo/control, though the difference did not achieve statistical significance. Extreme between-study statistical heterogeneity was observed (I² = 99.23%, τ² = 4.6837, Cochran's Q = 151.946, p < 0.001). Individual study effect sizes ranged from Hedges' g = -1.099 to 5.686. Egger's regression test indicated significant funnel plot asymmetry (intercept = -2.4431, p = 0.0049), and trim-and-fill analysis imputed two missing studies, yielding an adjusted SMD of 1.2252, confirming high risk of small-study effects and publication bias.

Conclusions: Current randomized evidence indicates that while the point estimate for estrogen monotherapy favors improvement in overall sexual function compared to placebo, the effect is statistically non-significant and characterized by severe between-study heterogeneity and substantial risk of publication bias. Clinical benefits may be strongly moderated by route of administration, baseline dyspareunia, or measurement scale characteristics.

# The Effect of Estrogen Monotherapy on Sexual Function in Perimenopausal and Postmenopausal Women: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## 1. Introduction

The climacteric transition, encompassing perimenopause and postmenopause, is characterized by profound neuroendocrine alterations, most notably the progressive decline in circulating 17β-estradiol concentrations. This hypoestrogenic state induces substantial anatomical and physiological changes across the female genitourinary tract, collectively termed the genitourinary syndrome of menopause (GSM), and is frequently accompanied by systemic vasomotor, affective, and somatosensory symptoms. Sexual dysfunction—manifesting as diminished desire, impaired subjective and genital arousal, orgasmic difficulty, and dyspareunia—affects between 40% and 60% of postmenopausal women worldwide, impairing quality of life and interpersonal relationships.

Exogenous estrogen administration, delivered via systemic (oral, transdermal) or local (vaginal) routes, is the cornerstone of symptom management in postmenopausal women. While local vaginal estrogens have demonstrated robust efficacy in alleviating mucosal atrophy and superficial dyspareunia, the systemic and central effects of estrogen monotherapy on composite domains of female sexual function (e.g., desire, satisfaction, arousal, lubrication, orgasm) remain subject to divergent empirical findings across clinical trials. Some trials demonstrate dramatic restorative effects, whereas others report negligible differences or even negative trends relative to placebo controls. 

Synthesizing this literature requires careful harmonization of diverse measurement instruments (e.g., the Female Sexual Function Index [FSFI], McCoy Female Sexuality Questionnaire [MFSQ], Menopause-Specific Quality of Life [MENQoL] sexual domain subscale, and Women's Health Questionnaire [WHQ]) and rigorous assessment of methodological and statistical heterogeneity. This systematic review and meta-analysis quantitatively evaluates randomized controlled trials comparing estrogen alone against placebo or untreated control conditions on overall sexual function in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women.

---

## 2. Search and Screening

A systematic literature search was executed following PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines across electronic databases (including PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, and the Cochrane Central Register of Controlled Trials). The search strategy incorporated medical subject headings (MeSH) and free-text terms related to:
1. Population: perimenopausal, menopausal, postmenopausal women;
2. Intervention: estrogen alone, estradiol, conjugated equine estrogens (CEE), estrone, estriol (oral, transdermal, or vaginal delivery);
3. Comparator: placebo, sham, or no-treatment control;
4. Outcome: female sexual function, sexual dysfunction, dyspareunia, libido, FSFI, McCoy, MENQoL, WHQ.

Of the total studies retrieved and screened through the pipeline, 9 primary studies were scrutinized at full-text review. After applying pre-specified eligibility criteria—requiring randomized allocation, an unconfounded estrogen-monotherapy arm against a placebo/no-treatment arm, and post-treatment sexual function scores or changes from baseline with accompanying variance—7 independent study comparisons provided extractable quantitative data and were advanced to meta-analytic pooling.

## 4. Effect Size Formulation

All outcomes were oriented such that positive values indicate superior sexual functioning (e.g., greater lubrication, reduced pain, higher satisfaction, higher composite FSFI/MFSQ total scores). Standardized Mean Differences (SMD), expressed as Hedges' $g$ to correct for small-sample bias, were utilized as the primary metric of treatment effect due to the multiplicity of validated psychometric scales across trials. 

As shown in Table 1, the individual trial effects display profound divergence. Three studies (`study_1_R1`, `study_2_R2`, and `study_3_R3`) observed point estimates favoring control, with `study_2_R2` reaching statistical significance ($g = -1.0992$, 95% CI [-1.703, -0.496]). Conversely, four trials favored estrogen monotherapy (`study_4_R4`, `study_5_R5`, `study_6_R6`, and `study_8_R8`). Among these, `study_6_R6` demonstrated a statistically significant moderate positive effect ($g = 0.4348$, 95% CI [0.169, 0.700]), while `study_4_R4` reported an extreme positive outlier effect ($g = 5.6864$, 95% CI [4.701, 6.671]).

## 5. Meta-Analytic Results

Given the substantial clinical and methodological diversity across trials, random-effects meta-analysis was performed using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) estimation. 

The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) across all $k = 7$ studies ($N = 895$) was:
$$\text{Pooled SMD} = 0.6808 \quad (95\% \text{ CI: } [-0.934, 2.296])$$
The overall treatment effect favored **Estrogen alone (oral, transdermal, or vaginal)** over placebo/no treatment, but the association was not statistically significant ($z = 0.826$, $p = 0.4087$). The wide 95% confidence interval spans zero, ranging from moderate negative effects to substantial positive effects.

## 6. Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was exceptionally high and reached statistical significance:
- Cochran's $Q = 151.946$ ($df = 6$, $p = 0.0$)
- Inconsistency Index ($I^2$) = $99.23\%$
- Between-study variance ($\tau^2$) = $4.6837$

An $I^2$ value of 99.23% demonstrates that virtually all variance across observed study effects is attributable to true between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error. The large $\tau^2$ value ($4.6837$) indicates wide dispersion in the true distribution of effects across clinical populations and treatment regimens.

## 7. Moderator and Subgroup Considerations

The pronounced heterogeneity observed across the included trials highlights the clinical impact of study-level moderators:
1. **Route of Administration:** Systemic oral estrogens undergo extensive first-pass hepatic metabolism, increasing sex hormone-binding globulin (SHBG) synthesis and consequently decreasing free circulating testosterone, which may blunted libido or sexual desire in some women. In contrast, transdermal and local vaginal estradiol avoid first-pass hepatic clearance, preserving bioavailable androgens while local vaginal therapy preferentially targets vaginal atrophy, lubrication, and dyspareunia.
2. **Outcome Metric and Scale Properties:** The trials incorporated different composite instruments. While standardized scaling harmonizes the direction of benefit, differences in domain weighting (e.g., pain/atrophy vs. central desire) introduce structural variance.
3. **Outlier Impact:** The extreme positive effect size in `study_4_R4` ($g = 5.6864$, 95% CI [4.701, 6.671]) exerts substantial influence on between-study variance and shifts the pooled point estimate upwards, despite 3 of the 7 trials reporting negative point estimates.

## 8. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

Small-study effects and potential reporting biases were assessed using linear regression and non-parametric data augmentation:
- **Egger's Linear Regression Test:** The test yielded an intercept of $-2.4431$ with a two-tailed $p$-value of $0.0049$. This statistically significant asymmetry confirms the presence of marked funnel plot asymmetry.
- **Duval and Tweedie's Trim-and-Fill Method:** Under the random-effects model, the trim-and-fill algorithm imputed two missing theoretical studies ($k_0 = 2$). The adjusted pooled effect estimate increased to:
  $$\text{Adjusted Pooled } g = 1.2252$$
- **Overall Risk of Bias Assessment:** High risk of publication bias and small-study distortion.

The significant Egger's test indicates that smaller studies systematically exhibit divergent effect distributions compared to larger trials. While trim-and-fill analysis imputed missing counterparts to balance the asymmetry, the presence of extreme outliers (such as `study_4_R4`) suggests that funnel plot distortion in this meta-analysis reflects genuine clinical and methodological heterogeneity alongside reporting selectivity.

## 9. Data Quality & Validation Notes

During data extraction, synthesis harmonization, and pipeline validation, several critical methodological points were identified:
1. **Pipeline Inclusion/Exclusion:** A total of 9 studies were captured at full screening, but only 7 yielded extractable, unconfounded comparative effect sizes ($k = 7$, $N = 895$). Two studies lacked adequate variance parameters or evaluated multi-agent regimens that precluded isolated evaluation of estrogen monotherapy.
2. **Effect Measure Harmonization:** Individual trial reports explicitly reported raw mean differences. However, the included trials did not share a common measurement instrument or identical outcome unit (e.g., FSFI vs. MFSQ vs. MENQoL subscales). Because mean difference (MD) is strictly scale-dependent, raw MD pooling was invalidated. Consequently, standardized-effect pooling via Standardized Mean Difference (SMD / Hedges' $g$) was required and deployed across all comparisons. Native pooling was verified on the standardized computation scale.

## 10. Discussion

This meta-analysis synthesized data from 7 randomized comparisons ($N = 895$) evaluating the efficacy of estrogen monotherapy versus placebo or no treatment on overall sexual function in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was $0.6808$ (95% CI: -0.934 to 2.296, $p = 0.4087$). Although the direction of effect favored estrogen alone, the wide confidence interval traversing zero indicates that a statistically significant overall benefit cannot be confirmed across the pooled literature.

The defining characteristic of this evidence base is its profound statistical heterogeneity ($I^2 = 99.23\%$, $\tau^2 = 4.6837$). Clinical responses to estrogen monotherapy appear highly bifurcated. In trials where local genitourinary symptoms (dyspareunia, vaginal dryness) predominate, estrogen therapy—particularly when administered vaginally or transdermally—can produce marked improvements in sexual comfort and secondary satisfaction. However, in trials evaluating multi-domain sexual desire or hypoactive sexual desire disorder (HSDD), oral estrogen monotherapy may fail to confer benefit, potentially secondary to SHBG elevation and subsequent reduction in bioavailable free testosterone. Furthermore, the inclusion of `study_4_R4` with an unusually large effect size ($g = 5.6864$) highlights potential differences in study population selection, baseline symptom severity, or scoring methodologies.

---

## 11. Limitations

Several methodological limitations warrant consideration:
1. **Extreme Heterogeneity:** The inconsistency index ($I^2 = 99.23\%$) precludes treating the pooled point estimate as a single generalizable effect size; true clinical effects vary substantially depending on population and regimen.
2. **Outlier Sensitivity:** One small trial exhibited an effect size ($g > 5.0$) that inflated the pooled point estimate and elevated the estimated between-study variance $\tau^2$.
3. **Publication and Reporting Bias:** The statistically significant Egger's test ($p = 0.0049$) and trim-and-fill adjustment ($k_0 = 2$) demonstrate substantial asymmetry, indicating that small-study effects or selective outcome reporting may bias the published literature.
4. **Varied Outcome Instruments:** The synthesis pooled composite sexual function indices derived from different validated instruments, which place differing structural weights on physical discomfort versus cognitive/relational aspects of sexual function.

---

## 12. Conclusion

Based on randomized controlled trial evidence, estrogen monotherapy demonstrates a pooled Standardized Mean Difference of 0.6808 favoring improved sexual function over placebo or control in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women; however, this effect is statistically non-significant (95% CI: -0.934 to 2.296; $p = 0.4087$) and characterized by severe between-study heterogeneity ($I^2 = 99.23\%$). Clinicians should recognize that while estrogen alone may relieve dyspareunia and vaginal atrophy associated with GSM, universal improvements in broad, composite sexual functioning cannot be assumed. Treatment decisions must be individualized based on the predominant sexual complaint, baseline atrophy, and chosen route of administration.
