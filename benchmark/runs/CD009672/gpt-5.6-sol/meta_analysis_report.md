# Estrogen Alone for Overall Sexual Function in Perimenopausal and Postmenopausal Women: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

## Abstract

**Background:** Estrogen is used to manage menopausal symptoms, but its effect on overall or composite sexual function remains uncertain.

**Objective:** To evaluate estrogen alone—oral, transdermal, or vaginal estradiol or conjugated estrogens at any dose—versus placebo or no treatment for overall/composite sexual function at the end of treatment in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons evaluated estrogen monotherapy against placebo or no treatment and reported an overall/composite sexual-function outcome at the end of treatment. Outcomes were oriented so that higher scores represented better sexual function. Six effect sizes from nine included studies were available for quantitative synthesis. A random-effects model estimated the pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were examined using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** The six pooled comparisons included 828 women. The pooled SMD was 0.7717216939598948 (95% CI −1.140 to 2.684; p=0.4289139963636114), favoring estrogen alone according to the prespecified direction field, but the confidence interval included no effect. Heterogeneity was extreme (Q=151.917, I²=99.39%, τ²=5.637746; Q-test p reported as 0.0). Study-level Hedges’ g values ranged from −1.099187 to 5.686352. Egger’s intercept was −2.3678 (p=0.0096), indicating funnel-plot asymmetry consistent with small-study effects or publication bias. Trim-and-fill estimated two potentially missing studies and produced an adjusted effect of 1.4993. Publication-bias risk was classified as high.

**Conclusions:** The available synthesis does not establish a statistically significant end-of-treatment benefit of estrogen alone for overall/composite sexual function. Extreme between-study heterogeneity, one exceptionally large study-level estimate, incomplete quantitative data for three included studies, and evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry make the pooled estimate unstable and clinically uncertain.

## Introduction

Sexual function during the menopausal transition and after menopause is influenced by hormonal, genitourinary, psychological, relational, and medical factors. Estrogen therapy may affect sexual function directly or indirectly, including through changes in genital comfort, lubrication, sleep, and menopausal symptoms. However, improvements in specific symptoms do not necessarily translate into improvements in multidimensional or composite sexual function.

This review addressed the following question: among perimenopausal and postmenopausal women, what is the effect of estrogen alone—oral, transdermal, or vaginal estradiol or conjugated estrogens, at any dose—compared with placebo or no treatment, on overall/composite sexual function at the end of treatment?

The target outcome was one overall or composite sexual-function measure per trial. Validated overall scores, such as a Female Sexual Function Index total score or McCoy Female Sexuality Questionnaire total score, were preferred. When these were unavailable, a sexual domain or item from a broader quality-of-life instrument was eligible. Measures were to be oriented so that higher values represented better sexual function. End-of-treatment values were preferred; change scores were eligible only when endpoint data were unavailable.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood pooled six comparisons involving 828 women. The pooled **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** was **0.7717216939598948** (95% CI **−1.140 to 2.684**; p=**0.4289139963636114**). The result was designated as favoring **estrogen alone**, based on the supplied `favors` field. However, the confidence interval crossed zero and was very wide; therefore, the meta-analysis did not demonstrate a statistically significant benefit at the conventional 0.05 threshold.

The pooled point estimate is not sufficiently stable to support a confident statement about the magnitude of benefit. Its uncertainty spans effects in both directions, and the exceptionally large estimate from `study_4_R4` is visually and statistically important in a synthesis containing only six effects.

A cumulative meta-analysis plot was generated at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`. Because numerical cumulative estimates and the study-ordering rule were not supplied, no temporal or sequential interpretation is asserted here.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was extreme:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 151.917
- **Q-test p-value:** reported as 0.0
- **I²:** 99.39%
- **τ²:** 5.637746

The Q statistic greatly exceeded its degrees of freedom (k−1=5), and I² indicated that nearly all observed dispersion was attributable to between-study inconsistency rather than sampling error under the model. The p-value reported as 0.0 should be understood as a value below the reporting precision of the analytic output, not literally a probability of zero.

This heterogeneity makes the average effect difficult to generalize. Plausible sources could include estrogen formulation, administration route, dose, treatment duration, menopausal stage, baseline sexual dysfunction, outcome instrument, use of endpoint versus change scores, and study quality. Those explanations remain hypotheses because the necessary trial-level covariates were not provided.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression yielded an intercept of **−2.3678** with **p=0.0096**. This statistically significant result indicates funnel-plot asymmetry consistent with possible publication bias or small-study effects. It does not, by itself, prove selective publication: heterogeneity, outcome differences, methodological variation, and the exceptionally large estimate may also generate asymmetry.

Trim-and-fill estimated **two** potentially missing studies and returned an adjusted effect of **1.4993**. No confidence interval or p-value for this adjusted estimate was supplied. Moreover, trim-and-fill relies on assumptions that are particularly vulnerable when heterogeneity is extreme and the number of studies is small. The adjusted value should therefore be treated as a sensitivity analysis rather than a corrected truth.

The overall publication-bias risk was classified as **high**. This classification, together with I²=99.39%, materially reduces confidence in the pooled result.

## Discussion

This random-effects synthesis of six comparisons and 828 women produced a pooled SMD of 0.7717216939598948 favoring estrogen alone according to the supplied direction classification, but the 95% confidence interval ranged from −1.140 to 2.684 and the test of the pooled effect was not statistically significant (p=0.4289139963636114). Thus, the available quantitative evidence does not establish that estrogen monotherapy improves overall or composite sexual function by the end of treatment compared with placebo or no treatment.

Interpretation must focus less on the pooled point estimate than on its instability. Individual effects ranged from −1.099187 to 5.686352, and heterogeneity was extreme. In particular, `study_4_R4` reported Hedges’ g=5.686352 (95% CI 4.701 to 6.671), far outside the range of the other five estimates. Such a large standardized effect may represent a genuinely distinct population or intervention, but it can also arise from a very small within-group standard deviation, an unusual or poorly comparable measure, selective reporting, data-extraction error, or other design features. The supplied data do not allow these possibilities to be adjudicated.

The standardized analysis was appropriate to the supplied outcome structure because common native outcome units were not confirmed. The metadata note that some papers explicitly reported mean differences, but the rows did not share a common `outcome_units` value. A raw mean difference would therefore have been scale-dependent and potentially uninterpretable across instruments. Standardization permits pooling across different measures, although it does not remove differences in what those instruments measure or in their reliability.

The observed funnel-plot asymmetry further limits inference. Egger’s test was significant, and the trim-and-fill procedure imputed two studies. Nevertheless, only six effects were available, heterogeneity was extreme, and the trim-and-fill adjustment increased rather than attenuated the point estimate. Publication-bias methods under these conditions are fragile; the main defensible conclusion is that small-study effects or related asymmetry are present and that the pooled estimate warrants caution.

Clinically, these results should not be interpreted as showing that estrogen has no effect on every dimension of sexual health. The review specifically addressed overall/composite sexual-function measures, not isolated symptoms such as vaginal dryness, dyspareunia, or atrophy. Benefits for specific genitourinary symptoms could occur without a detectable or consistent improvement in a multidimensional sexual-function score. Conversely, improvements on a broad score may depend on baseline symptoms and contextual factors not captured in the aggregate data.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

The supplied analytic metadata raise several issues that should be explicit:

1. Nine studies were counted as included, but only six effect sizes were available for meta-analysis; reasons for the three-study shortfall were not provided.
2. Trial reports were represented by internal labels rather than bibliographic citations, preventing verification of study identity, overlapping samples, intervention details, and outcome selection.
3. The outcome rows did not share confirmed common native units. Although some source papers reportedly described mean differences, pooling on a raw mean-difference scale was not trusted; the quantitative synthesis therefore used the explicitly requested Standardized Mean Difference.
4. One study-level estimate (`study_4_R4`) was exceptionally large and is a major potential source of instability. No influence or leave-one-out statistics were supplied.
5. Details needed to validate the prescribed outcome hierarchy—including instrument validity, reversal of worse-is-higher scales, choice of the last assessment at or before treatment completion, and endpoint versus change-score use—were not available in the context.
6. No trial-level risk-of-bias assessments or certainty-of-evidence ratings were supplied.

## Limitations

This report is limited by both the evidence base and the completeness of the supplied review record.

First, only six effect sizes were pooled, restricting precision and making random-effects variance estimation and publication-bias diagnostics unstable. Second, heterogeneity was extreme (I²=99.39%), so the pooled SMD may not represent a clinically coherent common effect. Third, an exceptionally large positive effect strongly widened the range of results, but no prespecified outlier, influence, or leave-one-out analysis was available. Fourth, three of the nine included studies did not contribute to the meta-analysis for unexplained reasons, creating a risk of availability bias.

Fifth, the absence of trial-level characteristics prevented subgroup analyses by route, formulation, dose, treatment duration, menopausal stage, baseline dysfunction, and outcome instrument. Sixth, it was not possible to distinguish endpoint from change-score estimates or verify outcome orientation at the study level. Seventh, study-level risk of bias and overall certainty of evidence could not be assessed from the supplied data. Eighth, Egger’s test and trim-and-fill have limited reliability with only six studies and very high heterogeneity. Finally, the search and screening process was incompletely documented in the provided context, precluding a full assessment of review comprehensiveness and reproducibility.

## Conclusion

In six pooled comparisons involving 828 women, estrogen alone versus placebo or no treatment yielded a pooled Standardized Mean Difference of 0.7717216939598948 (95% CI −1.140 to 2.684; p=0.4289139963636114), designated as favoring estrogen alone but not statistically significant. The evidence was characterized by extreme heterogeneity (I²=99.39%; τ²=5.637746), marked dispersion of study effects, and significant funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger’s intercept −2.3678; p=0.0096). Consequently, the magnitude and even consistency of any effect on overall/composite sexual function remain uncertain.

Future syntheses should obtain complete trial-level data, verify outcome direction and assessment timing, document endpoint versus change-score use, investigate the exceptional estimate, avoid double-counting related reports, and conduct clinically grounded subgroup analyses where sufficient studies exist. Better reported trials using validated multidimensional sexual-function instruments are needed to determine whether effects differ by estrogen route, formulation, dose, baseline symptoms, and menopausal stage.
