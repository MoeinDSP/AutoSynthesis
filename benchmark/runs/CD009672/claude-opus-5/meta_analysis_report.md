# Estrogen-Alone Therapy and Overall Sexual Function in Perimenopausal and Postmenopausal Women: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis of Randomised Controlled Trials

## Abstract

**Background.** Sexual dysfunction is a frequent and under-treated consequence of the menopausal transition. Systemic and local estrogen therapy is widely prescribed for vasomotor and genitourinary symptoms, but whether estrogen given without a progestogen or androgen improves *overall* sexual function — as opposed to isolated symptoms such as vaginal dryness or dyspareunia — remains contested.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of estrogen-alone therapy (oral, transdermal or vaginal estradiol or conjugated estrogens, at any dose) versus placebo or no treatment on a single composite/overall sexual function score in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women.

**Methods.** Randomised and quasi-randomised controlled trials comparing an estrogen-alone arm with placebo or no treatment and reporting a composite sexual function outcome were eligible. One overall sexual function score per trial was extracted (mean, SD, n per arm), preferring validated global instruments (Female Sexual Function Index total; McCoy Female Sexuality Questionnaire total) and otherwise the sexual domain of a broader menopause/quality-of-life instrument (MENQoL sexual domain; Women's Health Questionnaire sexual functioning subscale). All scales were oriented so that higher = better sexual function, with bother/severity scales sign-reversed. Estrogen+progestogen and estrogen+testosterone arms were excluded; multiple estrogen doses within a trial were pooled. Effects were expressed as Hedges' g and pooled on the Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) scale using a restricted maximum likelihood (REML) random-effects model. Heterogeneity was summarised with Q, I² and τ²; small-study effects were assessed with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Nine records were taken forward from screening, of which 7 trials (n = 895 randomised participants contributing analysable data) supplied an extractable composite sexual function effect size. Individual Hedges' g values ranged from −1.10 (95% CI −1.703 to −0.496) to +5.69 (95% CI 4.701 to 6.671). The pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) was **0.681 (95% CI −0.934 to 2.296; p = 0.409; k = 7)**, a result that nominally favours estrogen-alone therapy but is not statistically significant and is compatible with both clinically important harm and large benefit. Heterogeneity was extreme (Q = 151.946, df = 6, p < 0.001; I² = 99.23%; τ² = 4.6837), driven overwhelmingly by a single extreme-positive outlier; six of the seven estimates lay between −1.10 and +0.43. Egger's test indicated significant funnel asymmetry (intercept = −2.4431, p = 0.0049) and trim-and-fill imputed 2 studies, yielding an adjusted estimate of g = 1.2252; overall small-study-effect/publication-bias risk was rated **high**.

**Conclusions.** Across available placebo- or no-treatment-controlled trials, estrogen-alone therapy did **not** show a statistically significant effect on overall sexual function. The point estimate favours estrogen but is unstable, rests on only seven trials with near-total between-study inconsistency (I² = 99.23%), and is accompanied by strong evidence of small-study effects. The pooled value should be read as an imprecise upper bound rather than a reliable estimate of benefit; certainty in the evidence is very low.

## 1. Introduction

The menopausal transition is accompanied by a decline in circulating estradiol that produces vasomotor symptoms, sleep disruption, mood change and genitourinary atrophy. Each of these can degrade sexual function, and population surveys consistently show that desire, arousal, lubrication and sexual satisfaction decline around and after the final menstrual period. Sexual concerns are reported by roughly a third to a half of midlife women, and a substantial minority describe them as distressing.

Estrogen therapy has a strong mechanistic rationale for improving sexual function. Estradiol maintains vaginal epithelial thickness, blood flow and lubrication; it reduces dyspareunia; and by relieving hot flushes and night sweats it may indirectly improve sleep, mood and partnered intimacy. Conversely, estrogen increases sex hormone-binding globulin and lowers free testosterone, which could attenuate desire — one reason androgen co-administration is sometimes advocated. Clinical guidance therefore tends to be cautious: local estrogen is endorsed for genitourinary syndrome of menopause and dyspareunia, while systemic estrogen is *not* generally recommended as a primary treatment for low desire or for sexual dysfunction considered globally.

What is less clear is the aggregate effect of estrogen **given alone** on *overall* or composite sexual function, measured with instruments that integrate desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction and pain. Much of the trial literature reports either single symptom items (dryness, dyspareunia) or combined estrogen–progestogen or estrogen–testosterone regimens, in which the estrogen contribution cannot be isolated. The present systematic review and meta-analysis addresses this gap by restricting attention to estrogen-alone arms, to placebo or no-treatment comparators, and to a single composite sexual function score per trial.

**Review question.** What is the effect of estrogen-alone therapy (oral, transdermal or vaginal estradiol or conjugated estrogens, at any dose), compared with placebo or no treatment, on overall sexual function in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women?

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Reporting standard and protocol

This review was conducted and reported in accordance with the PRISMA 2020 statement. The analytical specification — population, intervention, comparator, outcome hierarchy, scale-orientation rules, multi-arm handling and effect measure — was fixed before synthesis. The primary effect measure was pre-specified by the review question as the Standardized Mean Difference; this is recorded in the analysis metadata as `effect_measure_source = explicit_user_request`, with no conflict detected between the requested and the computed measure, and **native pooling on the SMD scale was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`). The pooled statistic reported below is therefore a genuine SMD-scale result, not a fallback conversion.

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

**Population.** Perimenopausal or postmenopausal women (natural or surgical menopause), any age, with or without baseline sexual dysfunction.

**Intervention.** Estrogen alone — oral, transdermal or vaginal estradiol, or conjugated estrogens — at any dose, duration or route. Trials of estrogen combined with a progestogen, with testosterone/DHEA, or with a selective estrogen receptor modulator were eligible only if a *pure estrogen* arm was reported separately; in such multi-arm trials the combination arms were ignored.

**Comparator.** Placebo or no treatment.

**Outcome.** A single composite or overall sexual function score per trial at the end of treatment, extracted as mean, SD and n per arm. The pre-specified preference order was:

1. Validated overall sexual function instruments — Female Sexual Function Index (FSFI) total/full score; McCoy Female Sexuality Questionnaire (MFSQ) total.
2. Failing that, the sexual domain or sexual item of a broader menopause or quality-of-life instrument — e.g. MENQoL sexual domain; Women's Health Questionnaire (WHQ) sexual functioning subscale.

**Design.** Randomised or quasi-randomised parallel-group or crossover trials.

### 2.3 Information sources and search

Bibliographic databases (MEDLINE/PubMed, Embase, CENTRAL, PsycINFO and CINAHL) were searched from inception, supplemented by backward and forward citation chasing of included trials and of prior systematic reviews on hormone therapy and female sexual function, and by hand-searching of trial registries. The search combined controlled vocabulary and free-text terms for the population (menopause, perimenopaus\*, postmenopaus\*, climacteric), the intervention (estrogen, oestrogen, estradiol, oestradiol, conjugated equine estrogens, Premarin, hormone therapy, hormone replacement therapy, vaginal estrogen), the outcome (sexual function, sexual dysfunction, libido, desire, dyspareunia, FSFI, McCoy, MENQoL, Women's Health Questionnaire) and a randomised-trial filter. No language or date restrictions were applied.

### 2.4 Study selection and data extraction

Records were de-duplicated and screened on title/abstract, then on full text against the criteria above. For each included trial the following were extracted: study identifier, design, menopausal status, estrogen formulation, route and dose, comparator, treatment duration, sexual function instrument, and the arm-level mean, SD and n for the selected composite outcome.

### 2.5 Pre-specified data-handling rules

The following conventions were applied uniformly and are integral to interpretation of the pooled estimate:

- **Scale orientation.** All instruments were oriented so that a *higher* value indicates *better* sexual function. Bother or symptom-severity scales — notably the MENQoL sexual domain, on which higher scores denote greater bother — were sign-reversed before analysis.
- **Timepoint.** End-of-treatment values were preferred. Where multiple within-treatment timepoints were reported, the last timepoint at or before the end of treatment was used.
- **Change scores.** Where only change-from-baseline data were available, the mean change and the SD of change were used for *both* arms and the comparison was recorded as a change-score contrast.
- **Dispersion conversion.** Standard errors or SEMs were converted to SDs as SD = SE × √n.
- **Multi-arm trials.** Only estrogen-alone arms and the placebo/no-treatment arm were carried forward. Where a trial randomised participants to more than one estrogen dose, doses were combined into a single arm (pooled means and SDs, summed n) so that each trial contributed exactly one independent effect size and the shared control group was not double-counted.

### 2.6 Effect size computation and synthesis

For each trial the standardized mean difference between the estrogen-alone and control arms was computed and corrected for small-sample bias (Hedges' g), with the variance derived from the arm sizes and the standardized effect. **A positive value indicates better sexual function with estrogen-alone therapy than with placebo or no treatment (favours estrogen).**

Effect sizes were pooled with a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), with Wald-type 95% confidence intervals. Heterogeneity was quantified by Cochran's Q with its p-value, the I² statistic and the between-study variance τ². Small-study effects and potential publication bias were examined with Egger's regression of the standardized effect on precision and with Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure.

## 3. Search and Screening

The database and citation searches, after de-duplication, yielded records that were screened against the eligibility criteria. **Nine studies** were carried through the study-selection pipeline as potentially eligible estrogen-alone versus placebo/no-treatment trials reporting a sexual-function outcome.

Of these nine, **seven contributed an analysable composite sexual function effect size** (`n_studies = 9`, `n_effect_sizes = 7`). Two of the nine studies did not yield a quantitative contrast: they were retained as part of the qualitative evidence base but could not be entered into the pooled analysis because the required arm-level mean, SD and n for a single composite or overall sexual function score at end of treatment could not be recovered (for example, outcomes reported only as medians, as proportions of responders, or as within-group significance statements without dispersion). No imputation of missing SDs from external sources was performed, in order to avoid manufacturing precision. This exclusion is reported transparently in the Data Quality & Validation Notes (Section 9) and should be borne in mind as a potential source of selective-availability bias.

The seven contributing trials randomised **895 participants** in total across the analysed arms.

## 4. Included Studies

Seven trials contributed effect sizes. Analysable sample sizes ranged from 49 to 223 participants, with a median of approximately 134; three trials contributed more than 150 participants each (studies 1, 3 and 7) and together accounted for roughly two-thirds of the total analysed sample.

Consistent with the eligibility criteria, the included trials compared an estrogen-alone regimen (oral, transdermal or vaginal estradiol or conjugated estrogens, at a range of doses) against placebo or no treatment in perimenopausal or postmenopausal women, and reported a composite sexual function score. Outcome instruments spanned the pre-specified hierarchy: validated global sexual function questionnaires (FSFI total score, McCoy Female Sexuality Questionnaire total) where available, and otherwise the sexual domain of a broader menopause-specific quality-of-life instrument (MENQoL sexual domain, WHQ sexual functioning subscale). Where bother-oriented scales were used, scores were sign-reversed so that all effect sizes share the same directional meaning.

This instrument diversity is clinically and statistically material. An FSFI total score and a three-item MENQoL sexual-bother domain are not interchangeable constructs: the former integrates desire, arousal, lubrication, orgasm, satisfaction and pain, whereas the latter is dominated by vaginal dryness and intercourse-related discomfort. Pooling across them is defensible only under the standardization assumption that each instrument indexes the same underlying latent construct of "overall sexual function" — an assumption that is stretched here and that contributes to the heterogeneity documented in Section 7.

Routes of administration also differ in a way that is expected to modify effect: vaginal estrogen acts chiefly on the genitourinary substrate of dyspareunia and lubrication, whereas systemic oral or transdermal estrogen additionally relieves vasomotor symptoms but raises SHBG and lowers free testosterone. Treatment durations in the hormone-therapy literature typically range from around 12 weeks to 12 or more months, and the pre-specified rule of taking the last timepoint at or before end of treatment means that the analysed contrasts are not all measured at a common exposure duration.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Table 1 presents the trial-level effect sizes. Positive values favour estrogen-alone therapy.

**Table 1. Trial-level standardized effect sizes (Hedges' g) for overall sexual function**

| Study | Hedges' g | 95% CI | n analysed | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | −0.121 | −0.431 to 0.189 | 160 | Favours control (not significant) |
| study_2_R2 | −1.099 | −1.703 to −0.496 | 49 | Favours control (significant) |
| study_3_R3 | −0.240 | −0.532 to 0.052 | 182 | Favours control (not significant) |
| study_4_R4 | **+5.686** | 4.701 to 6.671 | 80 | Favours estrogen (significant; extreme outlier) |
| study_5_R5 | +0.137 | −0.202 to 0.476 | 134 | Favours estrogen (not significant) |
| study_7_R7 | +0.435 | 0.169 to 0.700 | 223 | Favours estrogen (significant) |
| study_9_R9 | +0.169 | −0.310 to 0.649 | 67 | Favours estrogen (not significant) |

Several features of this distribution warrant emphasis before any pooled value is interpreted.

1. **The estimates are not merely variable in magnitude; they are variable in sign.** Three trials point in the direction of better sexual function with placebo/no treatment (g = −0.121, −0.240 and, significantly, −1.099) and four in the direction of better sexual function with estrogen (g = +0.137, +0.169, +0.435 and +5.686). Two trials produce confidence intervals that exclude the null in *opposite* directions (study_2_R2 and study_7_R7), a formal contradiction that cannot be reconciled by sampling error alone.

2. **One trial is a profound outlier.** study_4_R4 reports g = +5.686 (95% CI 4.701 to 6.671) in 80 participants. An effect of nearly six standard deviations is far outside the range credible for any menopausal pharmacotherapy on a patient-reported composite; for comparison, effects regarded as large in this literature are of the order of g = 0.5–0.8. Its lower confidence limit (4.701) lies more than four standard-deviation units above the upper confidence limit of every other trial. Such a value typically arises from one of: an unusually homogeneous (artificially small) within-arm SD, a dispersion statistic misreported or mis-transcribed as an SD when it was an SE or an interquartile measure, analysis of change scores with highly correlated repeated measures, a per-protocol or responder-restricted analysis, or an open-label design with unblinded self-report. Section 11 treats this explicitly as the dominant threat to the validity of the synthesis.

3. **Excluding the outlier, the evidence is tightly clustered and unimpressive.** The remaining six estimates span −1.099 to +0.435, with four of the six confidence intervals crossing zero. The largest trials — study_7_R7 (n = 223, g = +0.435), study_3_R3 (n = 182, g = −0.240) and study_1_R1 (n = 160, g = −0.121) — straddle the null and collectively suggest, at most, a small effect.

The distribution of effect sizes, including the isolation of the extreme positive value, is displayed in `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/effect_distribution.png`.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

A single meta-analytic group was specified (one composite sexual function outcome per trial at end of treatment); no separate outcome/timepoint subgroups were required, and no group was flagged as having insufficient data.

**Primary result (REML random-effects model, k = 7, n = 895):**

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | Standardized Mean Difference (SMD) |
| Pooled Standardized Mean Difference | **0.681** |
| 95% confidence interval | **−0.934 to 2.296** |
| p-value | **0.409** |
| Favours | **Estrogen alone (oral, transdermal or vaginal estradiol / conjugated estrogens, any dose)** |
| Number of trials (k) | 7 |
| Total participants | 895 |
| Estimation method | REML random-effects |
| Native pooling on the specified measure | Yes |

The pooled Standardized Mean Difference is **0.681**, and the direction field for this analysis records that the estimate **favours estrogen-alone therapy**. However, the confidence interval (−0.934 to 2.296) is extraordinarily wide — spanning nearly 3.2 standardized units — and comfortably includes zero; the associated p-value is **0.409**. The analysis therefore provides **no statistically significant evidence that estrogen-alone therapy improves overall sexual function relative to placebo or no treatment.** The interval is consistent with a large detriment (approaching one standard deviation worse with estrogen) as readily as with a very large benefit, and conveys essentially no usable information for clinical decision-making.

Because `native_pooling_used = true` and the measure was requested explicitly, this is a confirmed SMD-scale result rather than a standardized fallback; its weakness lies in imprecision and inconsistency, not in measure conversion.

The forest plot (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`) makes the structural problem visible: the diamond is centred to the right of the null yet overlaps it, and its position is determined largely by one study whose estimate lies off the scale of the others. The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows the running estimate lurching rather than converging as studies accumulate — the hallmark of an unstable synthesis in which no amount of additional weight from ordinary trials stabilises the summary.

An important corollary follows from Section 5: under a random-effects model with τ² of this magnitude, weights are strongly equalised across studies, so a single extreme trial of moderate size (n = 80) exerts leverage far beyond its sample contribution. The pooled 0.681 should be read as an arithmetic consequence of including study_4_R4, not as a consensus of the evidence. Six of seven trials individually exclude an effect as large as 0.681 from their confidence intervals.

## 7. Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity was extreme and is the defining feature of this dataset.

| Statistic | Value |
|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | 151.946 (df = 6) |
| p-value for Q | < 0.001 |
| I² | **99.23%** |
| τ² (between-study variance) | **4.6837** |
| τ (between-study SD) | ≈ 2.164 |

An I² of **99.23%** indicates that essentially all observed variability in effect estimates reflects genuine between-study differences rather than within-study sampling error. Cochran's Q of **151.946** on 6 degrees of freedom (p < 0.001, reported as q_p_value = 0.0) rejects homogeneity emphatically; the expected value of Q under homogeneity is 6, so the observed value is more than 25 times its null expectation.

The between-study variance τ² = **4.6837** corresponds to a between-study standard deviation of roughly 2.16 standardized units. Taking the pooled estimate together with τ², an approximate 95% prediction interval for the true effect in a new, exchangeable trial spans roughly **−5.0 to +6.4** standardized units. A prediction interval of that width is, for practical purposes, vacuous: it asserts that the next trial of estrogen-alone therapy could show an enormous benefit or an enormous harm. No clinically meaningful inference about the typical effect can be drawn from a distribution this dispersed. The heterogeneity summary figure is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/heterogeneity_summary.png`.

**Sources of the heterogeneity.** Three classes of explanation are plausible and, importantly, they are not mutually exclusive:

1. *A single aberrant estimate.* study_4_R4 (g = +5.686) is incompatible with the remaining six trials. Its inclusion alone can account for the bulk of Q and τ². The other six estimates, spanning −1.099 to +0.435, would still be heterogeneous — study_2_R2 and study_7_R7 conflict directionally — but on a scale that is routine in behavioural outcome research rather than pathological. A formal leave-one-out and outlier-adjusted re-analysis was not among the pre-specified computational outputs supplied for this report, so no adjusted pooled value is quoted here; its absence is itself a limitation (Section 11), and it is the single most important sensitivity analysis required before this body of evidence is cited.

2. *Clinical and methodological diversity.* The trials differ in estrogen formulation (estradiol vs conjugated estrogens), route (oral, transdermal, vaginal), dose, treatment duration, menopausal stage (perimenopausal vs postmenopausal; natural vs surgical), baseline sexual function, and partner availability. Vaginal estrogen would be expected to act strongly on dyspareunia-weighted outcomes and weakly on desire, whereas systemic estrogen may help indirectly through vasomotor relief while reducing free testosterone.

3. *Outcome-construct heterogeneity.* Pooling FSFI totals, McCoy totals, MENQoL sexual-domain scores (sign-reversed) and WHQ sexual functioning subscales treats structurally different instruments as measures of one latent construct. Differences in instrument responsiveness, score range, ceiling effects and item content translate directly into differences in standardized effect magnitude, and the mixing of end-of-treatment values with change-from-baseline contrasts adds a further layer of non-comparability (change-score SMDs are systematically larger when pre–post correlations are high).

Given I² = 99.23%, the fixed-effect model is inadmissible and even the random-effects summary should be regarded as a description of the *centre* of a hopelessly dispersed distribution rather than as an estimate of a common effect.

## 8. Moderators

With only **seven** effect sizes, formal moderator analysis is not statistically defensible. Conventional guidance requires roughly ten studies per covariate for meta-regression; here, a single covariate would be fitted to seven observations with τ² of 4.68, giving essentially no power to detect genuine effect modification and a high probability of spurious findings. No subgroup or meta-regression models were therefore estimated, and none should be read into the figures.

What can be said descriptively:

- **Sample size / precision.** The bubble chart (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`) plots effect size against study precision. There is no orderly monotone relation, but the two most extreme estimates in absolute magnitude (study_2_R2, g = −1.099, n = 49; study_4_R4, g = +5.686, n = 80) come from the two *smallest* samples, while the three largest trials (n = 160–223) yield estimates between −0.240 and +0.435. This pattern — dispersion shrinking as precision rises — is the classic signature of small-study effects and is corroborated by the formal tests in Section 9.
- **Route of administration** (systemic vs vaginal), **formulation** (estradiol vs conjugated estrogens), **dose**, **treatment duration**, **menopausal status** and **instrument type** (global sexual function questionnaire vs menopause-QoL sexual domain) are all a priori plausible moderators with strong mechanistic or psychometric justification. All remain untested here. Investigating them — particularly instrument type and route — should be a priority for any future update that assembles a larger trial set, ideally using individual participant data.
- **Outcome metric** (end-of-treatment value vs change from baseline) is a methodological moderator specific to this dataset that could not be examined with k = 7 but could plausibly account for part of the dispersion.

Any apparent association visible in the figures should be treated as hypothesis-generating only.

## 9. Publication Bias and Small-Study Effects

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | **−2.4431** |
| Egger's test p-value | **0.0049** |
| Trim-and-fill: studies imputed (k₀) | **2** |
| Trim-and-fill: adjusted estimate | **g = 1.2252** |
| Overall small-study / publication-bias risk | **High** |

Egger's regression test indicates **statistically significant funnel-plot asymmetry (p = 0.0049)**, consistent with small-study effects. Duval and Tweedie's trim-and-fill procedure imputed **two** hypothetically missing studies; the filled estimate was **g = 1.2252**, i.e. *larger* than the primary pooled estimate of 0.681.

This combination requires careful, non-mechanical interpretation.

- The standard narrative — that asymmetry reflects suppression of small negative trials and that correction should therefore shrink the effect — does **not** describe what happened here. Trim-and-fill imputed studies on the side that *increases* the estimate, because the observed asymmetry in this dataset is driven by small trials lying at both extremes, with the algorithm responding to the configuration of the funnel rather than to a simple one-sided censoring pattern. An "adjusted" value of 1.2252 should emphatically **not** be presented as a bias-corrected estimate of benefit; with I² = 99.23%, trim-and-fill is operating far outside the conditions under which it behaves sensibly, and both the primary and the filled estimates are better understood as artefacts of an outlier-dominated distribution.
- The analysis metadata nonetheless records the overall bias risk as **high**, and the accompanying interpretation notes that, combined with the overall high risk-of-bias rating, the meta-analytic summary should be treated as **an upper bound rather than an unbiased estimate**, with the adjusted (filled) effect reported alongside the primary result as a sensitivity analysis. We report it in that spirit while cautioning against reading it as a corrected effect.
- Important caveats apply in both directions. The imputation of only two studies means the magnitude of any adjustment is modest, so the discrepancy between a strongly significant Egger's test and a small trim-and-fill correction warrants scrutiny. Funnel asymmetry can arise not only from selective publication but also from between-study heterogeneity, systematic differences in methodological quality between small and large trials, or genuine effect modification by sample size (for instance, small single-centre open-label trials enrolling highly symptomatic women). With k = 7, Egger's test is also underpowered and its result is sensitive to the single extreme data point; conventional guidance discourages funnel-plot tests with fewer than ten studies.

**Practical conclusion.** Both the formal test and the precision pattern in the bubble chart point to small-study effects, and the two studies excluded at the data-extraction stage for lack of usable dispersion statistics (Section 3) add a further, unquantified selective-availability component. Confidence in the pooled effect should be downgraded for publication bias — for example, under GRADE — on top of the downgrades already warranted for inconsistency and imprecision.

## 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-handling and validation issues are reported plainly so that readers can calibrate their confidence in the synthesis:

- **Two of nine studies contributed no effect size.** Nine studies entered the analytical pipeline but only seven (`n_effect_sizes = 7`) yielded an extractable composite sexual function contrast. The two non-contributing studies lacked the arm-level mean, SD and n required for a single overall sexual function score at end of treatment. Their findings are consequently absent from every quantitative result in this report, and because availability of dispersion statistics is not random with respect to results, this is a potential source of selective-availability bias.
- **Heterogeneous outcome instruments pooled as one construct.** Effect sizes derived from global sexual function questionnaires (FSFI total, McCoy total) were pooled with sexual domains of broader menopause/QoL instruments (MENQoL sexual domain, WHQ sexual functioning subscale) under the pre-specified outcome hierarchy. These are not psychometrically equivalent, and some contrasts derive from bother-oriented scales that required **sign reversal** to align direction.
- **Mixed outcome metrics.** End-of-treatment values were used where available, but change-from-baseline contrasts (with the SD of change applied to both arms) were used where only change data were reported. Mixing value-based and change-based standardized effects is a known source of inflation and inconsistency.
- **Derived dispersion statistics.** Where trials reported SE or SEM, SDs were reconstructed as SE × √n. Any misreporting of a dispersion statistic in a primary report propagates directly and undetectably into the corresponding effect size and its weight.
- **Multi-arm trials collapsed to a single contrast.** Where trials randomised multiple estrogen doses, doses were pooled into one arm (pooled means and SDs, summed n) so that each trial contributes exactly one independent estimate and the shared control arm is not double-counted. Estrogen+progestogen and estrogen+testosterone arms were excluded by protocol, so this review speaks only to unopposed estrogen.
- **One extreme outlier dominates the synthesis.** study_4_R4 (g = +5.686, 95% CI 4.701 to 6.671) is biologically and psychometrically implausible for a patient-reported composite outcome and is incompatible with the other six trials. It is the principal driver of τ² = 4.6837 and I² = 99.23% and of the positive sign of the pooled estimate. No outlier-excluded sensitivity analysis was among the supplied computational outputs, so no outlier-adjusted pooled value is reported here.
- **Effect measure.** The SMD was specified explicitly by the review question, no measure conflict was detected, and pooling was performed natively on the SMD scale (`native_pooling_used = true`). The headline statistic is correctly named a **pooled Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)**; trial-level estimates are Hedges' g.
- **Overall risk-of-bias rating.** The body of evidence carries a high overall risk-of-bias rating, which, together with the significant Egger's test, underpins the recommendation to treat the pooled estimate as an upper bound.

## 11. Discussion

This meta-analysis of seven randomised comparisons (895 participants) found **no statistically significant effect of estrogen-alone therapy on overall sexual function** in perimenopausal and postmenopausal women. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was 0.681 (95% CI −0.934 to 2.296; p = 0.409), a value that nominally favours estrogen but whose confidence interval spans more than three standardized units and includes substantial harm as well as substantial benefit.

**The headline number should not be taken at face value.** Three considerations converge on this conclusion. First, the pooled estimate is driven by a single trial (study_4_R4, g = +5.686) whose effect is roughly thirteen times the size of the next largest positive estimate and far outside the plausible range for any menopausal therapy on a composite patient-reported outcome. Second, between-study inconsistency is near-total (I² = 99.23%, τ² = 4.6837), so the random-effects summary describes the centre of a distribution so dispersed that its prediction interval (approximately −5.0 to +6.4) is uninformative. Third, Egger's test is significantly asymmetric (p = 0.0049) and overall bias risk is rated high.

**What the more credible trials suggest.** Setting aside the outlier, the picture is one of, at most, a small and inconsistent effect. The three largest trials yielded g = +0.435 (significant, favouring estrogen), −0.240 and −0.121 (both non-significant); the two remaining non-outlier trials yielded +0.137 and +0.169 (non-significant), and one small trial yielded a significant negative effect (−1.099). A body of evidence in which the best-powered studies cluster near the null and the extreme values come from the smallest samples is most parsimoniously read as showing little or no overall benefit, with the apparent positive pooled value an artefact of outlier leverage under a high-τ² random-effects model.

**Consistency with the wider literature and guidance.** This conclusion aligns with prevailing clinical guidance, which supports estrogen — especially local vaginal estrogen — for genitourinary syndrome of menopause, vaginal dryness and dyspareunia, but does not endorse systemic estrogen as a treatment for low sexual desire or for sexual dysfunction considered globally. A composite score such as the FSFI total averages a dryness/pain component (where estrogen plausibly helps) with desire, arousal, orgasm and satisfaction components (where its effect is weak, absent, or potentially offset by SHBG-mediated reductions in free testosterone). Dilution of a genuine domain-specific benefit within a composite is a credible explanation for a near-null overall result and is a reason not to interpret these findings as evidence against the established genitourinary indications. It also means the present analysis cannot adjudicate whether vaginal estrogen improves dyspareunia — an outcome for which the direct evidence is considerably stronger than anything reported here.

**Mechanistic and measurement considerations.** Sexual function in midlife is multiply determined: relationship quality and partner availability, partner sexual function, mood, sleep, body image, comorbidity and medication (notably antidepressants) all contribute, and several of these are more powerful determinants than estradiol concentration. Against that background, even a real pharmacological effect may be small relative to the within-arm variability of composite instruments, and trials are frequently underpowered to detect it. In addition, hormone therapy trials are prone to unblinding through vasomotor relief and vaginal symptom change, which can inflate self-reported outcomes in the active arm.

**What would change the answer.** The decisive requirements are: (i) adjudication of study_4_R4 against its primary report, with re-analysis excluding it; (ii) stratification by route of administration and by outcome instrument; and (iii) separation of end-of-treatment from change-score contrasts. Until these are done, the present pooled value functions as little more than a demonstration that the available evidence is too inconsistent to support a quantitative summary.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Very small evidence base (k = 7).** Seven effect sizes from 895 participants leave the pooled estimate highly imprecise and preclude any credible moderator analysis.
2. **Extreme, unresolved heterogeneity.** I² = 99.23% and τ² = 4.6837 mean the model estimates no common effect in any practical sense. The prediction interval is uninformative.
3. **A dominant outlier that was not sensitivity-tested.** study_4_R4 (g = +5.686) determines the sign and magnitude of the pooled estimate. No leave-one-out or outlier-excluded re-analysis was available among the supplied outputs, so the robustness of the primary result to its removal could not be quantified — the single most consequential limitation of this report.
4. **High risk of small-study effects and publication bias.** Egger's p = 0.0049 with an overall high bias rating; trim-and-fill behaved counterintuitively (k₀ = 2, filled g = 1.2252) under extreme heterogeneity and should not be read as a bias-corrected effect.
5. **Incomplete outcome availability.** Two of nine candidate studies could not contribute data, introducing potential selective-availability bias.
6. **Construct heterogeneity across instruments.** FSFI totals, McCoy totals and sexual domains of menopause/QoL instruments were standardized onto a common scale; they index related but distinct constructs, with differing responsiveness and ceiling behaviour, and some required sign reversal.
7. **Mixed value and change-score contrasts.** Combining end-of-treatment and change-from-baseline standardized effects is a recognised source of inconsistency and potential inflation.
8. **Derived SDs.** SE→SD conversion propagates any primary-report misstatement of dispersion directly into effect sizes and weights.
9. **Pooling across heterogeneous interventions.** Oral, transdermal and vaginal estrogens at any dose were combined despite differing pharmacology and target symptoms; dose arms within trials were also collapsed, obscuring any dose–response relation.
10. **Composite outcome masks domain effects.** A single overall score cannot distinguish improvement in lubrication or pain from absence of effect on desire, so a clinically real but domain-restricted benefit could be rendered invisible.
11. **No formal risk-of-bias item-level synthesis or GRADE table** is presented here; the available rating is a summary judgement of high overall risk of bias.
12. **Unassessed blinding integrity and harms.** Functional unblinding is likely in hormone trials, and this review did not address safety outcomes, which are essential to any benefit–risk judgement.

---

## 13. Conclusion

In this random-effects meta-analysis of seven randomised comparisons involving 895 perimenopausal and postmenopausal women, estrogen-alone therapy (oral, transdermal or vaginal estradiol or conjugated estrogens, any dose) was **not** associated with a statistically significant improvement in overall sexual function relative to placebo or no treatment. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was **0.681 (95% CI −0.934 to 2.296; p = 0.409)**; per the analysis metadata this estimate **favours estrogen-alone therapy**, but it is non-significant, extraordinarily imprecise, and dominated by one implausible outlier.

Between-study inconsistency was essentially complete (Q = 151.946, df = 6, p < 0.001; I² = 99.23%; τ² = 4.6837), and significant funnel asymmetry (Egger's p = 0.0049) together with a high overall risk-of-bias rating means the summary should be regarded as an **upper bound rather than an unbiased estimate** of effect. Excluding the outlying trial, the remaining six estimates cluster between −1.10 and +0.43, with the largest and best-powered trials straddling the null.

**Certainty in the evidence is very low** — downgraded for risk of bias, serious inconsistency, serious imprecision and suspected publication bias. The appropriate clinical reading is that there is currently insufficient reliable evidence that unopposed estrogen meaningfully improves *global* sexual function in menopausal women. This does not contradict the established role of vaginal estrogen for genitourinary syndrome of menopause, vaginal dryness and dyspareunia, which this composite-outcome analysis was not designed to evaluate. Future work should prioritise adjudication and sensitivity analysis of the outlying trial, stratification by route of administration and outcome instrument, consistent reporting of arm-level means and SDs for validated composite instruments such as the FSFI, and individual participant data synthesis to separate domain-specific from global effects.
