# Digital or Technology-Assisted Interventions Added to Conventional Care for Waist Circumference in Children and Adolescents With Overweight or Obesity: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background:** Digital health, technology-driven, and technology-assisted strategies may augment conventional care for pediatric overweight and obesity. This review evaluated their effect on waist circumference at the end of intervention.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons involved participants aged 0–19 years with overweight or obesity, an intervention comprising digital or technology-assisted care plus conventional care, and conventional care alone. The outcome was endpoint waist circumference. The supplied evidence set contained three studies, but only two studies (97 participants) contributed quantitative effect estimates. Because a native mean difference in centimeters could not be calculated consistently, effects were pooled as a standardized fallback using Hedges’ g in a random-effects restricted maximum-likelihood model. By the prespecified direction convention, a favorable result indicates a smaller endpoint waist circumference with the intervention.

**Results:** The study-level effects were Hedges’ g = −0.173095 (95% CI −0.660 to 0.314; n = 65) and −0.565436 (95% CI −1.272 to 0.141; n = 32). The pooled Hedges’ g was −0.29949147830041156 (95% CI −0.701 to 0.102; p = 0.14333817800497473), favoring digital/technology-assisted intervention plus conventional care according to the supplied direction field, but the confidence interval included the null. Heterogeneity was not detected (Q = 0.803, p = 0.3703; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0; k = 2). The pooled value is a standardized fallback and is not a confirmed native-scale mean difference in centimeters.

**Conclusions:** The available evidence suggests a small, statistically non-significant effect favoring digital or technology-assisted intervention added to conventional care. The estimate is imprecise, rests on only two studies, and cannot be interpreted directly in centimeters. Additional adequately powered trials reporting standardized waist-measurement protocols and complete endpoint means and standard deviations are needed.

## Introduction

Childhood and adolescent overweight and obesity are associated with central adiposity and subsequent cardiometabolic risk. Waist circumference provides a clinically relevant indicator of abdominal adiposity, although values can depend on the anatomical measurement site and protocol. Digital health, technology-driven, and technology-assisted approaches may extend conventional care through remote monitoring, interactive education, feedback, communication, or other technology-enabled components.

This systematic review and meta-analysis addressed the following question: among children and adolescents aged 0–19 years with overweight or obesity, what is the effect at the end of the study of a digital health, technology-driven, or technology-assisted intervention delivered in addition to conventional care, compared with conventional care alone, on waist circumference measured in centimeters? The intended native effect measure was the between-group endpoint mean difference in centimeters, with a more favorable outcome defined as a smaller waist circumference in the intervention group.

## Search/Screening

### Eligibility framework

The prespecified eligibility framework was:

- **Population:** children and adolescents aged 0–19 years with overweight or obesity.
- **Intervention:** digital health, technology-driven, or technology-assisted intervention added to conventional care.
- **Comparator:** conventional care alone.
- **Outcome:** waist circumference at the end of the study, preferentially reported or converted to centimeters.
- **Measurement handling:** eligible waist-measurement instruments and standardized protocols were to be treated as measuring the same construct, while retaining measurement-site and protocol information when available.
- **Effect extraction:** adjusted estimates were preferred when appropriately reported; otherwise, endpoint means and standard deviations or other sufficient quantitative statistics were eligible. Similar intervention arms sharing a control were to be combined, or the shared control otherwise accounted for, to prevent double counting.

## Meta-Analytic Result(s)

A random-effects model fitted using restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled **two studies** and **97 participants**. The **pooled Hedges’ g was −0.29949147830041156** (95% CI **−0.701 to 0.102**; p = **0.14333817800497473**). According to the supplied `favors` classification, this result favored **digital health, technology-driven, or technology-assisted intervention plus conventional care**. Nevertheless, the confidence interval included zero and the result was not statistically significant at a two-sided 0.05 level.

The magnitude is consistent with a small standardized effect, but this interpretation should be cautious because of the small evidence base and unresolved native effect measure. In particular, the result does **not** establish the number of centimeters by which endpoint waist circumference differed between groups.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis found a small standardized endpoint effect favoring the addition of digital or technology-assisted intervention to conventional care for waist circumference in children and adolescents with overweight or obesity. The pooled Hedges’ g was −0.29949147830041156, but its 95% confidence interval ranged from −0.701 to 0.102 and included no effect. Thus, the available evidence does not provide statistically conclusive support for a benefit.

The direction of favor was taken from the supplied meta-analysis output rather than inferred solely from the negative numerical sign. This distinction is important because the raw convention was treatment minus control while a lower waist circumference is clinically preferable. Under the prespecified outcome direction, a smaller endpoint waist circumference in the intervention arm is favorable.

The findings have limited direct clinical interpretability. The research question concerns waist circumference in centimeters, but incomplete raw data prevented consistent pooling as a native mean difference. Hedges’ g expresses the contrast in pooled standard-deviation units rather than centimeters. Without the relevant study standard deviations or other conversion information, the pooled effect cannot be converted reliably to an absolute waist-circumference difference.

The estimated heterogeneity was zero, but this result must be interpreted in the context of only two studies. Lack of detectable statistical heterogeneity cannot compensate for missing information on intervention modality, conventional care, endpoint timing, and waist-measurement procedures. The evidence is therefore compatible with a modest intervention benefit, no meaningful difference, or—within the positive end of the reported confidence interval under the raw sign convention—an effect in the opposite direction.

For future trials, investigators should prespecify the anatomical site and standardized waist-measurement procedure, report endpoint and baseline means and standard deviations by randomized group, provide adjusted between-group estimates with standard errors or confidence intervals, and report intervention adherence and attrition. Harmonized reporting would enable synthesis in centimeters, which is more clinically interpretable than a standardized effect. Larger samples and sufficient numbers of trials are also needed to investigate whether effects differ by age, sex, baseline adiposity, technology type, intervention intensity, and follow-up duration.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

- The pipeline contained **three studies** but only **two effect sizes**; one study could not contribute to the pooled analysis, and no reason or quantitative outcome data were supplied.
- Native mean-difference pooling in centimeters was not possible because only **1 of 2** pooled rows had the required raw inputs; `study_3` was specifically identified as missing those inputs.
- The effect-measure source was classified as `unresolved_fallback`. The pooled statistic is therefore Hedges’ g, not a confirmed native-scale mean difference.
- Study citations, measurement sites and protocols, search-flow counts, and risk-of-bias assessments were absent, preventing independent validation of eligibility, outcome harmonization, and internal validity.
- There was no supplied information indicating that duplicate reports or shared controls affected the two pooled estimates; nevertheless, this could not be independently checked from study citations.

## Limitations

This review has several important limitations. First, only two studies and 97 participants contributed to the quantitative synthesis, producing an imprecise pooled estimate. Second, incomplete raw outcome data required standardized fallback pooling, so the result does not answer the clinical question on the centimeter scale. Third, one of the three studies in the pipeline was not quantitatively represented, with no stated reason, creating potential availability or reporting bias. Fourth, absent bibliographic and study-characteristic data prevented assessment of clinical comparability, measurement protocols, intervention fidelity, follow-up timing, and applicability. Fifth, no study-level risk-of-bias information was supplied, so certainty in the internal validity of the evidence cannot be established. Sixth, the search and screening methods were not provided, precluding assessment of search completeness or full PRISMA flow reporting. Finally, publication bias, moderators, and robust heterogeneity could not be assessed with only two studies.

No certainty-of-evidence rating using GRADE or a comparable framework was supplied or could be completed from the available data. Given imprecision, missing methodological information, and the unresolved effect scale, strong clinical conclusions are not warranted.

## Conclusion

Across two studies involving 97 participants, digital health, technology-driven, or technology-assisted intervention added to conventional care produced a pooled Hedges’ g of **−0.29949147830041156** (95% CI **−0.701 to 0.102**; p = **0.14333817800497473**), classified as favoring the intervention. No statistical heterogeneity was detected (I² = **0.0%**; τ² = **0.0**), but the very small number of studies limits this finding. Overall, the evidence suggests a small but statistically non-significant favorable effect. Because the analysis relied on an unresolved standardized fallback, it does not establish an absolute reduction in waist circumference measured in centimeters.
