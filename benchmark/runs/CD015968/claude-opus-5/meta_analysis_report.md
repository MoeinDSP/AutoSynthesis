# Digital Health and Technology-Assisted Interventions Added to Conventional Care for Waist Circumference in Children and Adolescents with Overweight or Obesity: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

**Background.** Central adiposity, indexed by waist circumference, is a clinically important treatment target in paediatric overweight and obesity because it tracks cardiometabolic risk more closely than body mass index alone. Digital health and technology-assisted components (mobile applications, text messaging, web platforms, wearables, telehealth, serious games, remote monitoring) are increasingly layered on top of conventional multidisciplinary weight-management care, but their incremental benefit on waist circumference is uncertain.

**Objective.** To quantify the effect of digital health / technology-driven or technology-assisted interventions added to conventional care, versus conventional care alone, on waist circumference (cm) at end of study in children and adolescents aged 0–19 years with overweight or obesity.

**Methods.** A systematic search and structured screening pipeline identified controlled studies comparing a technology-assisted intervention plus conventional care with conventional care alone and reporting waist circumference (including waist circumference z-score or percentile where that was the study's waist measure) at end of intervention. Three studies met the inclusion criteria for the review; two contributed extractable end-of-study waist circumference data suitable for quantitative synthesis. Effects were pooled using a random-effects model (restricted maximum likelihood, REML). Heterogeneity was assessed with Cochran's Q, I² and τ².

**Results.** Two studies (k = 2; N = 97 participants) were pooled. The planned native effect measure was the mean difference in centimetres, but raw mean/SD inputs were available for only one of the two contributing studies; the synthesis therefore fell back to a standardized (Hedges' g) scale. The pooled effect was Hedges' g = −0.299 (95% CI −0.701 to 0.102; p = 0.143), a small-to-moderate effect that the analysis engine records as favouring digital health / technology-assisted intervention plus conventional care. The confidence interval crossed the null, so the data are compatible with anything from a moderate benefit of the digital intervention to a small advantage for conventional care alone. Between-study heterogeneity was nil (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0000, Q = 0.803, df = 1, p = 0.3703), but with only two studies these statistics are highly imprecise. Individual study estimates were g = −0.173 (95% CI −0.660 to 0.314; n = 65) and g = −0.565 (95% CI −1.272 to 0.141; n = 32). Publication bias could not be assessed meaningfully with k = 2.

**Conclusions.** Current evidence is too sparse to determine whether adding digital health or technology-assisted components to conventional care reduces waist circumference in children and adolescents with overweight or obesity. The pooled point estimate is directionally consistent with a small-to-moderate benefit of the digital intervention but is statistically non-significant and imprecise, and the effect measure was a standardized fallback rather than a confirmed mean difference in centimetres. Adequately powered randomised trials that report waist circumference as a pre-specified outcome, with complete means and standard deviations, are required.

**Registration.** Not registered.


## 1. Introduction

Paediatric overweight and obesity remain among the most prevalent chronic conditions of childhood and adolescence worldwide, and they carry a well-documented burden of cardiometabolic, musculoskeletal and psychosocial sequelae that frequently persist into adult life. Within the anthropometric panel used to monitor these children, waist circumference occupies a distinctive position. Whereas body mass index (BMI) and BMI z-score summarise total body mass relative to height, waist circumference is a direct surface marker of central and visceral fat deposition, and it is central adiposity — rather than total adiposity — that is most tightly linked to insulin resistance, dyslipidaemia, hepatic steatosis and elevated blood pressure in young people. Waist circumference is therefore frequently nominated as a co-primary or key secondary endpoint in paediatric weight-management trials, and in some populations it changes detectably even when BMI z-score is comparatively static (for example, during linear growth, when fat redistribution may occur without a large change in weight-for-height).

Conventional care for paediatric overweight and obesity is typically a multicomponent, clinic-delivered package: dietary counselling, physical activity prescription, behavioural and family-based support, and periodic anthropometric review, usually delivered by a multidisciplinary team. Its effectiveness is constrained in practice by well-known implementation barriers — limited clinic contact time, travel and scheduling burden for families, attrition between visits, and the difficulty of sustaining behaviour change in the long intervals between appointments.

Digital health and technology-assisted strategies have been proposed as a way of extending the reach and intensity of conventional care without proportionally increasing clinic resources. The modality set is heterogeneous and includes smartphone applications for self-monitoring of diet and activity, SMS or instant-messaging prompts, web-based education and goal-setting portals, wearable activity trackers with feedback loops, exergames and serious games, telehealth or videoconference consultations, and remote clinician dashboards that permit between-visit titration of advice. The theoretical rationale is coherent: these tools can increase the frequency of self-monitoring, deliver just-in-time behavioural prompts, extend therapeutic contact into the home environment, and engage a developmental cohort that is already heavily technology-native.

Whether this translates into measurable change in central adiposity is a separate empirical question. Several systematic reviews of digital interventions in paediatric obesity have focused predominantly on BMI or BMI z-score, where the evidence base is larger; waist circumference is reported inconsistently, often as an unplanned secondary outcome, and frequently without the complete summary statistics required for quantitative synthesis. The present review addresses this specific gap by asking a narrow, additive question: in children and adolescents aged 0–19 years with overweight or obesity, does adding a digital health / technology-driven or technology-assisted component to conventional care, compared with conventional care alone, change waist circumference (cm) at the end of the intervention or study period?

## 2. Search and Screening

### 2.1 Reporting standard

This review is reported in accordance with the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA 2020) statement. The review was not prospectively registered, which is acknowledged as a limitation (Section 11).

### 2.2 Eligibility criteria

Eligibility was defined using a PICOS framework:

| Element | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Children and adolescents aged 0–19 years with overweight or obesity, defined by any recognised criterion (IOTF, WHO, CDC, or national BMI percentile/z-score cut-offs) |
| **Intervention** | A digital health / technology-driven or technology-assisted component (mobile app, SMS/messaging, web platform, wearable or activity tracker, telehealth/videoconsultation, exergame or serious game, remote monitoring, or combination) delivered **in addition to** conventional care |
| **Comparator** | Conventional care alone (standard multidisciplinary or usual clinical weight-management care, without the digital component) |
| **Outcome** | Waist circumference measured in centimetres at end of intervention/study, including waist circumference z-score or percentile where this was the study's reported waist measure |
| **Study design** | Controlled comparative designs (randomised controlled trials and controlled non-randomised comparative studies) with a concurrent conventional-care-alone arm |

Exclusions were: studies in adults or in unselected (non-overweight) paediatric populations; interventions in which technology constituted the entire comparison (i.e. no conventional-care-alone arm, or digital-versus-digital comparisons); studies not reporting any waist measure at end of study; reviews, editorials, protocols, conference abstracts without extractable data; and duplicate reports of the same trial cohort.

### 2.3 Information sources and search strategy

Records were retrieved and processed through a structured systematic-review pipeline combining bibliographic database retrieval with automated deduplication, document-type classification and outcome-level extraction. The search concept blocks combined: (i) population terms (*child\*, adolescen\*, p[a]ediatric, youth, overweight, obes\*, adiposity*); (ii) intervention terms (*digital health, mHealth, eHealth, telehealth, telemedicine, smartphone, mobile app\*, text messag\*, SMS, web-based, internet-based, wearable, activity tracker, accelerometer feedback, exergam\*, serious game, remote monitoring, technology-assisted*); and (iii) outcome terms (*waist circumference, central adiposity, abdominal obesity, waist-to-height*). Comparator terms (*usual care, standard care, conventional care, control*) were used to refine rather than restrict retrieval. Reference lists of retrieved reviews were hand-searched for additional eligible primary studies.

### 2.4 Selection process

Records were screened in two stages — title/abstract, then full text — against the criteria in Section 2.2. Document-type filters removed secondary literature (reviews, meta-analyses, commentaries) and duplicate publications of the same cohort. At the full-text stage, each candidate study was further assessed for (a) the presence of a genuine *additive* design (digital + conventional care vs conventional care alone) and (b) the availability of end-of-study waist circumference data in a form amenable to effect-size computation.

### 2.5 Results of screening

The screening pipeline yielded **3 studies** meeting the full eligibility criteria for this review question. Of these, **2 studies** provided end-of-study waist circumference data in a form that permitted computation of a comparative effect size and were carried forward into the quantitative synthesis; the third eligible study reported a waist measure descriptively but did not supply the paired group-level summary statistics (or an equivalent reported contrast) needed to derive a comparable effect estimate, and so contributed to the narrative synthesis only.

## 3. Included Studies

### 3.1 Overview

Three studies were eligible for the review; two contributed to the meta-analysis. All contributing studies compared a technology-assisted package delivered on top of conventional multidisciplinary weight management against conventional care alone in children or adolescents with overweight or obesity, and reported waist circumference at the end of the intervention/study period.

**Table 1. Studies contributing effect sizes to the quantitative synthesis**

| Study ID | Effect-size label | Comparison | Outcome | Total n | Hedges' g | 95% CI | Analysis weight |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Study 1 | study_1_R1 | Digital health + conventional care vs conventional care alone | Waist circumference, end of study | 65 | −0.173 | −0.660 to 0.314 | ≈67.8% |
| Study 3 | study_3_R3 | Digital health + conventional care vs conventional care alone | Waist circumference, end of study | 32 | −0.565 | −1.272 to 0.141 | ≈32.2% |
| **Pooled** | — | — | — | **97** | **−0.299** | **−0.701 to 0.102** | 100% |

Analysis weights are inverse-variance random-effects weights derived from the reported confidence intervals; because τ² estimated to zero, the random-effects and fixed-effect weighting schemes coincide in this analysis.

### 3.2 Study-level characteristics

Both pooled studies were small: 65 and 32 participants respectively, for a combined analysed sample of 97. Neither study, taken individually, was powered to detect a small-to-moderate difference in waist circumference; an individually powered two-arm trial targeting g ≈ 0.30 at 80% power and α = 0.05 would require approximately 350 participants, roughly 3.6 times the entire pooled sample assembled here. This disparity between available and required sample size is the single most consequential feature of the evidence base and frames the interpretation of every result that follows.

Both contributing comparisons were additive in design — that is, the comparator arm received the same conventional care as the intervention arm, with the digital component constituting the only planned between-arm difference. This is the design most directly informative for the research question, because it isolates the incremental contribution of the technology rather than confounding it with differences in the underlying care package.

### 3.3 The non-pooled eligible study

One of the three eligible studies could not be included in the quantitative synthesis. It satisfied the population, intervention and comparator criteria and reported a waist measure, but the available report did not provide the end-of-study group means with standard deviations (or a reported mean difference with a dispersion estimate) required to compute a comparable standardized effect. Its exclusion from pooling is a data-availability exclusion, not a judgement about study quality or relevance, and it means that the quantitative result rests on two-thirds of the eligible literature identified.

### 3.4 Risk of bias considerations

Formal domain-level risk-of-bias scoring was not completed within this synthesis, and no study-level risk-of-bias ratings are available to report. Several generic bias concerns nonetheless apply structurally to this literature and should be assumed to be operative:

- **Blinding of participants and personnel** is effectively impossible in trials of digital interventions, since participants necessarily know whether they have been given an app, a wearable or a messaging programme. Performance bias is therefore intrinsic to the design.
- **Outcome measurement.** Waist circumference is a tape-measure outcome that is sensitive to technique, measurement landmark (iliac crest vs narrowest point vs umbilicus), clothing, respiratory phase and observer. Unless assessors were blinded and protocolised, both random measurement error (which widens confidence intervals and attenuates effects) and directional detection bias are plausible.
- **Attrition.** Digital interventions in adolescents are characterised by engagement decay, and differential dropout between arms can bias end-of-study anthropometric comparisons in either direction.
- **Outcome reporting.** Waist circumference is frequently a secondary outcome in this literature. The fact that one of three eligible studies reported it without poolable statistics is itself an indicator of inconsistent, possibly selective, outcome reporting for this endpoint.

In the absence of formal ratings, the overall certainty of the body of evidence for this outcome should be regarded as **low to very low**, downgraded principally for imprecision (very small total sample, confidence interval spanning both benefit and harm), with additional concern for risk of bias and indirectness arising from the heterogeneous definition of "digital health" across modalities.

## 4. Effect Sizes

### 4.1 Effect measure: planned, obtained, and the fallback that was applied

This point requires explicit statement because it materially affects how the headline number should be read.

The **planned native effect measure** for this outcome was the **mean difference (MD) in centimetres** — the natural and clinically interpretable scale for waist circumference. Native-scale pooling was attempted but **could not be completed**. Of the two rows contributing to the synthesis, only **1 of 2** carried the raw inputs (group means, standard deviations and sample sizes) required to compute a mean difference; the inputs for Study 3 were missing. The synthesis engine therefore fell back to pooling on the **standardized mean difference scale, reported as Hedges' g**, for the whole group.

Accordingly:

- The effect measure reported in this analysis is labelled **Hedges' g**, and that is how every pooled and study-level value in this report is named.
- **`native_pooling_used` is FALSE.** The effect measure was **not confirmed** on its native (centimetre) scale. The pooled value is a **standardized fallback**, not a confirmed native-scale mean difference in centimetres.
- The effect-measure source is recorded as an **unresolved fallback** arising from a measure conflict, not as a resolved, verified native-scale pooling.

Practically, this means the result **cannot be read as "X centimetres of additional waist reduction."** It can only be read as a standardized, unit-free difference, and back-conversion to centimetres would require pooled standard deviations that were not available for one of the two studies. Readers seeking a clinically actionable magnitude in centimetres will not find one here, and should not infer one.

### 4.2 Sign convention and direction of benefit

The pooled and study-level estimates retain the raw statistical sign convention (treatment minus control). For waist circumference, a **lower** value is the clinically better outcome, so the raw sign cannot be read naively as "negative = worse." The direction of benefit reported here is taken **from the analysis engine's `favors` field**, which records that the pooled estimate **favours the digital health / technology-assisted intervention plus conventional care** over conventional care alone. Both individual study estimates (−0.173 and −0.565) lie on the same side of the null and are directionally consistent with that pooled finding.

### 4.3 Individual study effects

**Study 1 (study_1_R1):** Hedges' g = **−0.173** (95% CI −0.660 to 0.314), n = 65. This is a small effect whose confidence interval is comfortably compatible with no difference at all; the interval spans roughly from a moderate benefit to a small effect in the opposite direction. Standard error ≈ 0.249.

**Study 3 (study_3_R3):** Hedges' g = **−0.565** (95% CI −1.272 to 0.141), n = 32. This is a moderate-to-large point estimate but accompanied by a very wide interval reflecting the small sample (standard error ≈ 0.360); it too crosses the null, though it is substantially more consistent with benefit than with no effect.

Neither study was individually statistically significant. Notably, the smaller study produced the larger point estimate — a pattern that in larger literatures is often the signature of small-study effects, but which with k = 2 is entirely consistent with chance and cannot be evaluated formally.

## 5. Meta-Analytic Result

### 5.1 Model

A random-effects meta-analysis was fitted using **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of the between-study variance component, with inverse-variance weighting of study-level standardized effects. A random-effects specification was retained a priori because the intervention category ("digital health / technology-assisted") spans heterogeneous modalities and delivery intensities, so a common true effect could not reasonably be assumed — even though, as it happens, the fitted between-study variance was zero.

The context data contain a **single** meta-analytic group for this outcome and timepoint (`meta_analysis_groups` with multiple entries was not present); all pooled statistics below therefore refer to one synthesis of end-of-study waist circumference.

The pooled effect is **Hedges' g = −0.299 (95% CI −0.701 to 0.102; p = 0.143)**. By conventional benchmarks this is a **small-to-moderate** magnitude. The analysis engine records the direction as **favouring the digital health / technology-assisted intervention plus conventional care**.

The result is **not statistically significant at α = 0.05**. The 95% confidence interval crosses the null, and its two limits carry materially different clinical implications: the lower bound (−0.701) corresponds to a moderate-to-substantial advantage for the digital intervention, while the upper bound (+0.102) corresponds to a small advantage for conventional care alone. The data are therefore **compatible with anything from a moderate benefit of the digital intervention to a small advantage for conventional care alone**, and no directional conclusion can be asserted with confidence.

The forest plot for this synthesis — showing both study-level estimates with confidence intervals, their relative weights, and the pooled diamond — is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`.

### 5.3 Cumulative and sensitivity perspective

A cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) was generated, but with only two contributing studies it carries essentially no information about the stability of the estimate over accumulating evidence: it consists of a single-study estimate followed by the two-study pool.

Leave-one-out sensitivity analysis is formally degenerate with k = 2, since removing either study leaves a single uncombined estimate. Nevertheless, the arithmetic is instructive about fragility. Removing Study 3 leaves g = −0.173 (95% CI −0.660 to 0.314); removing Study 1 leaves g = −0.565 (95% CI −1.272 to 0.141). The pooled point estimate thus moves by a factor of more than three depending on which single study is retained, and in neither case does the interval exclude the null. **The synthesis is maximally fragile: it has no redundancy, and every conclusion depends on both of its two constituent studies.**

## 6. Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was quantified as **I² = 0.0%** with **τ² = 0.0000**, and Cochran's **Q = 0.803 on 1 degree of freedom (p = 0.3703)**. Taken at face value, these statistics indicate **fully consistent estimates** across the two contributing studies, with no detectable dispersion beyond sampling error, and they explain why the random-effects (REML) solution collapses onto the fixed-effect solution.

This apparent homogeneity must, however, be interpreted with considerable caution. With **k = 2**, heterogeneity statistics are **highly imprecise and should not be interpreted as firm evidence of homogeneity**:

1. **Q has one degree of freedom** and very low power to detect real heterogeneity. A non-significant Q with df = 1 is close to uninformative; failure to reject homogeneity is not evidence for it.
2. **I² is a ratio with a boundary at zero.** With two studies and wide individual confidence intervals, I² is routinely estimated at exactly 0% even when substantial true heterogeneity exists, simply because within-study variance dominates the decomposition.
3. **τ² estimated by REML with k = 2** has an extremely wide sampling distribution and is frequently pinned to the zero boundary. A τ² of 0.0000 here should be read as "not estimable with any useful precision," not as "demonstrated to be zero."
4. **A prediction interval is not reportable.** With τ² = 0 and df = 1, a prediction interval for the effect in a future setting would be either identical to the confidence interval (misleadingly narrow) or undefined; it is therefore not presented.

It is also worth observing that the two point estimates (−0.173 and −0.565) differ by roughly 0.39 standardized units — a difference that would be clinically meaningful if real, but which falls comfortably within the overlapping confidence intervals of two small studies and so generates no statistical signal of heterogeneity.

## 7. Moderators

**No moderator or subgroup analyses were conducted, and none could legitimately be conducted.**

Meta-regression requires, at an absolute minimum, substantially more studies than covariates; common guidance suggests roughly ten studies per moderator examined. With k = 2, any meta-regression would be saturated or over-parameterised, and any subgroup comparison would reduce to a one-study-versus-one-study contrast with no inferential value. Furthermore, τ² estimated to zero, so there is no between-study variance for a moderator to explain.

Moderators that would be of substantive interest in a larger evidence base, and which future reviews should pre-specify, include:

- **Digital modality** (mobile application vs SMS/messaging vs wearable with feedback vs telehealth consultation vs exergame/serious game vs multi-component digital package);
- **Intervention duration and dose** (weeks of exposure; frequency of prompts or contacts);
- **Engagement/adherence** with the digital component, which is the most plausible mediator of any effect and is seldom reported quantitatively;
- **Age band** (pre-pubertal children vs adolescents — developmentally very different in both autonomy over diet/activity and relationship to technology);
- **Baseline adiposity severity** (overweight vs obesity vs severe obesity);
- **Degree of parental/caregiver involvement** in the digital component;
- **Intensity of the underlying conventional care**, since a more intensive usual-care backbone compresses the scope for incremental digital benefit;
- **Waist measurement protocol** (anatomical landmark, assessor blinding), which bears directly on measurement error.

The bubble chart at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png` displays effect magnitude against study precision/size but, with two data points, should be treated as descriptive only and must not be read as indicating any trend.

## 8. Publication Bias

**Publication bias could not be assessed.**

With **k = 2**, all standard diagnostics are inapplicable:

- **Funnel plot asymmetry** cannot be visually or statistically evaluated with two points; no funnel plot is reported.
- **Egger's regression test** requires a minimum of roughly 10 studies for adequate power and is undefined in any useful sense with k = 2.
- **Begg's rank correlation test** is similarly uninformative.
- **Trim-and-fill** cannot impute missing studies from a two-point distribution.
- **p-curve / selection models** require a far larger set of estimates.

Qualitative considerations nonetheless warrant mention. The small-study-larger-effect pattern noted in Section 4.3 (the n = 32 study yielding g = −0.565 against the n = 65 study's g = −0.173) is the direction one would expect if small-study effects or selective reporting were operating, although with two studies it is equally consistent with ordinary sampling variation. More importantly, there is **direct evidence of incomplete outcome reporting within this literature**: of three eligible studies, one reported a waist measure without the summary statistics needed for pooling, and a second lacked the raw mean/SD inputs needed for native centimetre-scale pooling. Waist circumference is commonly a secondary endpoint in paediatric digital-health trials, and secondary endpoints are precisely where selective and incomplete reporting concentrate. The risk that the pooled estimate here is not representative of all waist circumference data actually collected in this field should be regarded as **non-trivial and unquantifiable**.

## 9. Discussion

### 9.1 Principal findings

Across two small controlled studies comprising 97 children and adolescents with overweight or obesity, adding a digital health or technology-assisted component to conventional care produced a pooled standardized effect on end-of-study waist circumference of **Hedges' g = −0.299 (95% CI −0.701 to 0.102; p = 0.143)**, an estimate the analysis records as **favouring the digital health / technology-assisted intervention plus conventional care**. Both individual studies pointed in the same direction, and between-study heterogeneity was nil (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0000). However, the confidence interval crossed the null, the result was not statistically significant, and the entire synthesis rests on two studies with a combined sample roughly one-third of what a single adequately powered trial would require.

The honest summary is therefore: **a directionally consistent, small-to-moderate point estimate favouring the digital intervention, embedded in an evidence base too sparse to support any firm conclusion.** The appropriate inference is uncertainty, not benefit.

### 9.2 Interpreting a non-significant small-to-moderate estimate

It is important not to over-read either the point estimate or the p-value.

Over-reading the point estimate (g ≈ −0.30 "shows digital health works") ignores that the interval extends to +0.102 — a small advantage for conventional care alone — and that the estimate more than triples or thirds depending on which of the two studies is removed.

Over-reading the p-value ("p = 0.143, therefore no effect") commits the opposite error. A non-significant result in an analysis with ~0.2 standard-error precision and 97 participants is a statement about *power*, not about *effect*. The interval excludes only large effects in the direction of conventional care; a clinically meaningful benefit of the digital intervention remains well within the range of values the data support. This analysis is best described as **inconclusive**, and it would be a misreading of it to cite this work as evidence that digital adjuncts do not affect central adiposity.

### 9.3 The measurement-scale problem

A distinctive and consequential finding of this review is methodological rather than clinical. Waist circumference is measured in centimetres — a scale that clinicians and families understand intuitively, and for which meaningful thresholds exist. Yet native-scale (mean difference, cm) pooling was impossible here because only one of two contributing studies reported the raw means and standard deviations required. The synthesis consequently had to default to a standardized metric.

This has two costs. First, it removes clinical interpretability: a standardized g of −0.299 conveys nothing directly about centimetres of waist reduction, and cannot responsibly be converted into centimetres without the pooled standard deviations that were unavailable. Second, standardization introduces its own distortion, because standardized effects depend on each study's sample variance, which in small samples is itself poorly estimated and is sensitive to population restriction (a trial recruiting a narrow BMI band will inflate g relative to a trial recruiting broadly, for identical centimetre effects).

The remedy is straightforward and entirely within the control of trialists: **report end-of-study waist circumference as group means with standard deviations (or mean change with SD of change, plus the correlation or sufficient detail to derive it), for every arm, in the primary publication.** Reporting only p-values, only percentage changes, or only graphical summaries renders an outcome unusable for synthesis — which, as this review illustrates, effectively removes a study from the evidence base.

### 9.4 Context within the wider literature

Reviews of digital health interventions in paediatric obesity have generally reported modest effects on BMI z-score, with effect sizes typically in the small range and considerable heterogeneity across modalities. The present finding — a small-to-moderate but non-significant point estimate on central adiposity — is not inconsistent with that broader picture, but it also cannot be said to corroborate it, given the k = 2 evidence base.

Two features of the additive design deserve emphasis when comparing this result with the wider literature. First, because the comparator receives full conventional care, the estimand here is the *incremental* value of the digital layer, not the total value of treatment. Incremental effects are, by construction, smaller and harder to detect than treatment-versus-nothing effects, and the more effective the conventional-care backbone, the smaller the detectable increment. Second, digital adjuncts plausibly act partly through sustaining engagement between clinic visits; if so, their effect may depend heavily on adherence to the digital component, which is rarely reported and was not available as a moderator here.

### 9.5 Clinical implications

For clinicians, the practical implication is that **this evidence does not currently justify a recommendation either for or against adding digital health components to conventional paediatric weight management on the basis of waist circumference outcomes.** Decisions about deploying apps, wearables or messaging programmes in this setting should rest on other grounds — effects on BMI and BMI z-score (a larger literature), engagement, acceptability, equity of access, cost, and safety — rather than on central-adiposity evidence of this density. Where such tools are deployed, waist circumference should be measured to a standardised protocol and reported, so that the evidence base can grow.

### 9.6 Research implications

Priorities for future research follow directly:

1. **Adequately powered randomised trials.** Powering for a small-to-moderate standardized effect (g ≈ 0.30) at 80% power requires on the order of 350 participants — far beyond any individual study here.
2. **Waist circumference as a pre-specified outcome,** with a documented measurement protocol (landmark, respiratory phase, number of repeats) and blinded assessment where feasible.
3. **Complete summary statistics in centimetres** for both arms, enabling native-scale mean-difference pooling.
4. **Reporting of digital engagement metrics** (logins, days of self-monitoring, message response rates) to permit dose–response and mediator analysis.
5. **Modality-specific trials or factorial designs,** so that "digital health" is disaggregated into interventions with distinct mechanisms rather than treated as a single category.
6. **Longer follow-up beyond end of intervention,** to establish whether any effect on central adiposity persists after the digital support is withdrawn.
7. **Prospective registration and full outcome reporting,** to reduce the selective-reporting risk evidenced in this review.

## 10. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-quality issues arose during extraction and synthesis and are reported plainly, as they bear directly on how the results should be read:

- **Effect measure could not be confirmed on its native scale.** The planned effect measure for this outcome was the **mean difference (MD) in centimetres**. Only **1 of the 2** rows entering the synthesis carried the raw inputs required for MD computation; the inputs for **Study 3 were missing**. The analysis therefore **fell back to Hedges' g pooling for the whole group**. The effect-measure source is recorded as an **`unresolved_fallback`**, and **`native_pooling_used` is FALSE**. The headline value of −0.299 is a **standardized fallback estimate, not a confirmed mean difference in centimetres**, and must not be reported or cited as centimetres of waist change.

- **One eligible study was excluded from pooling for insufficient data.** Three studies met the review's eligibility criteria, but only **2 contributed effect sizes**. The third reported a waist measure without the group-level summary statistics needed to compute a comparable effect, and so informs the narrative synthesis only. This is a data-availability exclusion, not a quality judgement.

- **The pooled analysis is based on k = 2.** This is below the threshold at which heterogeneity statistics (I², τ², Q), prediction intervals, publication-bias diagnostics, leave-one-out sensitivity analysis, and any moderator or subgroup analysis can be meaningfully computed. The reported I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0000 and Q = 0.803 (p = 0.3703) are presented for completeness but are **highly imprecise and must not be read as evidence of genuine homogeneity**.

- **Raw sign convention retained.** Pooled and study-level values keep the treatment-minus-control sign convention. Because a *lower* waist circumference is the better outcome, the sign alone does not indicate which arm is favoured. The direction reported throughout — **favouring the digital health / technology-assisted intervention plus conventional care** — is taken from the analysis engine's `favors` field.

- **Secondary literature and duplicate cohort reports were removed during screening** (reviews, meta-analyses, commentaries, protocols, and multiple publications of the same trial cohort) to avoid double-counting participants in the pooled estimate.

- **No formal risk-of-bias ratings are available** for the included studies within this synthesis; the certainty assessment in Section 3.4 is therefore structural and qualitative rather than domain-scored.

---

## 11. Limitations

1. **Extremely small number of studies (k = 2).** This is the dominant limitation. It precludes heterogeneity interpretation, moderator analysis, publication-bias assessment and meaningful sensitivity analysis, and it makes the pooled estimate maximally fragile — removal of either study changes the point estimate by more than a factor of three.

2. **Very small total sample (N = 97).** The analysis is substantially underpowered for the magnitude of effect observed; the non-significant result reflects limited precision at least as much as any absence of effect.

3. **Effect measure not confirmed on the native centimetre scale.** As detailed in Section 10, the result is a standardized fallback. Clinical interpretability in centimetres is lost, and standardization introduces variance-dependent distortion.

4. **Incomplete capture of eligible evidence.** One of three eligible studies could not be pooled for want of summary statistics, so the quantitative result does not represent the full identified literature.

5. **Intervention heterogeneity masked by a single category label.** "Digital health / technology-assisted" subsumes mechanistically distinct modalities (apps, wearables, messaging, telehealth, games). With k = 2 these cannot be separated, so the pooled estimate averages over an ill-defined intervention class, creating indirectness.

6. **Comparator heterogeneity.** "Conventional care" varies across settings in intensity, frequency and discipline mix; because the estimand is incremental, variation in the comparator directly scales the achievable effect.

7. **Outcome measurement error.** Waist circumference is operator- and protocol-dependent. Without documented standardised protocols and blinded assessors, non-differential error would attenuate effects while differential error could bias them in either direction.

8. **No formal risk-of-bias or GRADE assessment** was completed, limiting the formality of the certainty judgement; blinding of participants is in any case structurally impossible in this intervention class.

9. **No prospective registration** of the review protocol, raising the possibility of post hoc decisions in eligibility or analysis.

10. **Restriction to end-of-intervention timepoints.** Durability after withdrawal of digital support is not addressed, and no multi-timepoint group was available for separate synthesis.

11. **Potential language, publication and grey-literature restrictions** inherent in the retrieval strategy, which with a two-study base could disproportionately affect the pooled estimate.

---

## 12. Conclusion

In children and adolescents aged 0–19 years with overweight or obesity, pooling of two small controlled studies (N = 97) produced a **small-to-moderate standardized effect of Hedges' g = −0.299 (95% CI −0.701 to 0.102; p = 0.143)** on waist circumference at end of study, which the analysis records as **favouring digital health / technology-assisted intervention plus conventional care** over conventional care alone. Between-study heterogeneity was nil (I² = 0.0%, τ² = 0.0000, Q = 0.803, df = 1, p = 0.3703), though with only two studies these statistics are uninformative about true consistency.

Two caveats must travel with this number. First, it is **not statistically significant**, and its confidence interval is compatible with anything from a moderate benefit of the digital intervention to a small advantage for conventional care alone. Second, it is **not a confirmed native-scale result**: the planned mean difference in centimetres could not be computed because raw inputs were missing for one of the two studies, so the value is a **standardized fallback** and cannot be interpreted as centimetres of waist reduction.

**The evidence base for this outcome is too sparse to support firm conclusions in either direction.** The present synthesis should be read as a precise characterisation of current uncertainty rather than as an estimate of effect. Adequately powered randomised trials that pre-specify waist circumference, measure it to a standardised blinded protocol, and report complete group means and standard deviations in centimetres are required before the incremental value of digital health adjuncts for central adiposity in paediatric overweight and obesity can be established.
