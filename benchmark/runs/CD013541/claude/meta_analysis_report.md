# Family-Based Interventions and Family Caregiver Burden in Schizophrenia and Related Psychoses: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Immediate Post-Intervention Outcomes

## Abstract

**Background.** Relatives of people with schizophrenia and related psychoses frequently carry a substantial objective and subjective caregiving load. Structured family-focused programmes — psychoeducation, relative support groups, family therapy and similar interventions — are widely recommended, but their effect on *caregiver* burden (as distinct from patient relapse) at the point of programme completion has been quantified less often.

**Objective.** To estimate the effect of family-based interventions plus standard care, compared with standard care alone, on family caregiver burden measured at the first assessment occurring within one month of the end of the intervention.

**Methods.** Randomised comparisons of a structured relative-focused programme versus treatment as usual in families of people with schizophrenia or related psychosis were eligible. The outcome was total caregiver burden on a validated instrument (e.g. Family Burden Interview Schedule, Zarit Burden Interview, Burden Assessment Scale, Involvement Evaluation Questionnaire), one measure per trial, scored so that higher = more burden, taken from the earliest post-intervention assessment ≤ 1 month after the end of treatment. Standard errors were converted to standard deviations (SD = SE × √n); instruments oriented in the opposite direction were reverse-scored. Random-effects pooling used restricted maximum likelihood (REML). The pre-specified effect measure was the Mean Difference (MD). Heterogeneity was summarised with Q, I² and τ²; small-study effects were examined with Egger's regression and trim-and-fill.

**Results.** Seven records entered data extraction and six randomised comparisons (n = 377 family caregivers) contributed usable end-of-intervention burden data. The pooled Mean Difference was **−4.481 (95% CI −6.301 to −2.660; p = 1.41 × 10⁻⁶; k = 6; REML)**, favouring the **family-based intervention plus standard care** arm — that is, lower burden scores in families receiving a structured programme. Study-level standardised effects ranged from g = +0.156 to g = −1.979, with five of six point estimates in the direction of reduced burden. Heterogeneity was moderate and statistically significant (Q = 12.672, df = 5, p = 0.0267; I² = 55.53%; τ² = 2.677232). There was no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry (Egger's intercept = −0.3567, p = 0.917) and trim-and-fill imputed no missing studies (k₀ = 0); publication-bias risk was rated low.

**Conclusions.** Among families of people with schizophrenia and related psychoses, structured family-based interventions produced a statistically significant and clinically appreciable reduction in caregiver burden at the end of the programme relative to standard care. The pooled estimate is, however, a Mean Difference computed across trials that did not all use the same burden instrument or report a common scale range, so its numerical magnitude should be read as indicative rather than as a single interpretable point on one questionnaire. With only six trials, moderate heterogeneity and one very large single-trial effect, the direction of benefit is more secure than its precise size, and durability beyond one month was not assessed here.

## 1. Introduction

Schizophrenia and related psychotic disorders are typically managed in community settings, and in most health systems the practical and emotional work of day-to-day support falls on relatives. Caregivers commonly report supervision demands, disrupted employment and household routines, financial strain, restricted social life, grief, stigma-by-association and worry about the future — a cluster usually operationalised as *family* or *caregiver burden*, and measured with instruments such as the Family Burden Interview Schedule (FBIS), the Zarit Burden Interview (ZBI), the Burden Assessment Scale (BAS) and the Involvement Evaluation Questionnaire (IEQ).

Family-based interventions — structured psychoeducation, multi-family or single-family therapy, relative support groups, communication and problem-solving training — have a long and well-replicated evidence base for *patient* outcomes, particularly relapse and rehospitalisation. Their effect on the relatives themselves is a logically distinct question. A programme can reduce relapse without relieving the caregiver, and conversely can relieve the caregiver through information, normalisation, peer contact and coping skills even when patient-level outcomes are unchanged. Clinical guidelines increasingly frame family intervention as something offered partly *for* the family, which makes caregiver burden an outcome in its own right rather than a secondary byproduct.

This review addresses a deliberately narrow and well-defined question: the **immediate** post-intervention effect. We pooled caregiver burden measured at the first assessment within one month of the end of the intervention, excluding later follow-up. This timepoint captures the maximal "dose" of the programme while the support structure is still fresh, and avoids mixing immediate relief with questions of maintenance and decay, which require separate analysis.

**Research question.** In families of people with schizophrenia or related psychosis, what is the effect of family-based interventions (psychoeducation, family support groups, family therapy or other structured relative-focused programmes) plus standard care, compared with standard care alone, on family caregiver burden at the first assessment within one month after the end of the intervention?

**Direction of effect.** All burden scores were oriented so that higher = more burden. A *negative* Mean Difference therefore indicates lower burden in the family-intervention arm and favours the intervention.

## 2. Methods

### 2.1 Eligibility criteria

| Domain | Criterion |
|---|---|
| **Population** | Families, relatives or informal caregivers of people with schizophrenia, schizoaffective disorder or related non-affective psychosis |
| **Intervention** | Family-based intervention delivered to relatives — psychoeducation, family support/peer group, family therapy, or another structured relative-focused programme — in addition to standard care |
| **Comparator** | Standard care / treatment as usual for both patient and family |
| **Outcome** | Family caregiver burden on a validated burden instrument; total burden score preferred; one measure per trial |
| **Timepoint** | First post-intervention assessment occurring within one month of the end of the intervention; later follow-ups (> 1 month) excluded from this analysis |
| **Design** | Randomised or quasi-randomised controlled comparison |
| **Respondent** | Caregivers' own ratings only; where several relatives were assessed, the main/primary caregiver |

### 2.2 Outcome handling and data conventions

For each trial we extracted the mean, standard deviation and number of family caregivers per arm at the eligible timepoint. Pre-specified conventions were:

- **End-of-intervention values preferred.** Where only change-from-baseline was reported, change scores were used for both arms and the substitution noted.
- **Dispersion conversion.** Standard errors or SEMs were converted to standard deviations as SD = SE × √n.
- **Orientation.** Instruments scored so that higher values denote *less* burden (or more wellbeing) were reverse-scored so that, across all trials, higher = more burden.
- **One measure per trial.** Where multiple burden instruments or subscales were reported, the total burden score on the primary instrument was taken, to avoid within-trial dependency.
- **Respondent.** Only caregiver-reported burden was used; patient-reported or clinician-rated proxies were not substituted.

### 2.3 Effect measure and synthesis

The effect measure was specified by the review question as the **Mean Difference (MD)**, and MD pooling was carried out natively on the trials' reported burden-score units (`native_pooling_used = true`; source: explicit request). Study-level standardised effects (Hedges' g, with small-sample correction) were additionally computed to allow cross-instrument comparison of magnitude and to support the small-study-effect diagnostics.

Pooling used a random-effects model with **restricted maximum likelihood (REML)** estimation of the between-study variance τ². Heterogeneity was characterised by Cochran's Q with its p-value, the I² statistic (proportion of total variability attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error) and τ² (absolute between-study variance on the analysis scale). Small-study effects and publication bias were assessed by Egger's regression of effect size on standard error and by Duval & Tweedie trim-and-fill.

### 2.4 Rescaling caveat (pre-specified disclosure)

An instrument score range was available for only **4 of the 6** contributing rows. Because a complete set of ranges was not available, **no 0–100 rescaling was applied**, and the MD was pooled in the papers' **raw reported units**. This is the single most important interpretive constraint on the numerical magnitude reported below and is discussed in §8 and §9.

## 3. Search and Screening

Records were identified from the review's bibliographic corpus of controlled trials of family-focused interventions in schizophrenia and related psychoses, then screened against the eligibility criteria in §2.1. Screening proceeded in the standard PRISMA sequence: de-duplication, title/abstract screening, full-text assessment against the population–intervention–comparator–outcome–timepoint criteria, and finally data extraction with verification that the extracted outcome was (a) caregiver-reported, (b) a validated total burden score, and (c) measured within one month of the end of the intervention.

**PRISMA flow (as realised in this synthesis).**

| Stage | n |
|---|---|
| Records entering data extraction (eligible trial reports) | **7** |
| Trials contributing a usable effect size at the eligible timepoint | **6** |
| Trials excluded at the quantitative stage (insufficient or non-extractable burden data at ≤ 1 month) | **1** |
| Family caregivers in the pooled analysis | **377** |

Secondary and duplicate reports of the same trial were collapsed to a single comparison so that no cohort contributed more than once, and narrative reviews, overviews and non-randomised descriptive reports were removed before extraction. Records reporting family intervention effects only at follow-up points beyond one month, or only on patient-level outcomes, did not contribute to this analysis.

## 5. Effect Sizes

Study-level standardised effects (Hedges' g, negative = less burden in the intervention arm) are shown below. These are reported to display the spread and direction of individual findings on a common metric; the **primary pooled statistic is the Mean Difference** presented in §6.

| ID | Hedges' g | 95% CI | n | Direction | CI excludes 0? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| study_1_R1 | −0.4394 | [−0.904, 0.025] | 73 | Less burden with intervention | No (marginal) |
| study_2_R2 | −0.8565 | [−1.365, −0.348] | 65 | Less burden with intervention | Yes |
| study_3_R3 | +0.1557 | [−0.417, 0.729] | 47 | More burden with intervention | No |
| study_4_R4 | −0.7423 | [−1.327, −0.157] | 48 | Less burden with intervention | Yes |
| study_5_R5 | −1.9788 | [−2.528, −1.430] | 76 | Less burden with intervention | Yes |
| study_6_R6 | −0.6204 | [−1.107, −0.134] | 68 | Less burden with intervention | Yes |

**Observations.**

1. **Consistency of sign.** Five of six comparisons (83%) point towards reduced caregiver burden following family intervention. Only study_3 shows a point estimate in the opposite direction, and it is small (g = +0.156) with a confidence interval spanning zero in both directions — a result statistically compatible with no effect rather than with harm.
2. **Four of six individually significant.** study_2, study_4, study_5 and study_6 have confidence intervals wholly below zero. study_1 is borderline (upper limit +0.025, i.e. just touching the null).
3. **A wide magnitude range.** Excluding study_3, standardised effects span g = −0.44 to g = −1.98 — from a modest to a very large reduction. study_5 is a clear high-magnitude outlier: its effect is more than twice the median of the remaining negative estimates, and its confidence interval does not overlap those of study_1 or study_6. This single trial is the principal driver of both the magnitude of the pooled estimate and the observed heterogeneity.
4. **No size–effect gradient is visually obvious.** The largest trial (study_5, n = 76) reports the largest effect, while the smallest (study_3, n = 47) reports the only null-to-adverse estimate — a pattern that runs *counter* to the classic small-study-effect signature, consistent with the bias diagnostics in §8.

## 6. Meta-Analytic Result

### 6.1 Primary pooled estimate

| Quantity | Value |
|---|---|
| Effect measure | **Mean Difference (MD)** — pooled natively on the trials' raw burden-score units |
| Comparisons pooled (k) | **6** |
| Family caregivers (N) | **377** |
| Model | Random effects, REML |
| **Pooled Mean Difference** | **−4.4809** |
| 95% confidence interval | **[−6.301, −2.660]** |
| p-value | **1.4064 × 10⁻⁶** |
| Favours | **Family-based intervention (psychoeducation, family support group, family therapy or other structured programme for relatives) plus standard care** |

The pooled Mean Difference is **−4.481 burden-scale points (95% CI −6.301 to −2.660; p = 1.41 × 10⁻⁶)**. Because all instruments were oriented so that higher = more burden, this negative value corresponds to **lower caregiver burden in the family-intervention arm**, and the `favors` field confirms that the result favours **family-based intervention plus standard care**.

The confidence interval lies entirely on the beneficial side of the null and is some distance from it: even the most conservative bound compatible with the data (−2.660) still represents a reduction in burden, not an absence of effect. The p-value of approximately 1.4 in a million makes chance an implausible explanation for the overall direction, although — as always — it speaks to the reliability of the *sign*, not to the accuracy of the *magnitude*.

The forest plot is at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/forest_plot.png`.

### 6.2 Effect-measure status (explicit statement)

The effect measure is the **Mean Difference (MD)**, as specified in the review question, and **native MD pooling was used** (`native_pooling_used = true`): the pooled value is a genuine raw-scale result, not a standardised fallback substituted because the native measure could not be confirmed. It must **not** be described as a Hedges' g. The study-level g values in §5 are a secondary, descriptive layer.

One recorded qualification applies to the scale on which that MD sits: an instrument score range was stated for only **4 of the 6** contributing rows, so **no 0–100 rescaling was applied** and the MD is expressed in the papers' heterogeneous raw units. The figure "−4.481 points" is therefore a weighted average across burden questionnaires whose point-scales are not identical, and it should be read as an indicative magnitude on a generic burden metric rather than as a precise number of FBIS, ZBI, BAS or IEQ points. For readers who require a scale-free magnitude, the standardised study-level estimates in §5 (median of the five beneficial estimates ≈ g = −0.74) and the trim-and-fill standardised summary of **g = −0.7463** (§8) both indicate a **moderate-to-large** reduction in caregiver burden by conventional benchmarks.

### 6.3 Cumulative and sensitivity views

The cumulative meta-analysis (`outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/cumulative_plot.png`) shows the running pooled estimate as comparisons accumulate. The direction of effect is established early and never reverses; what changes as studies accrue is the width of the interval and the magnitude of the point estimate, which is pulled further from the null by the inclusion of the high-magnitude comparison (study_5) and pulled back towards it by study_3. Given k = 6, no single comparison can be removed without materially altering the pooled magnitude; the most influential is study_5, whose exclusion would be expected to attenuate the pooled effect substantially and to reduce heterogeneity, while leaving the direction of benefit intact (four of the five remaining comparisons still favour the intervention, three of them individually significantly).

## 7. Heterogeneity

| Statistic | Value | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|
| Cochran's Q | **12.672** (df = 5) | — |
| Q p-value | **0.0267** | Statistically significant heterogeneity |
| **I²** | **55.53%** | Moderate — just over half of total variability is between-study |
| **τ²** | **2.677232** | Between-study variance on the MD analysis scale |
| τ (√τ²) | ≈ 1.636 | Between-study SD ≈ 1.64 burden-scale points |

Heterogeneity is **moderate and statistically significant**. Q = 12.672 on 5 degrees of freedom (p = 0.0267) rejects the hypothesis that all six comparisons estimate a single common effect, and I² = 55.53% indicates that roughly **56% of the observed variability in results reflects real differences between trials** rather than within-trial sampling error. This sits in the conventional "moderate" band and justifies the random-effects specification used throughout; a fixed-effect model would have been inappropriate and would have produced spuriously narrow intervals.

With τ ≈ 1.64 points and a mean effect of −4.48, an approximate 95% **prediction interval** for the effect in a new, exchangeable trial would span roughly −8.3 to −0.7 burden points. Two features of that interval are worth stating plainly: it is wide, and it nonetheless remains on the beneficial side of zero across almost its entire range. In other words, the *amount* of burden relief a future family programme would deliver is genuinely uncertain, but the model does not predict that a typical future trial would show increased burden.

**Likely sources of the heterogeneity.**

- **Instrument heterogeneity.** The trials did not all use the same burden questionnaire, and raw-unit MD pooling without a common scale range mechanically inflates between-study variance: identical clinical effects expressed on differently scaled instruments appear as different MDs. Part of the 55.53% is therefore metric artefact, not clinical disagreement.
- **One high-magnitude trial.** study_5 (g = −1.979) is a clear outlier whose confidence interval does not overlap those of the lower-magnitude comparisons. Visual inspection of the forest plot suggests that much of Q is attributable to the contrast between study_5 and the remainder.
- **Intervention content and intensity.** Brief information-giving, extended skills-based family therapy and peer-led support groups plausibly differ in how much they relieve relatives, as do programmes of a few sessions versus several months.
- **Baseline burden and care context.** Trials conducted where usual care already includes substantial family contact have less headroom for improvement than those where standard care is essentially patient-only, generating genuine effect modification across settings.
- **Outcome construction.** A minority of comparisons may have contributed change-from-baseline rather than end-of-intervention values (a pre-specified fallback), and change and endpoint contrasts do not have identical variance properties.

## 8. Moderators

No formal moderator analysis was pre-specified or conducted as a hypothesis test. With **k = 6**, meta-regression is not defensible: the conventional rule of thumb of roughly ten studies per covariate is violated several times over, and any subgroup split would produce strata of two or three trials whose estimates are dominated by single studies. Formal subgroup contrasts by intervention format (psychoeducation vs family therapy vs support group), delivery modality (single-family vs multi-family/group), programme duration, burden instrument, or caregiver relationship to the patient were therefore not attempted, and no subgroup p-values are reported here because none can be computed credibly.

A descriptive **bubble plot** of effect size against study precision is provided at `outputs/bJG9avlBat4bRDhUYzkgRQ/bubble_chart.png`. It is offered as a visual aid only. The one informal pattern visible in Table §5 — that the largest trial reports the largest effect and the smallest trial the only non-beneficial one — argues against precision-driven inflation of the pooled result, but with six points this is an observation, not a finding.

The implication for practice is that the present evidence supports *that* structured family work reduces caregiver burden at programme end, but is far too thin to say *which kind* of family programme does so best, for *which* relatives, or at *what* dose. Those are questions for a larger evidence base or for individual-participant-data synthesis.

## 9. Publication Bias

| Test | Result |
|---|---|
| Egger's regression intercept | **−0.3567** |
| Egger's p-value | **0.917** |
| Trim-and-fill imputed studies (k₀) | **0** |
| Trim-and-fill adjusted standardised estimate | **g = −0.7463** |
| Overall risk rating | **Low** |

Egger's regression test shows no evidence of funnel plot asymmetry (p = 0.917, far from conventional significance), indicating no detectable relationship between effect size and standard error — i.e. no signal of small-study effects. The intercept itself (−0.3567) is close to zero and its sign is immaterial given the p-value. Consistent with this, the trim-and-fill procedure imputed **zero** missing studies (k₀ = 0), meaning the funnel plot was already symmetric and the pooled estimate requires no adjustment for potentially suppressed results. The trim-and-fill standardised summary of g = −0.7463 is accordingly an *unadjusted* value — nothing was filled in — and is useful chiefly as a scale-free corroboration that the benefit is moderate-to-large.

**Caveat.** These converging results support the "low risk" rating, but they should be read as an **absence of evidence rather than evidence of absence**. Egger's test has limited power when fewer than about ten studies are pooled or when heterogeneity is high — and both conditions apply here (k = 6, I² = 55.53%). Neither method can detect bias that affects all studies uniformly, such as outcome reporting bias within trials (e.g. selective presentation of the most favourable burden subscale) or a literature-wide tendency for family-intervention trials to be designed, analysed and written up by investigators committed to the approach. A symmetric funnel with six points is weak reassurance, not a clean bill of health.

## 10. Discussion

This synthesis of six randomised comparisons and 377 family caregivers found that adding a structured family-based intervention to standard care produced a statistically significant reduction in caregiver burden at the first assessment within one month of programme completion: **pooled Mean Difference −4.481 (95% CI −6.301 to −2.660; p = 1.41 × 10⁻⁶)**, favouring **family-based intervention plus standard care**. The standardised view of the same data places the typical benefit in the moderate-to-large range (median study-level g ≈ −0.74 among the beneficial comparisons; trim-and-fill standardised summary g = −0.7463).

Three features make the *direction* of this result reasonably robust. First, the consistency of sign: five of six comparisons point towards reduced burden, and the sixth is a small null-to-slightly-adverse estimate whose interval straddles zero. Second, four of six comparisons reach individual statistical significance despite samples of 47–76 caregivers — effects large enough to be detected in small trials. Third, there is no detectable funnel asymmetry and nothing for trim-and-fill to impute, and the within-sample pattern (largest trial, largest effect) is the opposite of what small-study bias typically produces.

What is far less secure is the *magnitude*. The pooled MD of −4.48 points is an average across burden instruments whose score ranges were documented for only four of six contributing rows, so no common rescaling could be applied. A four-and-a-half-point reduction means something quite different on a short Zarit short-form than on a multi-domain Family Burden Interview Schedule. Readers should take the number as indicating a non-trivial, clinically appreciable shift in reported burden rather than as a transferable quantity. The standardised layer is the more portable summary, and it too carries an important dependency: much of the pooled magnitude is contributed by a single high-effect trial (study_5, g = −1.979), without which the pooled benefit would be meaningfully smaller though still in the same direction.

The finding is mechanistically plausible and coherent with the wider family-intervention literature, which has long shown reductions in expressed emotion, improved caregiver knowledge and coping, and reduced patient relapse. Several pathways could generate immediate burden relief: information that reframes puzzling or frightening behaviour as illness-related rather than wilful; concrete problem-solving and crisis-planning skills that reduce the sense of helpless vigilance; peer contact that counters isolation and stigma; and, in some programmes, improved patient symptom control that directly lightens supervision demands. The design of the included trials does not permit these mechanisms to be distinguished.

A crucial boundary condition is the **timepoint**. By design, this analysis captures burden at the moment the intervention ends — when support, contact with facilitators and group cohesion are at their peak. Immediate relief does not establish durable relief. Whether the effect persists once the programme stops, attenuates over months, or requires booster contact to maintain is a separate question that this analysis, which explicitly excluded assessments beyond one month, cannot address. Clinicians and commissioners should be careful not to read a strong end-of-programme result as evidence of lasting change.

Finally, the clinical reading. Even on the conservative interpretation — a moderate, possibly heterogeneous, possibly not durable reduction in burden — the practice implication is favourable, because the intervention is low-risk, broadly acceptable, and already recommended on patient-outcome grounds. The present evidence adds that relatives themselves appear to benefit at programme end, which strengthens the case for offering family work as a family-facing service and not solely as a relapse-prevention tool.

## 11. Data Quality & Validation Notes

The following data-handling and validation issues are recorded explicitly, as they bear directly on how the pooled number should be read:

- **Effect-measure/scale conflict (recorded).** The pre-specified measure was the Mean Difference, and native MD pooling was used. However, an instrument score range was stated for only **4 of the 6** contributing rows. Because the ranges were incomplete, **no 0–100 rescaling was applied** and the MD was pooled in the papers' **raw reported units**. The pooled value of −4.481 is therefore a weighted average across non-identical burden metrics, and its numerical magnitude is not directly interpretable as a fixed number of points on any one questionnaire.
- **Seven reports, six pooled comparisons.** Seven trial reports entered extraction but only **six** supplied usable arm-level burden data (mean, SD, n) at a timepoint within one month of the end of the intervention. The seventh is described narratively and contributes no weight to any pooled estimate; no data were imputed to make it poolable.
- **One measure per trial enforced.** Where trials reported more than one burden instrument or multiple subscales, only the total burden score on the primary instrument was extracted, to prevent a single cohort from contributing correlated effect sizes and being double-counted in the weighting.
- **Duplicate and secondary reports collapsed.** Multiple publications of the same trial were reduced to a single comparison; narrative reviews, overviews and non-randomised descriptive reports were removed before extraction so that no cohort entered the analysis twice.
- **Derived dispersion statistics.** Where trials reported standard errors or SEMs rather than standard deviations, SDs were reconstructed as SD = SE × √n. This is an exact algebraic conversion, but it inherits any reporting error or ambiguity in the original SE and in the n to which that SE referred.
- **Change-score substitution.** The protocol permitted use of change-from-baseline values for both arms where endpoint means were unavailable. Any comparison entered on that basis is not strictly on the same footing as an endpoint contrast, since change and endpoint differences have different variance structures.
- **Reverse-scoring applied.** Instruments oriented so that higher scores denote less burden were reverse-scored to align all comparisons on a "higher = more burden" axis. Mis-specification of an instrument's orientation would flip the sign of that comparison's contribution; orientation was checked per row.
- **No single pre-specified group split.** This analysis constitutes one outcome/timepoint group (end-of-intervention caregiver burden ≤ 1 month). No additional outcome/timepoint groups were defined, so no separate per-group pooling was required, and no group was dropped for insufficient data.
- **Bias diagnostics are underpowered.** Egger's test and trim-and-fill were run as specified and both returned reassuring values, but with k = 6 and I² = 55.53% their power is limited and the "low risk" rating should be treated as provisional.

## 12. Limitations

1. **Small number of trials.** With k = 6, the between-study variance τ² is estimated imprecisely, the confidence interval for the pooled effect is correspondingly fragile, and no moderator analysis is possible. All conclusions about magnitude are provisional.
2. **Small trials.** Individual caregiver samples ranged from 47 to 76. Small trials are more susceptible to allocation and attrition artefacts and tend to produce more dispersed and sometimes exaggerated estimates.
3. **Unrescaled raw-unit MD.** As recorded above, the pooled MD mixes burden instruments without a common score range (ranges known for 4/6 rows). This limits both interpretability and comparability, and mechanically inflates heterogeneity.
4. **Dependence on one outlying trial.** study_5 (g = −1.979) exerts substantial leverage on the pooled magnitude and on Q. The pooled estimate should not be regarded as representative of what a typical programme achieves.
5. **Moderate, significant heterogeneity.** I² = 55.53% (Q p = 0.0267) means the trials do not estimate a single common effect; the prediction interval for a new trial is wide (approximately −8.3 to −0.7 burden points).
6. **Immediate timepoint only.** Assessments beyond one month were excluded by design. Nothing in this analysis speaks to durability, and end-of-programme measurement is the point at which non-specific support and attention effects are strongest.
7. **Unblinded, self-reported outcome.** Caregiver burden is rated by relatives who know whether they attended a programme, and who may have formed expectations of benefit or a desire to validate the facilitators' efforts. Performance and response bias cannot be excluded, and no included design could plausibly blind participants.
8. **Heterogeneous "standard care" comparator.** Treatment as usual differs widely across settings; where usual care already includes family contact, the measured contrast is smaller than where it does not. The pooled effect is thus relative to a moving baseline.
9. **Risk-of-bias and GRADE not quantified here.** This report does not present domain-level risk-of-bias judgements or a formal certainty-of-evidence rating. Informally, the combination of unblinded self-reported outcomes, small samples, moderate heterogeneity and imprecision would be expected to place certainty below the highest tier.
10. **No individual-participant data.** Aggregate pooling cannot identify which relatives (for example, co-resident parents versus partners, or those with the highest baseline burden) benefit most.
11. **Residual language and reporting constraints.** Family-intervention trials are conducted in many health systems and languages; any restriction in the underlying corpus, plus outcome reporting bias within trials, could operate uniformly and would be invisible to the funnel-based diagnostics used here.

---

## 13. Conclusion

In families of people with schizophrenia and related psychoses, structured family-based interventions — psychoeducation, family support groups, family therapy and similar relative-focused programmes — added to standard care reduced family caregiver burden at the first assessment within one month of the end of the intervention. Across six randomised comparisons and 377 caregivers, the pooled **Mean Difference was −4.481 (95% CI −6.301 to −2.660; p = 1.41 × 10⁻⁶; REML)**, a result that **favours family-based intervention plus standard care**, with no detectable funnel asymmetry (Egger's p = 0.917) and no studies imputed by trim-and-fill (k₀ = 0).

The direction of benefit is consistent (five of six comparisons) and statistically secure. Its size is not: heterogeneity was moderate and significant (I² = 55.53%; Q = 12.672, p = 0.0267; τ² = 2.677232), the pooled MD was computed on unrescaled raw units from instruments whose score ranges were documented for only four of six rows, and one high-effect trial contributes disproportionate leverage. The scale-free view (standardised effects clustering around g ≈ −0.74) indicates a moderate-to-large benefit, which is the more portable summary.

Practically, these findings strengthen the case for offering family interventions as a service *to relatives* as well as a relapse-prevention measure for patients, since the immediate burden relief appears real and the intervention is low-risk. Research priorities follow directly from the limitations: larger multi-site trials; consistent use and full reporting of a small number of validated burden instruments with their score ranges, enabling genuine raw-scale or properly rescaled pooling; pre-specified assessment beyond one month to establish durability; and head-to-head comparison of programme formats and intensities, ideally with individual-participant data, to identify which relatives benefit from which kind of family work.
