# Structured Family-Based Interventions and Caregiver Burden in Schizophrenia-Spectrum Psychosis: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Family caregivers of people with schizophrenia or related psychosis commonly experience substantial burden. This review evaluated whether structured family-based interventions reduce validated caregiver-burden scores compared with standard care or treatment as usual.

### Methods
Eligible comparisons evaluated psychoeducation, family support groups, family therapy, or another structured programme for relatives against standard care without another active family intervention. The target outcome was the main caregiver’s validated burden score at the first post-intervention assessment occurring no later than one month after programme completion. Higher raw scores indicated greater burden, so a negative raw mean difference favoured intervention. Seven studies were represented in the supplied dataset; five studies, comprising 312 caregivers, contributed to the quantitative synthesis. Because a fixed-effect estimate was not supplied, a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) was used.

### Results
The pooled Mean Difference (MD) was -4.138907067950453 burden-scale points (95% CI -6.422 to -1.856; p = 0.00038099687971218904), favouring structured family-based intervention. Heterogeneity was substantial (Q = 12.672, p = 0.013; I² = 65.16%; τ² = 4.100948). Egger’s intercept was -0.2147 (p = 0.9085), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0), providing no statistical evidence of small-study effects or funnel-plot asymmetry. These assessments had low power because only five studies were pooled.

### Conclusions
Structured family-based interventions were associated with lower caregiver burden immediately after treatment, with an estimated average reduction of 4.138907067950453 raw burden-scale points relative to standard care. Substantial between-study heterogeneity, sparse reporting, unreported risk-of-bias information, and uncertainty about comparability of raw units across burden instruments limit confidence in the magnitude and generalisability of this estimate.

## Introduction

Schizophrenia and related psychotic disorders can impose sustained emotional, practical, social, and financial demands on family caregivers. Structured interventions for relatives—including psychoeducation, support groups, family therapy, and multicomponent programmes—seek to improve knowledge, coping, communication, problem-solving, and access to support. Although these programmes may benefit both patients and families, their direct effect on caregiver burden requires assessment using caregiver-reported, validated burden instruments.

This review addressed the following question: among family caregivers of people with schizophrenia or related psychosis, what is the effect of a structured family-based intervention, compared with standard care or treatment as usual without another active family intervention, on validated caregiver-burden scores at the first assessment after intervention and no later than one month after programme completion?

## Search and Screening

The supplied results did not include database names, search dates, complete search strategies, registry searches, deduplication counts, or title/abstract and full-text exclusion counts. Consequently, a reproducible literature-search narrative and complete PRISMA flow diagram cannot be reconstructed from the available information.

The prespecified eligibility framework was as follows:

- **Population:** family caregivers of people with schizophrenia or related psychosis.
- **Intervention:** a structured programme for relatives, including psychoeducation, family support groups, family therapy, or another structured family-based intervention.
- **Comparator:** standard care or treatment as usual for the patient and family, without another active family intervention.
- **Outcome:** burden measured in caregivers, rather than patients, using a validated burden scale; where several relatives were reported, the main caregiver was preferred.
- **Time point:** the first post-intervention assessment occurring no later than one month after the programme ended; later follow-up assessments were excluded.
- **Effect data:** endpoint means, standard deviations, and caregiver counts were preferred. Change scores were eligible only when reported for both groups and were not to be mixed with endpoint scores. Total burden scores were preferred over subscales.

Higher burden scores were treated as worse. Under the requested raw mean-difference convention, negative values therefore favoured structured family-based intervention. Oppositely oriented scales were to be reversed. Standard errors were to be converted to standard deviations using `SD = SE × √n` after checking table footnotes. For eligible multi-arm studies, relevant intervention groups were to be combined and compared only with the standard-care group.

The pipeline summary contained seven studies. Five yielded effect sizes suitable for quantitative synthesis, while two did not contribute to the pooled analysis. The supplied information does not state why those two studies were not meta-analysed; no exclusion reason should therefore be inferred.

## Effect Sizes

The explicitly requested synthesis statistic was the **Mean Difference (MD)** in burden-scale points, calculated as intervention minus control. Because higher burden scores represent worse outcomes, an MD below zero favours the structured family-based intervention.

The analysis used native raw units and did not rescale burden instruments to a common 0–100 range. Instrument ranges were stated for only four of the five pooled rows. This preserves the requested raw-unit convention but creates an important interpretive concern if different burden scales, versions, or ranges were combined. The available dataset did not identify which results were endpoint scores and which, if any, were change scores. Compliance with the rule against mixing endpoint and change scores therefore could not be independently verified from the supplied aggregate output.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A fixed-effect result was not supplied. The available synthesis used a random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), incorporating both within-study sampling error and estimated between-study variation.

Across five studies and 312 caregivers, the pooled **Mean Difference (MD)** was **-4.138907067950453 burden-scale points** (95% CI **-6.422 to -1.856**; p = **0.00038099687971218904**). As recorded in the supplied analysis, this result favoured **structured family-based intervention for relatives of people with schizophrenia or related psychosis** over standard care or treatment as usual.

Thus, at the first eligible assessment after treatment, structured family-based programmes were associated on average with burden scores approximately 4.14 raw scale points lower than those under standard care. The confidence interval excluded no difference, although its practical meaning depends on the burden instrument and its minimally important difference, neither of which was provided.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was substantial:

- Cochran’s Q = **12.672**
- Q-test p = **0.013**
- I² = **65.16%**
- τ² = **4.100948**

The significant Q test indicates that observed differences among study effects exceeded those expected from sampling error alone. I² suggests that approximately 65.16% of the observed variability was attributable to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error under the model. The τ² estimate represents between-study variance in squared raw burden-scale units; its clinical interpretation is limited where instruments do not share fully comparable units.

Potential sources include differences in family-programme content and intensity, caregiver populations, patient illness stage, cultural or service settings, comparator care, burden measures, and analytic time points. These explanations remain hypotheses because corresponding study-level data were unavailable.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **-0.2147** with p = **0.9085**. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies. The software also reported a trim-and-fill standardized summary estimate of **-0.7242**; this is not directly comparable with the pooled raw-unit MD and does not alter the analysis because no studies were imputed.

These results provide no statistical evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects, and the supplied classification was a **low** risk of publication bias. This conclusion must remain cautious: with only five pooled studies, Egger’s test and trim-and-fill have limited power and may fail to detect genuine selection effects. Absence of detected asymmetry is not proof that publication bias is absent.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This meta-analysis found that structured family-based interventions reduced immediate post-intervention caregiver burden relative to standard care. The pooled MD of -4.138907067950453 burden-scale points was statistically significant, and its 95% CI ranged from -6.422 to -1.856. The result therefore favoured structured family-based intervention according to the prespecified direction of benefit.

The finding is clinically plausible. Psychoeducation may reduce uncertainty and improve illness understanding; support groups may reduce isolation and normalise caregiver experiences; family therapy may improve communication and conflict management; and multicomponent programmes may strengthen coping and service navigation. Nevertheless, the aggregate data cannot establish which components produced benefit.

### Interpretation of magnitude

The pooled estimate is expressed in raw burden-scale points. In principle, an MD retains direct scale interpretation. In practice, the absence of instrument identities, ranges for one of five rows, and minimally important differences prevents determining whether an average 4.138907067950453-point reduction is small, moderate, or large from a caregiver perspective. If materially different instruments were pooled without valid common units, the numerical MD would be less interpretable than a within-instrument synthesis or a prespecified standardized effect.

### Consistency and generalisability

Substantial heterogeneity indicates that the average effect should not be assumed to apply uniformly across all interventions and settings. The pooled benefit may vary according to programme intensity, caregiver needs, health-system resources, cultural context, and baseline burden. Generalisability cannot be assessed adequately without study locations, demographic characteristics, eligibility criteria, and intervention descriptions.

### Certainty of evidence

A formal certainty assessment using GRADE was not supplied and cannot be reconstructed. Confidence is reduced by the small evidence base, substantial heterogeneity, missing risk-of-bias information, incomplete reporting of screening and study characteristics, and uncertainty regarding cross-instrument raw-unit comparability. Conversely, the confidence interval excluded no effect, and statistical analyses did not detect small-study effects, although the latter tests were underpowered.

## Limitations

1. **Incomplete search documentation:** Databases, dates, search strings, grey-literature methods, deduplication, and screening counts were unavailable, precluding verification of search completeness and full PRISMA reporting.
2. **Only five studies were pooled:** The synthesis included 312 caregivers, limiting precision, robustness, and the reliability of publication-bias tests.
3. **Two of seven studies did not contribute:** Reasons were not reported, so the possibility of availability or selective-outcome bias cannot be assessed.
4. **Substantial heterogeneity:** I² was 65.16%, and the source of between-study variability could not be investigated.
5. **Potential scale incompatibility:** Raw MDs were pooled even though instrument ranges were reported for only four of five rows, and no 0–100 rescaling was applied. If instruments differed materially, a shared raw-point interpretation may not hold.
6. **Mismatch between displayed and pooled metrics:** Available row-level summaries were standardized, whereas the pooled result was a native raw MD. The underlying arm-level means and standard deviations were not provided for independent reconstruction.
7. **Unverified outcome timing and score type:** The aggregate output did not report exact assessment dates or identify endpoint versus change-score analyses for each study.
8. **No risk-of-bias assessment:** Randomisation, allocation concealment, missing data, outcome measurement, selective reporting, and other bias domains could not be evaluated.
9. **No clinical-importance threshold:** Minimally important differences for the burden instruments were unavailable.
10. **No moderator information:** Differences by intervention type, duration, setting, caregiver characteristics, or burden instrument could not be evaluated.

## Conclusion

Structured family-based interventions for relatives of people with schizophrenia or related psychosis were associated with lower caregiver burden at the first post-intervention assessment occurring within the prespecified one-month window. The pooled **Mean Difference (MD)** was **-4.138907067950453 burden-scale points** (95% CI **-6.422 to -1.856**; p = **0.00038099687971218904**), favouring structured family-based intervention. However, substantial heterogeneity, incomplete reporting, lack of risk-of-bias information, and uncertainty about the comparability of raw burden-scale units warrant cautious interpretation. Future reviews should report complete search and screening information, preserve instrument-level transparency, assess risk of bias and evidence certainty, and investigate whether intervention characteristics explain variation in effects.
