# Fibrin-Based Versus Active Non-Fibrin Haemostatic Strategies for Perioperative Mortality After Liver Resection: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis Report

## Abstract

**Background:** Fibrin-based haemostatic agents are used to control bleeding from the hepatic transection surface, but their effect on perioperative mortality relative to active non-fibrin agents or standard surgical haemostasis is uncertain.

**Objective:** To estimate the effect of fibrin-based haemostatic agents on all-cause perioperative mortality among adults undergoing liver resection, using a random-effects risk ratio (RR) defined as mortality risk in the fibrin-based arm divided by mortality risk in the eligible non-fibrin comparator arm.

**Data source and eligibility:** The supplied analytical context reported 9 studies but did not provide database searches, screening counts, study citations, arm-level mortality events, randomised denominators, mortality definitions, or risk-of-bias assessments. Eligible comparators were active non-fibrin haemostatic agents or standard surgical haemostatic techniques; comparisons against no haemostatic agent were outside scope.

**Results:** The supplied dataset contained 1 effect-size record (`study_1_R1`; n = 127), reported only as a standardized fallback value of 0.593144 with a 95% confidence interval (CI) of 0.238 to 0.949. This was not a confirmed risk ratio and was unsupported by arm-level perioperative death counts. Consequently, the requested mortality RR could not be calculated, a random-effects meta-analysis could not be performed, and no valid treatment direction could be assigned. The 9 reported studies therefore cannot be treated as 9 mortality-reporting trials.

**Conclusions:** The available results are insufficient to determine whether fibrin-based haemostatic agents increase or decrease all-cause perioperative mortality after liver resection compared with eligible non-fibrin haemostatic strategies. Recovery of trial-level deaths, denominators, follow-up definitions, and treatment assignments is required before a valid mortality meta-analysis can be conducted.

## Effect Sizes

The prespecified effect measure was the **risk ratio (RR)**:

\[
RR = \frac{d_F/n_F}{d_C/n_C},
\]

where \(d_F\) and \(n_F\) are perioperative deaths and participants in the fibrin-based arm, and \(d_C\) and \(n_C\) are the corresponding values in the eligible non-fibrin comparator arm. Under this definition, **RR > 1** indicates more deaths with the fibrin-based agent and favors the non-fibrin comparator; **RR < 1** indicates fewer deaths with the fibrin-based agent and favors the fibrin-based strategy.

The only supplied effect-size record contained a value stored in a standardized-effect field: **0.593144 (95% CI 0.238 to 0.949; n = 127)**. No effect-measure label or native-pooling confirmation was provided, and no arm-level deaths or denominators accompanied the value. It is therefore a **standardized fallback value, not a confirmed native-scale mortality RR**. It must not be re-labelled or interpreted as a risk ratio. Its sign also cannot be used to assign a favored arm because no validated direction field was supplied.

A standardized continuous-outcome effect is not an appropriate substitute for a binary mortality RR. Converting the supplied value to an RR without the underlying event risks, transformation assumptions, and sampling information would introduce unsupported assumptions and potentially reverse the clinical interpretation.

## Discussion

This review was intended to determine whether fibrin-based haemostatic agents alter all-cause perioperative mortality after liver resection compared with active non-fibrin agents or standard surgical haemostatic techniques. The supplied analytical output does not answer that question. The required binary outcome data—deaths and denominators in each eligible arm—were absent, and the sole numerical effect was not confirmed as a native-scale RR.

The distinction is clinically and statistically important. Mortality is a harmful binary outcome, and the requested RR has a direct interpretation relative to the comparator risk. A standardized fallback effect lacks that interpretation and may arise from a continuous endpoint unrelated to perioperative death. Its apparently positive value and CI excluding zero cannot be translated into evidence of benefit or harm for mortality. In addition, without intervention descriptions, it is impossible to ensure that the fibrin-based and non-fibrin arms were assigned correctly.

The absence of a calculable pooled estimate should not be interpreted as evidence that the interventions are equivalent or that neither affects mortality. It instead reflects insufficient and inadequately classified data. Perioperative death after liver resection may be uncommon, so valid synthesis often depends on retaining trials with explicitly reported zero events. Such trials can contribute information about sample size and event absence, but trials that do not mention mortality must be excluded from this outcome rather than entered as zero-event studies.

A valid reanalysis should retrieve each eligible report and extract, for every randomized comparison: the treatment descriptions; deaths in hospital or within the trial's approximately 30–90-day period; arm totals based on randomisation where available; the precise follow-up window; and whether mortality was all-cause. For multi-arm studies, eligible non-fibrin arms should be combined by summing events and participants. Duplicate publications should be linked so that participants are counted once. The resulting study RRs should then be pooled using a prespecified random-effects method, with transparent handling of zero-event arms and studies.

## Limitations

The principal limitation is the absence of extractable mortality data. Without deaths and denominators by arm, the requested effect measure cannot be calculated or checked. Second, the lack of study citations and intervention details precludes independent eligibility verification. Third, incomplete screening information prevents a reproducible PRISMA flow description. Fourth, the analytic population and follow-up window for the single record are unknown. Fifth, risk of bias could not be assessed across the usual domains of randomisation, allocation concealment, deviations from assigned intervention, missing outcome data, outcome measurement, and selective reporting. Finally, heterogeneity, moderators, publication bias, and robustness analyses were all infeasible.

These limitations are not merely imprecision around an otherwise valid pooled estimate; they prevent construction of the prespecified mortality effect altogether. Reporting the standardized fallback as though it were an RR would be misleading.

## Conclusion

The available results do not permit estimation of the effect of fibrin-based haemostatic agents versus active non-fibrin haemostatic agents or standard surgical haemostasis on all-cause perioperative mortality after liver resection. No pooled risk ratio can be reported. The only numerical record—**0.593144 (95% CI 0.238 to 0.949; n = 127)**—is an unconfirmed standardized fallback and is not interpretable as the requested mortality RR. Trial-level perioperative deaths, denominators, intervention assignments, mortality windows, and source citations must be recovered before a valid random-effects meta-analysis can be completed.
