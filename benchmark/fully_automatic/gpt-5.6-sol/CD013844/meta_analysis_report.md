# Indicated Interventions for Suicidal Ideation Among Young People in Education Settings: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis of Immediate Postintervention Effects

## Abstract

**Background:** Young people in education settings who already exhibit suicidal ideation, self-harm, or related suicide-risk symptoms are a priority population for indicated prevention. This review evaluated the effect of indicated interventions on suicidal ideation measured immediately at the end of intervention.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons involved young people aged up to 25 years in education settings with existing suicide-risk symptoms, an indicated intervention, and an eligible control condition. Only immediate postintervention suicidal ideation outcomes were targeted; later follow-up measurements were outside the estimand. The supplied pipeline recorded 16 studies, of which 6 contributed quantitative effect estimates, representing 331 participants. Effects were synthesized using a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Because the original effect measure could not be resolved, analysis used Hedges’ g as a standardized fallback rather than a confirmed native-scale measure.

**Results:** Across 6 effect estimates, the pooled Hedges’ g was −0.5203450456051786 (95% CI [−2.364, 1.323]; p = 0.5800709407889724). According to the prespecified direction field supplied with the analysis, the point estimate favored indicated interventions, but the confidence interval crossed the null and the pooled effect was not statistically significant. Heterogeneity was extreme (Q = 124.413, p = 0.0; I² = 98.15%; τ² = 5.178536). Individual estimates ranged from −4.377831 to 2.997347. Egger’s intercept was −1.8796 (p = 0.6825), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀ = 0), leaving the pooled estimate at −0.5203. The supplied publication-bias classification was low risk, although tests based on only 6 estimates have limited power.

**Conclusions:** The available point estimate was categorized as favoring indicated interventions, but the evidence did not establish an immediate postintervention benefit because the estimate was highly imprecise and between-study heterogeneity was extreme. Unresolved effect-measure provenance, sparse analyzable evidence, and unavailable study-level methodological information substantially limit interpretation.

## Introduction

Suicidal ideation and self-harm among young people are major public-health concerns and important predictors of subsequent suicidal behavior. Education settings can provide opportunities to identify young people with emerging risk and to deliver timely support. Unlike universal interventions delivered to an entire population, indicated interventions target individuals who already show signs or symptoms of suicide risk, such as suicidal ideation or a history of self-harm.

This review addressed the following question: among young people aged up to 25 years in education settings who already show early signs or symptoms of suicide risk, what is the effect of indicated interventions, compared with control conditions, on suicidal ideation measured immediately at the end of intervention?

The target estimand was the between-group effect on suicidal ideation at immediate postintervention only. Outcomes measured at short-term follow-up (1–3 months), medium-term follow-up (4–12 months), or the longest available follow-up were outside the scope of this analysis.

## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) synthesized the 6 available estimates (total n = 331). The pooled Hedges’ g was **−0.5203450456051786**, with a 95% confidence interval of **[−2.364, 1.323]** and **p = 0.5800709407889724**.

The supplied direction field classifies this point estimate as favoring **indicated interventions for young people with existing suicidal ideation, self-harm history, or other early suicide-risk symptoms in education settings**. Nevertheless, the confidence interval crossed zero by a wide margin, and the hypothesis test was not statistically significant. The analysis therefore does not provide conclusive evidence that indicated interventions reduce suicidal ideation more than control conditions immediately after intervention.

The confidence interval includes potentially substantial effects in either direction. Consequently, the result should not be interpreted as evidence of equivalence or absence of an effect. Rather, it indicates substantial uncertainty around the average effect.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was extreme:

- **Cochran’s Q:** 124.413
- **Q-test p value:** 0.0
- **I²:** 98.15%
- **τ²:** 5.178536

An I² of 98.15% indicates that almost all observed dispersion was attributed by the model to between-estimate heterogeneity rather than sampling error. The large τ² further indicates substantial variation on the standardized-effect scale. This heterogeneity is directly visible in the presence of very large estimates in opposing directions, particularly `study_7_R7` (g = −4.377831) and `study_9_R9` (g = 2.997347).

The pooled average therefore provides an incomplete representation of the evidence. Under such heterogeneity, the true effect may differ considerably across populations, settings, interventions, comparators, or measurement approaches. ## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **−1.8796** with **p = 0.6825**. This test did not detect funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. The trim-and-fill procedure estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies and returned an adjusted pooled value of **−0.5203**, unchanged from the rounded unadjusted estimate. The supplied analysis classified publication-bias risk as **low**.

This finding requires caution. With only 6 effect estimates, Egger’s test has limited power, while trim-and-fill can perform poorly under extreme heterogeneity. A nonsignificant Egger test is therefore not proof that publication bias or selective nonreporting is absent. No funnel-plot path was included among the supplied figure outputs.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

The random-effects point estimate was categorized by the supplied direction field as favoring indicated interventions, but it was not statistically significant and was highly imprecise. The 95% confidence interval ranged from −2.364 to 1.323 on the standardized fallback scale. Consequently, the available synthesis does not establish that indicated interventions improve suicidal ideation relative to controls at the immediate postintervention assessment.

More importantly, the 6 study estimates were profoundly inconsistent. Several estimates suggested effects in one numerical direction, one small study produced a very large estimate in the opposite direction, and two estimates were individually compatible with no difference. An average effect in this setting may combine materially different intervention effects and should not be treated as universally applicable.

### Interpretation and applicability

The focus on immediate postintervention assessment provides a clearly bounded estimand and avoids mixing end-of-treatment outcomes with later follow-ups. However, it does not address persistence of any effect. Suicide-risk interventions may produce benefits that emerge or dissipate after treatment, but follow-up outcomes were deliberately excluded from this analysis.

Clinical interpretation is additionally constrained because the original outcome scales and effect-measure provenance were unresolved. Standardization can facilitate synthesis across instruments, but the reported Hedges’ g was a fallback rather than a confirmed native-scale measure. The practical meaning of a standardized difference of this magnitude cannot be translated into points on a particular ideation scale, a response rate, or an absolute risk difference using the supplied data.

The extreme heterogeneity could reflect variation in intervention content and intensity, participant selection, baseline suicidal ideation, educational context, control conditions, outcome instruments, or implementation quality. These explanations remain hypotheses because the necessary study-level characteristics were unavailable.

### Implications for research and practice

The present evidence is insufficient to support a precise estimate of the average immediate effect. Decisions should therefore not rely solely on the pooled result. Future syntheses should verify each outcome instrument and its direction, preserve native effect measures where appropriate, harmonize direction explicitly, and maintain links among contrasts sharing a control group. Primary reports should provide group-specific sample sizes, means and standard deviations or dichotomous event counts, attrition by arm, and clearly timed end-of-intervention outcomes.

Given the extreme inconsistency, additional well-reported trials and adequately powered analyses of prespecified effect modifiers are needed. Safety outcomes, suicide attempts, self-harm, adverse effects, engagement, and longer-term suicidal ideation should be assessed separately rather than inferred from an immediate ideation endpoint.

## Limitations

First, only 6 effect estimates were quantitatively synthesized despite 16 studies being recorded by the pipeline, and the reason for this discrepancy was not supplied. Selective outcome availability cannot therefore be excluded.

Second, the effect measure was not confirmed. The analysis used Hedges’ g as a standardized fallback, limiting confidence in the measurement-scale assumptions and clinical interpretability of the pooled value.

Third, heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 98.15%; τ² = 5.178536), making the pooled mean a potentially poor summary of effects across settings. The small number of estimates prevented credible investigation of sources of heterogeneity.

Fourth, no study-level risk-of-bias information was supplied. Randomization procedures, allocation concealment, missing outcome data, selective reporting, adherence, and outcome-assessment quality could not be evaluated.

Fifth, publication-bias analyses were based on only 6 estimates and therefore had limited sensitivity. The low-risk classification should be regarded as provisional.

Sixth, no information was available to verify whether multiple estimates shared control participants or whether any equal-allocation assumptions were made. If dependence was present but unmodeled, precision may have been overstated.

Finally, this review addressed immediate postintervention suicidal ideation only. It provides no direct evidence about suicide attempts, suicide deaths, self-harm, adverse effects, or durability of intervention effects.

## Conclusion

Across 6 immediate-postintervention estimates involving 331 participants, the REML random-effects pooled Hedges’ g was −0.5203450456051786 (95% CI [−2.364, 1.323]; p = 0.5800709407889724). The supplied direction classification indicated that the point estimate favored indicated interventions, but the result was not statistically significant and heterogeneity was extreme (I² = 98.15%). Thus, the available evidence does not provide a conclusive estimate of benefit at the end of intervention. The result should be interpreted as uncertain rather than as demonstrating either effectiveness or no effect. Better-reported trials, verified outcome metrics, explicit handling of shared controls, and study-level information sufficient to investigate heterogeneity are required.
