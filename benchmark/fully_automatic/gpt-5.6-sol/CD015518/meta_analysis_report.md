# Resistance Training During Anticancer Therapy for Short-Term Cancer-Related Fatigue: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

### Background
Cancer-related fatigue is common during anticancer therapy. Resistance training may reduce fatigue, but its short-term effect relative to no structured training is uncertain.

### Objective
To evaluate the effect of resistance training delivered during anticancer therapy, compared with no exercise training or usual care without a structured training program, on cancer-related fatigue assessed no later than 12 weeks after intervention initiation.

### Methods
Validated fatigue outcomes were eligible when assessed at a post-baseline time point of 12 weeks or less. When multiple eligible assessments existed, the prespecified primary endpoint—or otherwise the assessment closest to 12 weeks—was to be selected. The supplied pipeline processed 19 studies, but only five study-level effect sizes were available for quantitative synthesis. These five effects represented 556 participants. Standardized Mean Differences (SMDs) were pooled using a random-effects model with restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Statistical heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were examined using Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

### Results
The five study-level SMDs ranged from -1.136045 to 1.409833. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was -0.07553857759276121 (95% CI [-0.987, 0.836]; p = 0.8709821471340643), with the supplied analysis metadata designating the result as favoring resistance training. The estimate was nevertheless very close to the null and statistically imprecise. Heterogeneity was considerable (Q = 114.374, reported p = 0.0; I² = 95.96%; τ² = 1.027941). Egger’s intercept was 0.4959 (p = 0.7639), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀ = 0), leaving the pooled estimate at -0.0755. Publication-bias risk was classified as low, although only five effects were available.

### Conclusions
The available quantitative evidence does not demonstrate a statistically significant short-term reduction in cancer-related fatigue from resistance training during anticancer therapy. Extreme between-study heterogeneity and the wide confidence interval make the average effect difficult to interpret and leave open the possibility of clinically important benefit or harm in particular settings. The evidence should be interpreted cautiously because screening details, study characteristics, risk-of-bias assessments, and reasons why only five of 19 processed studies contributed effects were not supplied.

## Introduction

Cancer-related fatigue is a multidimensional symptom that can affect physical functioning, emotional well-being, treatment tolerance, and quality of life. Resistance training is a plausible supportive intervention during anticancer treatment because it may preserve muscular strength and functional capacity. Its effect may nevertheless vary with cancer type, treatment regimen, baseline fatigue, training intensity, adherence, and the fatigue instrument used.

This review addressed the following question: among people with cancer receiving anticancer therapy, what is the effect of resistance training, compared with no training, on cancer-related fatigue measured at 12 weeks or less?

The intervention was resistance training delivered during anticancer therapy. The comparator was no exercise training or usual care without a structured training program. Eligible outcomes were measurements of cancer-related fatigue obtained using a validated self-report, clinician-rated, or performance-related fatigue instrument. Outcomes had to be assessed after baseline and no later than 12 weeks after intervention initiation. If a study reported multiple eligible assessments, the prespecified primary endpoint was to be selected; if none was specified, the assessment closest to 12 weeks was to be used. Outcomes measured only after 12 weeks were outside the scope of this analysis.

Standardization was appropriate for a synthesis potentially combining different validated fatigue measures. The numerical estimate was extremely close to zero, however, and its confidence interval included both appreciable negative and positive values. ## Meta-Analytic Result

A random-effects model estimated with restricted maximum likelihood included five effects and 556 participants. The pooled **Standardized Mean Difference (SMD)** was **-0.07553857759276121**, with a **95% CI of [-0.987, 0.836]** and **p = 0.8709821471340643**. The supplied result designated the effect as favoring resistance training delivered during anticancer therapy.

The pooled estimate was near the null, and the hypothesis test did not reject a null average effect. The confidence interval was very wide: it remained compatible with materially different effects in either direction. Accordingly, the synthesis does not establish that resistance training reduces short-term cancer-related fatigue compared with no structured exercise training or usual care.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was extreme:

- **Cochran’s Q = 114.374**;
- **Q-test p value = 0.0 as reported by the analysis output**;
- **I² = 95.96%**; and
- **τ² = 1.027941**.

An I² of 95.96% indicates that nearly all observed variation in the study-level estimates was attributed to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error under the assumptions of the model. The τ² estimate likewise indicates substantial dispersion on the SMD scale. With only five effects, τ² and any implied prediction interval would be uncertain; no prediction interval was supplied and none was reconstructed.

The high heterogeneity materially limits the clinical interpretability of the pooled mean. It suggests that a single average effect may not adequately summarize the intervention across the included settings. Plausible sources include differences in cancer type and stage, anticancer treatment, baseline fatigue, resistance-training dose and supervision, comparator behavior, adherence, co-interventions, fatigue instruments, endpoint timing, and study quality. None of these explanations could be tested with the available data.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **0.4959** with **p = 0.7639**. This did not provide evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. Trim-and-fill estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies, and the trim-and-fill pooled estimate remained **-0.0755**. The supplied analysis classified publication-bias risk as low.

These findings should be interpreted cautiously. With only five included effects, Egger’s test has low power and may fail to detect genuine asymmetry. Trim-and-fill can also perform poorly in the presence of severe heterogeneity, which was prominent here. The absence of detected asymmetry is therefore not proof that publication bias, selective outcome reporting, or other dissemination biases were absent.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

This random-effects meta-analysis of five effects and 556 participants did not show a statistically significant average effect of resistance training on cancer-related fatigue within 12 weeks of intervention initiation. The pooled SMD was -0.07553857759276121 (95% CI [-0.987, 0.836]; p = 0.8709821471340643). Although the supplied direction metadata classified the estimate as favoring resistance training, its magnitude was close to zero, and the confidence interval was compatible with widely differing effects.

The dominant result was not the near-null mean but the marked inconsistency across studies. Individual SMDs ranged from -1.136045 to 1.409833, while I² reached 95.96% and τ² was 1.027941. Some studies had confidence intervals wholly on opposite sides of the null. This pattern suggests that intervention effects may differ substantially across populations or implementation contexts, or that methodological differences influenced the results.

### Clinical interpretation

On the available evidence, resistance training cannot be said to provide a consistent short-term reduction in cancer-related fatigue during anticancer therapy. At the same time, the wide pooled confidence interval does not exclude clinically important effects in either direction. The result should not be interpreted as demonstrating equivalence or confirming absence of benefit.

Resistance training may have outcomes other than fatigue, such as effects on strength, physical function, or body composition, but these were outside the research question and were not analyzed. Likewise, the supplied data did not permit assessment of adverse events, feasibility, adherence, treatment interruptions, or acceptability. No conclusions about safety can therefore be drawn from this fatigue analysis.

### Implications for research

Future syntheses should report a reproducible search strategy, a complete PRISMA flow diagram, study-level citations, duplicate-review procedures, and explicit reasons why potentially relevant studies do not enter the meta-analysis. Primary studies and reviews should consistently report:

1. the fatigue instrument and its scoring direction;
2. baseline and follow-up means, standard deviations, and sample sizes by randomized group;
3. change scores or appropriately adjusted between-group estimates;
4. the prespecified primary assessment time;
5. intervention frequency, intensity, duration, progression, and supervision;
6. adherence, comparator contamination, and co-interventions;
7. cancer and treatment characteristics; and
8. adverse events and intervention feasibility.

Given the observed heterogeneity, adequately powered analyses should prespecify a small number of clinically credible effect modifiers rather than conduct numerous post hoc subgroup analyses. Harmonization of outcome direction and clear linkage of multiple reports from the same trial are also essential.

## Limitations

This report has several important limitations:

1. **Incomplete search documentation.** Databases, search dates, search strings, registry searches, and supplementary search methods were not supplied, preventing assessment or replication of search coverage.
2. **Incomplete screening accounting.** Although 19 studies were processed, only five effects were pooled. The reasons the other 14 studies did not contribute are unknown, and a complete PRISMA flow diagram could not be reconstructed.
3. **Limited study identification.** Primary-study citations and descriptive characteristics were unavailable, so applicability to specific cancers, treatments, demographic groups, and resistance-training prescriptions cannot be evaluated.
4. **No study-level risk-of-bias assessment.** Randomization, allocation concealment, missing data, outcome measurement, selective reporting, and other bias domains could not be assessed. The certainty of evidence therefore cannot be formally graded from the supplied information.
5. **Few quantitative effects.** Five effects provide limited precision for estimating a random-effects mean and between-study variance and very low power for publication-bias tests.
6. **Extreme heterogeneity.** I² = 95.96% and τ² = 1.027941 make the average effect difficult to generalize. No moderator data were available to explain the inconsistency.
7. **Imprecise pooled estimate.** The 95% CI [-0.987, 0.836] is compatible with materially different effects and does not establish equivalence.
8. **Standardized outcome scale.** SMDs facilitate pooling across instruments but are less directly interpretable clinically than native-scale differences. No minimally important difference or representative scale standard deviation was available.
9. **No safety or adherence synthesis.** The analysis addresses fatigue efficacy only and cannot establish the feasibility or safety of resistance training during anticancer therapy.
10. **Publication-bias assessment constraints.** The nonsignificant Egger result and trim-and-fill estimate of zero missing studies are weak evidence with only five effects and extreme heterogeneity.

## Conclusion

Among people receiving anticancer therapy, the available meta-analysis does not demonstrate a statistically significant effect of resistance training, compared with no structured exercise training or usual care, on cancer-related fatigue assessed within 12 weeks. The pooled Standardized Mean Difference was -0.07553857759276121 (95% CI [-0.987, 0.836]; p = 0.8709821471340643), and the supplied analysis classified the direction as favoring resistance training. However, the estimate was essentially null and highly imprecise.

Extreme heterogeneity (I² = 95.96%; τ² = 1.027941) substantially weakens the interpretability and generalizability of the pooled average. Although formal analyses did not detect publication bias, those analyses were underpowered. More transparently reported and clinically characterized trials—and a fully documented systematic-review process—are needed before firm conclusions can be drawn about which patients, treatment settings, and resistance-training programs may reduce short-term cancer-related fatigue.
