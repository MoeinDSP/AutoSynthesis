# Extracorporeal Shock Wave Lithotripsy Versus Retrograde Intrarenal Surgery for Renal Stones: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-analysis of Treatment Success at Approximately Three Months

## Abstract

**Background:** Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL/SWL) and retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) are commonly used treatments for renal stones. This review evaluated treatment success at approximately three months.

**Methods:** Eligible comparisons involved adults with renal stones randomised to ESWL/SWL or RIRS and reported binary treatment success—stone-free status or freedom from clinically significant residual fragments—preferably at three months and after the full planned treatment course. The supplied pipeline identified 14 studies, of which 10 contributed effect estimates. A random-effects model fitted by restricted maximum likelihood (REML) pooled risk ratios (RRs). An RR greater than 1 was prospectively defined as a higher success rate with ESWL; an RR less than 1 represented a lower success rate with ESWL. Heterogeneity was assessed using Cochran’s Q, I², and τ². Small-study effects were assessed with Egger’s test and trim-and-fill.

**Results:** Ten comparisons, comprising 1,715 participants, were pooled. The pooled Risk Ratio (RR) was 0.7864231483950038 (95% CI 0.707–0.875; p = 9.686304419927763 × 10⁻⁶). The supplied analysis classified favourability as “neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted)”; this classification is reported as provided and was not independently reassigned. Heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 76.72%; τ² = 0.021103; Q = 37.422; reported Q-test p = 0.0). Egger’s intercept was −0.7925 (p = 0.6331), and trim-and-fill imputed no studies (k₀ = 0), indicating no detected funnel-plot asymmetry or missing studies.

**Conclusions:** The meta-analysis produced a statistically significant pooled RR of 0.7864231483950038, but substantial heterogeneity limits the certainty and universality of the pooled estimate. The supplied favourability field classified the result as favouring neither arm. Interpretation is additionally constrained by absent study-level event counts, success definitions, assessment timings, risk-of-bias assessments, and bibliographic details, as well as an internal mismatch between the negative-valued study-level effect-size records and the stated native RR pooling scale.

## Introduction

Renal stones are common and recurrent, and procedural treatment is often required when spontaneous passage is unlikely or when symptoms, obstruction, infection risk, stone burden, or patient preference justify intervention. Extracorporeal shock wave lithotripsy (ESWL/SWL) fragments stones non-invasively using externally generated acoustic waves, whereas retrograde intrarenal surgery (RIRS) uses flexible ureteroscopy and intracorporeal laser lithotripsy. These approaches differ in invasiveness, anaesthetic requirements, likelihood of requiring repeated procedures, and capacity to achieve early stone clearance.

The prespecified review question was: among adults with renal stones, what is the effect of ESWL/SWL, compared with RIRS, on binary treatment success at approximately three months? Treatment success was intended to encompass each trial’s definition of stone-free status or freedom from clinically significant residual fragments, based on the imaging modality and residual-fragment threshold used by that trial. The target analysis used the number randomised as the intention-to-treat denominator and considered success after the full planned treatment course when repeated sessions were permitted.

## Heterogeneity

Between-study heterogeneity was substantial:

- **Cochran’s Q = 37.422**;
- **reported Q-test p = 0.0**;
- **I² = 76.72%**;
- **τ² = 0.021103**.

The Q-test p value was stored as exactly 0.0 by the pipeline; this should be understood as a numerical reporting or rounding result rather than a literal probability of zero. The I² value indicates that a large proportion of the observed dispersion was attributed to between-study heterogeneity rather than sampling error. The τ² estimate quantifies between-study variance on the model’s analysis scale.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an **intercept of −0.7925** with **p = 0.6331**. This was not statistically significant and provided no evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects under the assumptions of the test.

The trim-and-fill procedure estimated **k₀ = 0** missing studies. The pipeline also reported a trim-and-fill value of **−0.5772** under the field name `trim_fill_g`. Because this value is not on a valid RR scale and no imputed studies were added, it is not interpreted as an adjusted clinical effect. The publication-bias risk was classified by the pipeline as **low**.

These findings are reassuring but not definitive. Publication-bias tests have limited power with only 10 studies, can be distorted by substantial heterogeneity, and do not detect all forms of selective reporting. No funnel-plot path was supplied.

## Discussion

### Principal findings

Across 10 comparisons and 1,715 participants, REML random-effects synthesis yielded a pooled RR of 0.7864231483950038 (95% CI 0.707–0.875; p = 9.686304419927763 × 10⁻⁶). The confidence interval excluded the null RR of 1. Nevertheless, heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 76.72%; τ² = 0.021103), indicating important variation among studies.

The supplied favourability metadata classified the pooled result as favouring neither arm. That designation must be distinguished from statistical significance: a confidence interval excluding 1 establishes that the pooled ratio differs from the null under the fitted model, whereas the treatment favoured depends on validated event coding and arm assignment. Because raw event counts are unavailable and the study-level effect entries are inconsistent with an RR scale, the treatment direction cannot be independently audited from the supplied dataset.

### Clinical interpretation

Treatment success is clinically important, but its meaning depends on how it is measured. Computed tomography is generally more sensitive for residual fragments than ultrasound or plain radiography, and thresholds such as completely stone-free versus residual fragments no larger than 4 mm can yield materially different event rates. Likewise, a result after one procedure is not equivalent to success after a planned course permitting repeated ESWL sessions. These factors may help explain the observed heterogeneity, but the absence of trial-level definitions prevents direct assessment.

The analysis intentionally concerns treatment success only. It does not synthesise operating time, hospital stay, complications, re-treatment, pain, stone-size reduction, or quality of life. Consequently, it should not be treated as a complete comparison of the overall benefits and harms of ESWL and RIRS. Shared decision-making may depend on those omitted outcomes as well as stone characteristics, anaesthetic suitability, available expertise, and patient preference.

### Robustness and certainty

The statistical evidence for a non-null pooled ratio is strong, but confidence in its clinical interpretation is reduced by three main issues: substantial heterogeneity; incomplete reporting of trial characteristics and outcome definitions; and inconsistency between the aggregate native-RR specification and the supplied study-level effect fields. No sensitivity analyses—such as leave-one-out analysis, fixed-effect synthesis, exclusion of abstract-only reports, or restriction to low-risk-of-bias studies—were provided.

The absence of detected small-study effects is supportive but cannot compensate for these limitations. A formal certainty-of-evidence assessment using GRADE was not supplied and cannot be completed reliably without risk-of-bias, inconsistency, indirectness, imprecision, and publication-bias judgements at the outcome level.

## Data Quality & Validation Notes

Although no formal `validation_flags` object was supplied, the available data reveal several validation issues that affect interpretation:

1. **Study-count mismatch:** 14 studies were recorded, but only 10 effect sizes were pooled. Reasons for exclusion of the other four studies were not supplied.
2. **Effect-scale inconsistency:** the meta-analysis is explicitly identified as native-scale RR pooling, but study-level values are stored under `hedges_g` and include negative values, which are impossible for RRs. The study-level records therefore could not be validated as RRs.
3. **Direction inconsistency:** the protocol defines success-event RR direction, yet the supplied `favors` field classifies the pooled estimate as favouring neither arm. The report preserves that supplied classification and does not overwrite it.
4. **Trim-and-fill scale inconsistency:** the reported `trim_fill_g = −0.5772` is not a valid RR and was not clinically interpreted.
5. **Missing extraction details:** arm-level events and denominators, exact success definitions, imaging methods, assessment timing, treatment-course rules, and abstract-only status were not available for verification.
6. **Incomplete PRISMA information:** search sources, dates, queries, duplicate removal, screening exclusions, and full-text exclusion reasons were not provided.

These issues should be resolved against the source trial reports and the analysis code before submission for publication or use in clinical guidance.

## Limitations

This review has important limitations:

- The search strategy and screening process were not supplied, so completeness and reproducibility cannot be established.
- Four of 14 recorded studies did not enter the quantitative synthesis, without stated reasons.
- Trial citations and study characteristics were unavailable.
- The exact definitions and timing of treatment success could not be recorded, despite being central to the research question.
- Arm-level randomised denominators and success counts were absent, preventing verification of intention-to-treat calculations, percentage-to-count conversions, failure-to-success conversions, and arm assignment.
- Study-level effect entries could not be reconciled with the stated RR scale.
- Substantial heterogeneity reduces the generalisability of the pooled average.
- No risk-of-bias assessment, sensitivity analysis, influence analysis, prediction interval, or GRADE assessment was provided.
- Egger’s test had limited power with k = 10 and may be unreliable in the presence of heterogeneity.
- No moderator results were available, despite a generated bubble plot.
- The analysis addresses treatment success only and does not provide a benefit–harm comparison.

## Conclusion

In 10 comparisons involving 1,715 participants, random-effects meta-analysis produced a **pooled Risk Ratio of 0.7864231483950038** (95% CI 0.707–0.875; p = 9.686304419927763 × 10⁻⁶) for approximately three-month treatment success. The supplied analysis classified the result as favouring **neither arm**. Between-study heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 76.72%; τ² = 0.021103; Q = 37.422; reported p = 0.0), while Egger’s test and trim-and-fill did not detect publication bias (Egger p = 0.6331; k₀ = 0).

The pooled result is statistically non-null but should not be used as a definitive directional comparison until event coding, treatment-arm assignment, study-level RRs, success definitions, and follow-up timings have been verified. Access to the source reports and arm-level binary data is necessary for a fully auditable, PRISMA-compliant synthesis and a reliable judgement about which treatment improves success.
