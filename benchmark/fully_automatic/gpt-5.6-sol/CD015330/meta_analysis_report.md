# Short-Term Effects of Combined Dietary and Physical Activity Programmes on BMI in Adolescents: A Systematic Review and Random-Effects Meta-Analysis

## Abstract

## Abstract

**Background:** Combined dietary and physical activity programmes are widely used to address excess weight during adolescence, but their short-term effect on body mass index (BMI) requires evaluation on the native kg/m² scale.

**Objective:** To estimate, among adolescents aged approximately 12–18 years, the effect of combined dietary and physical activity interventions delivered through school-, community-, or family-based programmes, compared with no intervention, usual curriculum, or usual care, on BMI at the first post-intervention assessment occurring no more than 12 months after intervention completion.

**Methods:** The supplied evidence set contained 17 studies and eight extracted effect-size records. The prespecified outcome was BMI in kg/m², expressed as an intervention-minus-control mean difference (MD), for which values below 0 kg/m² favor the intervention. Four records lacked the raw inputs required for native-scale MD pooling and were excluded from that analysis. The remaining four studies (total N = 2,123) were synthesized using a restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) random-effects model.

**Results:** The pooled mean difference was −0.6331046204574716 kg/m² (95% CI −2.350 to 1.084; p = 0.46986876070835815). As designated in the supplied analysis, the point estimate favored the combined dietary and physical activity intervention, but the confidence interval included no effect and effects favoring either group. Heterogeneity was considerable (Q = 49.767, reported p = 0.0; I² = 98.64%; τ² = 2.968545). Egger’s intercept was −1.1591 (p = 0.5519), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing studies (k₀ = 0). These diagnostics were classified as indicating low publication-bias risk, although their reliability is limited by the small number of pooled studies.

**Conclusions:** The available native-scale evidence does not establish a statistically significant short-term reduction in adolescent BMI from combined dietary and physical activity programmes. The point estimate favored intervention, but extreme between-study heterogeneity and the availability of poolable raw BMI data for only four studies make the magnitude and generalizability of the result highly uncertain.

## Search/Screening

### Eligibility framework

The review question was defined using the following criteria:

- **Population:** adolescents aged approximately 12–18 years.
- **Intervention:** a school-, community-, or family-based programme containing both dietary and physical activity components.
- **Comparator:** a concurrent no-intervention, usual-curriculum, or usual-care condition.
- **Outcome:** BMI measured in kg/m².
- **Timing:** the first assessment after the intervention ended, provided that it occurred no more than 12 months after intervention completion.
- **Effect orientation:** intervention minus control; therefore, an MD below 0 kg/m² favors intervention and an MD above 0 kg/m² favors control.

BMI z-scores, BMI percentiles, waist circumference, body fat, dietary intake, physical activity, and other outcomes were not eligible for combination with BMI in kg/m². Directly reported adjusted or unadjusted between-group differences were preferred; otherwise, differences could be derived from follow-up means or, if necessary, changes from baseline. The supplied data did not provide enough information to determine which extraction route was used in each study.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was extreme:

- **Cochran’s Q = 49.767**
- **Reported Q-test p = 0.0**
- **I² = 98.64%**
- **τ² = 2.968545**

An I² of 98.64% indicates that nearly all observed variation in study estimates, under the conventional interpretation of I², was attributable to between-study inconsistency rather than sampling error. The estimated between-study variance was also large relative to the magnitude of the pooled MD. Accordingly, the average effect should not be interpreted as describing a single common programme effect.

Potential explanations could include differences in programme setting, intervention intensity and duration, age distribution, baseline BMI, follow-up timing, implementation fidelity, and study design. However, these are hypotheses only: the relevant study-level variables were not provided, so no specific source of heterogeneity could be tested.

## Publication Bias

The supplied publication-bias diagnostics were:

- **Egger’s intercept = −1.1591**
- **Egger’s-test p = 0.5519**
- **Trim-and-fill estimated missing studies, k₀ = 0**
- **Trim-and-fill estimate = −0.4751** (reported in the supplied output as `trim_fill_g`)

Egger’s test was not statistically significant, and trim-and-fill did not impute any missing studies. These results provide no detected evidence of funnel-plot asymmetry or small-study effects. However, absence of evidence is not evidence of absence: with a very small meta-analysis, Egger’s test has low power, funnel-plot patterns are difficult to interpret, and trim-and-fill can be unreliable—especially when heterogeneity is extreme.

The trim-and-fill value was supplied under a standardized-effect field rather than explicitly as a BMI MD; it should therefore not be interpreted as a kg/m² estimate or substituted for the primary native-scale result. The analysis set used for the publication-bias calculations was not explicitly identified in the supplied context, which further limits interpretation.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis addressed a narrowly defined clinical and public-health question: whether programmes that jointly target diet and physical activity produce a short-term reduction in BMI among adolescents. The native-scale synthesis produced an average intervention-minus-control difference of −0.6331046204574716 kg/m². The supplied direction designation indicates that this point estimate favors the combined intervention. Nonetheless, the 95% CI of −2.350 to 1.084 kg/m² included no difference and clinically divergent possibilities, and the p value was 0.46986876070835815.

The dominant feature of the evidence was inconsistency. An I² of 98.64% and τ² of 2.968545 imply that programme effects differed substantially across studies. Under these circumstances, the pooled MD is best regarded as an imprecise average across heterogeneous interventions and populations rather than a universally expected treatment effect. Any practical interpretation should prioritize the range and inconsistency of effects rather than the direction of the pooled point estimate alone.

The analysis also illustrates why outcome-scale discipline matters. Eight standardized estimates were available in the extraction output, but only four studies had the information required for native BMI pooling. Combining BMI z-scores, percentiles, anthropometric measures, or unconfirmed standardized effects with BMI in kg/m² would have violated the prespecified estimand and obscured clinical interpretation. Restricting the primary analysis to confirmed MDs preserved the requested unit but reduced the evidence base.

The findings do not demonstrate that combined programmes are ineffective. Rather, the available data fail to establish a statistically precise average short-term BMI benefit. Combined interventions may affect behaviors, fitness, body composition, or longer-term weight trajectories without producing an immediate detectable BMI change, but those outcomes fall outside this review question and cannot be inferred from the present analysis.

- Four records—`study_3`, `study_5`, `study_7`, and `study_8`—were excluded from native MD pooling because the raw inputs needed to confirm or calculate BMI mean differences were absent.
- The eight record-level values were supplied in a field labelled `hedges_g`; they must not all be reinterpreted as BMI differences in kg/m².

- Reasons that the other nine identified studies lacked effect-size records were not reported.
- The analysis did not provide enough information to verify the first eligible post-intervention time point, the derivation method for each MD, handling of multi-arm trials, cluster-adjustment methods, or avoidance of multiple reports from the same trial.
- The publication-bias analysis set was not explicitly identified, and its trim-and-fill estimate was labelled on a standardized scale.

## Limitations

First, only four studies contributed to the requested native-scale analysis. This small evidence base yields imprecise estimates, weak publication-bias diagnostics, and insufficient power for credible moderator analyses.

Second, heterogeneity was extreme. The absence of study-level descriptors prevented investigation of whether variability arose from programme setting, intervention content or intensity, participant characteristics, study design, follow-up timing, or implementation fidelity.

Third, reporting was incomplete. Bibliographic details, search methods, screening decisions, reasons for exclusion, study characteristics, raw outcome data, and risk-of-bias assessments were not supplied. Full PRISMA flow accounting and certainty-of-evidence grading could therefore not be completed.

Fourth, four otherwise extracted records could not be included in native MD pooling because their raw inputs were unavailable. Excluding these studies may reduce precision and could introduce availability bias if studies reporting native BMI data differ systematically from those reporting only standardized or alternative outcomes.

Fifth, the confidence interval around the pooled MD was wide and crossed the null. The data remain compatible with both intervention benefit and an effect favoring control.

Finally, no prediction interval was supplied. Given I² = 98.64%, a prediction interval would be particularly informative for describing the range of effects expected in a new setting, but it cannot be calculated reliably from the summarized information alone without the study-level native MD variances.

## Conclusion

Across four studies involving 2,123 adolescents, combined dietary and physical activity programmes produced a pooled short-term **Mean Difference (MD)** in BMI of **−0.6331046204574716 kg/m²** compared with concurrent control conditions (**95% CI −2.350 to 1.084; p = 0.46986876070835815**). The point estimate favored the combined intervention according to the supplied direction classification, but the result was not statistically significant and heterogeneity was extreme (**I² = 98.64%; τ² = 2.968545**).

The current evidence therefore does not establish a reliable average short-term BMI reduction. Better reported trials and syntheses are needed, including native-scale BMI results, clearly defined post-intervention time points, appropriate handling of clustered and multi-arm designs, detailed intervention descriptors, and sufficiently complete data to investigate sources of heterogeneity.
