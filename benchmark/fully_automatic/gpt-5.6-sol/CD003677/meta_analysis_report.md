# Blood Transfusion After Laparoscopic Versus Vaginal Hysterectomy for Benign Gynaecological Disease: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis

## Abstract

### Background
For women undergoing hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease, the need for blood transfusion is an important peri-operative safety outcome. This review compared laparoscopic hysterectomy—including laparoscopically assisted vaginal hysterectomy (LAVH), total laparoscopic hysterectomy (TLH), and laparoscopic hysterectomy of unspecified type—with vaginal hysterectomy.

### Methods
The eligible outcome was the number of women receiving any intra-operative or post-operative blood transfusion during the hospital stay or through the reported end of follow-up. The intended analysis used intention-to-treat denominators and an odds ratio (OR), with OR >1 indicating more transfusions with laparoscopic hysterectomy. The supplied pipeline identified 10 studies, of which five comparisons (805 participants) contributed to a random-effects meta-analysis using restricted maximum likelihood (REML). Search sources, dates, full study citations, arm-level event counts, and risk-of-bias assessments were not supplied.

### Results
The pooled Odds Ratio (OR) was 1.7165266704986981 (95% CI 0.734–4.017; p = 0.21294119884978777; k = 5; n = 805). The confidence interval included the null value, and the supplied favourability classification was “neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).” Heterogeneity was not detected (Q = 0.794, df = 4, p = 0.9392; I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0). Egger’s intercept was 0.1554 (p = 0.8248), and trim-and-fill estimated no missing comparisons (k₀ = 0), although publication-bias tests have very low power with only five comparisons.

### Conclusions
The available quantitative synthesis did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in transfusion between laparoscopic and vaginal hysterectomy. Although the point estimate was above 1 under the prespecified event coding, the estimate was imprecise and compatible with effects in either direction. Interpretation is limited by missing arm-level event data, incomplete reporting of the screening process and study characteristics, the inability to verify intention-to-treat denominators or visibly identify double-zero trials, and the small number of pooled comparisons.

## Search and Screening

The review question was structured around women undergoing hysterectomy for benign gynaecological disease; laparoscopic hysterectomy (LAVH, TLH, or an unspecified laparoscopic approach) as the intervention; vaginal hysterectomy as the comparator; and receipt of any blood transfusion as the binary outcome.

Eligible outcome data were defined as the number of women receiving at least one blood transfusion intra-operatively, post-operatively during the hospital admission, or through the reported end of follow-up. Blood-loss volume, postoperative haemoglobin change, and haematoma were not acceptable substitutes. The intended denominator was the number randomised to each group, consistent with an intention-to-treat analysis. Procedure descriptions, rather than table-column position, were to determine intervention and control assignment. For eligible multi-arm studies containing more than one laparoscopic approach and a shared vaginal-hysterectomy arm, each laparoscopic approach was to be analysed separately after dividing the shared vaginal arm equally across comparisons; abdominal and robotic arms were to be excluded.

The supplied pipeline summary contained 10 studies but only five quantitative effect sizes. No database names, search strings, search dates, language restrictions, registry searches, deduplication counts, title/abstract screening counts, full-text exclusion reasons, or PRISMA flow diagram were provided. Consequently, the reproducibility and completeness of the literature search cannot be assessed, and a numerical PRISMA flow cannot be reconstructed without inventing information.

Five labelled comparisons—`study_1_R1`, `study_2_R2`, `study_3_R3`, `study_4_R4`, and `study_6_R6`—contributed to the meta-analysis, representing 805 participants in total. Their reported sample sizes were 504, 84, 45, 72, and 100, respectively. The pipeline nevertheless listed 10 studies overall. Arm-specific transfusion events and intention-to-treat totals were also unavailable. ## Meta-Analytic Result

Using a random-effects model estimated by restricted maximum likelihood (REML), the pooled **Odds Ratio (OR)** was **1.7165266704986981** (95% CI **0.734–4.017**; p = **0.21294119884978777**), based on five comparisons and 805 participants.

The confidence interval crossed the null value of OR = 1, and the result was not statistically significant at the conventional two-sided 0.05 level. The supplied favourability field classified the result as **“neither (essentially a tie, or a ratio measure where favourability depends on which event was counted).”** Under the prespecified event coding, the point estimate lies above 1, but the uncertainty interval remains compatible with both fewer and more transfusions with laparoscopic hysterectomy relative to vaginal hysterectomy. Thus, the analysis does not establish a difference between procedures.

## Heterogeneity

Statistical heterogeneity was not detected:

- Cochran’s Q = **0.794** with 4 degrees of freedom;
- Q-test p = **0.9392**;
- I² = **0.0%**; and
- τ² = **0.0**.

These statistics indicate that the observed dispersion was no greater than expected from within-comparison sampling error. However, with only five comparisons—and apparently sparse binary events—the heterogeneity statistics have limited power to detect genuine between-study variation. An I² of 0% should therefore not be interpreted as proof that the studies were clinically or methodologically identical.

In particular, it was not possible to assess whether effects differed by laparoscopic approach (LAVH versus TLH versus unspecified laparoscopic hysterectomy), transfusion-assessment window, transfusion protocol, study size, operative complexity, or risk of bias.

## Publication Bias

Egger’s regression produced an intercept of **0.1554** with p = **0.8248**. The trim-and-fill procedure estimated **k₀ = 0** missing comparisons. The supplied pipeline classified publication-bias risk as **low**. An additional trim-and-fill output of **0.2699** was provided, but its effect-measure label was not specified; it is therefore not interpreted as an adjusted OR.

These diagnostics did not identify funnel-plot asymmetry or imputed missing studies. Nevertheless, five comparisons are far below the number generally considered adequate for reliable asymmetry testing. Egger’s test has low power in such a small meta-analysis, while trim-and-fill can perform poorly with sparse binary outcomes and cannot rule out selective publication or selective outcome reporting. The findings should consequently be described as an absence of detected asymmetry, not affirmative evidence that reporting bias is absent.

## Discussion

This meta-analysis addressed a clinically specific safety outcome: whether women undergoing laparoscopic rather than vaginal hysterectomy for benign disease were more likely to receive any blood transfusion. Across five comparisons and 805 participants, the pooled OR was 1.7165266704986981, but its 95% CI was wide (0.734–4.017) and crossed 1. The evidence therefore did not demonstrate a statistically significant difference in transfusion frequency. The supplied favourability classification was neither intervention.

The lack of detected heterogeneity supports the statistical coherence of the pooled analysis, but it does not resolve uncertainty about clinical comparability. LAVH and TLH may differ in operative technique, case selection, vascular control, uterine size, and conversion patterns. Likewise, transfusion practice may depend on local thresholds, baseline anaemia, symptoms, peri-operative haemoglobin testing, and institutional protocols. None of these characteristics was available for investigation.

The width of the confidence interval is clinically important. The estimate remains compatible with a potentially meaningful reduction as well as a substantial increase in transfusion odds. Non-significance should therefore not be equated with equivalence or non-inferiority. Sparse transfusion events could also make conventional odds-ratio estimation sensitive to continuity corrections and zero-cell handling, but the supplied output did not document those details.

The publication-bias diagnostics were reassuring only in a limited sense: no asymmetry or missing comparisons were detected. Given k = 5, these analyses cannot materially exclude small-study effects or selective reporting.

## Data Quality and Validation Notes

Several data-quality issues constrain validation of the synthesis:

1. The pipeline reported 10 studies but only five pooled comparisons; no disposition was supplied for the remaining five studies.
2. Arm-level transfusion events and randomised denominators were absent, preventing independent verification of the odds ratios and intention-to-treat analysis.
3. Double-zero trials could not be identified or visibly retained from the supplied aggregate results.
4. Multi-arm allocation and splitting of any shared vaginal-hysterectomy group could not be checked.
5. Comparison-level auxiliary values were not supplied with a confirmed effect-measure label and cannot be interpreted as ORs; only the meta-analysis itself was confirmed as native OR pooling.
6. Search, screening, study-characteristic, and risk-of-bias data were not provided, precluding a complete PRISMA flow and formal certainty assessment.
7. No information was available to verify whether multiple reports from the same trial had been linked to avoid double-counting.

## Limitations

The most important limitation is incomplete reporting of the evidence base. Without bibliographic citations, detailed study characteristics, event counts, and denominators, the analysis cannot be independently reproduced or audited. The intended outcome definition is precise, but it cannot be confirmed that every included comparison measured receipt of any transfusion over an eligible period rather than a surrogate such as blood loss or haemoglobin change.

Second, only five comparisons contributed to the pooled estimate. This limits precision, weakens heterogeneity and publication-bias diagnostics, and prevents credible moderator analysis. Third, the absence of risk-of-bias assessments means that potential problems in randomisation, allocation concealment, missing outcome data, outcome ascertainment, and selective reporting cannot be incorporated into interpretation. Fourth, the methods used for zero cells, including any continuity correction, were not reported. Finally, no GRADE assessment was supplied, so the certainty of evidence cannot be formally rated from the available information.

## Conclusion

In five comparisons involving 805 participants, laparoscopic hysterectomy versus vaginal hysterectomy yielded a pooled Odds Ratio (OR) for receipt of any blood transfusion of **1.7165266704986981** (95% CI **0.734–4.017**; p = **0.21294119884978777**). The supplied favourability classification was neither intervention. Statistical heterogeneity was not detected (I² = 0.0%; τ² = 0.0), but the estimate was imprecise and does not establish equivalence.

The current evidence should be interpreted cautiously because the arm-level data, status of five unpooled studies, study characteristics, risk-of-bias assessments, and search and screening details were unavailable. A definitive update should report exact transfusion events and intention-to-treat denominators, retain double-zero trials visibly, document multi-arm splitting, distinguish LAVH from TLH where possible, and provide complete PRISMA and risk-of-bias documentation.
