# Breastfeeding or Breast Milk for Procedural Crying in Neonates: A Systematic Review and Quantitative Synthesis

## Abstract

## Abstract

**Background:** Direct breastfeeding or breast milk may reduce crying during painful procedures in neonates. This report evaluates total crying duration during and immediately after such procedures relative to routine care without an analgesic or comfort measure.

**Methods:** The supplied evidence set identified 7 studies, but contained only 1 analyzable effect size. The eligible outcome was crying duration during and immediately after the procedure. The supplied effect was Hedges' *g*, based on 90 neonates. Because only one effect estimate was available, no pooled meta-analysis, heterogeneity analysis, moderator analysis, or meaningful publication-bias assessment could be performed. Search dates, databases, screening counts, trial identifiers, arm-level sample sizes, means, standard deviations, and risk-of-bias judgments were not supplied.

**Results:** The single supplied estimate was Hedges' *g* = -0.506264 (95% CI -0.926 to -0.086; total *n* = 90) under the sign convention used in the supplied results. This negative sign is consistent with a treatment-minus-control convention for an outcome on which less crying is preferable. Under the review's prespecified standardized orientation, in which positive values favor breastfeeding, the equivalent reoriented estimate is Hedges' *g* = 0.506264 (95% CI 0.086 to 0.926). Thus, the only analyzable estimate favored breastfeeding/breast milk and its confidence interval excluded the null. A raw mean difference in seconds could not be calculated from the supplied information.

**Conclusions:** The available quantitative result suggests that breastfeeding or breast milk may reduce procedural crying relative to no intervention, but confidence in the overall evidence is limited because only 1 of 7 studies contributed an analyzable effect and essential arm-level and methodological information was unavailable. This is a single-study quantitative synthesis rather than a pooled meta-analysis.

# Introduction

Painful procedures such as heel lance, venipuncture, and intramuscular or intradermal injection are common during neonatal care. Crying duration is a clinically interpretable behavioral response to procedural pain and distress. Direct breastfeeding may provide multisensory comfort through sucking, skin contact, maternal odor, and maternal interaction, while administered breast milk may provide familiar taste and nutritive components. Establishing whether either approach reduces crying relative to routine care without another analgesic or comfort measure is relevant to low-risk neonatal pain management.

The review question was: among term or preterm neonates up to 28 days of age undergoing a painful procedure, what is the effect of direct breastfeeding or breast milk administered during the procedure, compared with no intervention or routine care without an analgesic or comfort measure, on total crying duration during and immediately after the procedure?

The intended raw-scale effect was the mean difference (MD) in seconds, calculated as breastfeeding minus control; therefore, MD < 0 seconds would favor breastfeeding. For standardized effects, the prespecified orientation was that positive Hedges' *g* values indicate shorter crying and favor breastfeeding.

# Search and Screening

## Eligibility criteria

The prespecified eligibility framework was:

- **Population:** Term or preterm neonates up to 28 days of age.
- **Intervention:** Direct breastfeeding or breast milk administered during the painful procedure.
- **Comparator:** No intervention or routine care without an analgesic or comfort measure.
- **Procedures:** Painful procedures such as heel lance, venipuncture, or intramuscular/intradermal injection.
- **Outcome:** Total crying duration during and immediately after the procedure, including crying time or duration of first cry as defined by the study. Total crying time was preferred when multiple crying measures were available. Pain scores, percentage of time crying, physiological outcomes, and other non-duration outcomes were outside the target outcome.
- **Required quantitative data:** Ideally, the mean, standard deviation (SD), and number of neonates in each eligible arm. Minutes were to be converted to seconds; standard errors were to be converted using SD = SE × √*n*; and medians with ranges or interquartile ranges were to be transformed only using a recognized method with explicit documentation.

# Effect Sizes

## Available standardized effect

The supplied result for `study_1_R1` was:

- Hedges' *g* = **-0.506264**
- 95% CI **-0.926 to -0.086**
- Total *n* = **90**

The supplied sign appears to follow a conventional treatment-minus-control standardization for crying duration, under which a negative value denotes less crying in the breastfeeding/breast-milk group. This direction favors breastfeeding because a shorter crying duration is preferable.

However, the review question prespecified the opposite standardized orientation: positive Hedges' *g* should represent shorter crying and favor breastfeeding. Multiplication of the estimate and both confidence limits by -1 gives the equivalent prespecified orientation:

- Reoriented Hedges' *g* = **0.506264**
- 95% CI **0.086 to 0.926**

This reorientation changes only the sign and ordering of the confidence limits; it does not change the magnitude, precision, or statistical evidence.

# Discussion

## Principal findings

The only analyzable study-level estimate favored direct breastfeeding or breast milk over routine care for reducing crying during and immediately after a painful neonatal procedure. After alignment with the prespecified favorable-positive direction, the effect was Hedges' *g* = **0.506264** (95% CI **0.086 to 0.926**; *n* = **90**). The interval excluded zero and ranged from a small favorable standardized effect to a large favorable standardized effect.

The result cannot be translated into an absolute reduction in crying time because arm-level means and SDs were unavailable. This distinction is clinically important: a standardized mean difference expresses separation relative to within-study variability rather than the number of seconds by which crying was shortened.

## Interpretation

The point estimate is compatible with a moderate reduction in crying under common heuristic interpretations of standardized effects. Such thresholds should be applied cautiously because clinical importance depends on the underlying variability, procedural context, outcome definition, and baseline crying duration. Without the source SD or raw means, the clinical magnitude remains uncertain.

The confidence interval excludes no effect, but it describes uncertainty for one study rather than uncertainty around an average effect across a body of evidence. The finding therefore supports possible efficacy but does not establish consistency across procedures, neonatal populations, or methods of breast-milk delivery.

## Applicability

Applicability cannot be judged reliably because no details were supplied about term/preterm status, neonatal age, procedure type, care setting, direct feeding versus administered milk, or intervention timing. The estimate should not automatically be generalized to all neonates or all painful procedures.

## Certainty of evidence

A formal GRADE assessment was not possible because risk of bias, inconsistency across studies, directness, precision on the clinically interpretable scale, and publication bias could not be evaluated fully. The absence of six study-level effects from the quantitative dataset and the lack of raw outcome data materially limit confidence in the evidence base.

# Data Quality and Validation Notes

No explicit `validation_flags` were included in the supplied context. Nevertheless, the following data limitations are directly evident and should be retained in any interpretation:

- The pipeline identified **7 studies** but supplied only **1 effect size**.
- The reasons that 6 studies did not contribute to the quantitative analysis were not provided.
- The supplied effect used a negative sign for the favorable result, whereas the review prespecified a positive favorable orientation for standardized effects. The report therefore presents both the supplied estimate and its algebraically reoriented equivalent.
- Arm-level sample sizes, means, and SDs were missing, preventing verification and calculation of the requested MD in seconds.
- The source outcome definition, original unit, conversion history, and any imputation method were not documented.
- No information was available to determine whether multiple reports or overlapping samples were present.

# Limitations

This report has several substantial limitations:

1. **No pooled synthesis:** Only one effect size was available, so the report cannot estimate an average intervention effect.
2. **Incomplete quantitative reporting:** Raw means, SDs, and arm-level sample sizes were absent, preventing calculation of the prespecified MD in seconds.
3. **Unexplained evidence attrition:** Six of seven studies did not contribute an effect size, and the reasons were unavailable.
4. **No study descriptors:** Population, procedure, intervention, comparator, and outcome-measure details were not supplied.
5. **No risk-of-bias information:** Internal validity could not be appraised.
6. **No heterogeneity assessment:** Statistical and clinical consistency could not be evaluated.
7. **No publication-bias assessment:** With one estimate, standard methods for detecting small-study effects were inapplicable.
8. **No reproducible search record:** Search sources, dates, strategies, and screening decisions were unavailable, preventing full verification of PRISMA compliance.
9. **Direction convention required reconciliation:** The supplied standardized effect direction differed from the review's prespecified positive-favors-treatment convention; although algebraic reorientation is straightforward, the discrepancy highlights incomplete effect-size metadata.

# Conclusion

The single available quantitative estimate suggests that direct breastfeeding or breast milk administered during a painful neonatal procedure reduces crying relative to routine care without an analgesic or comfort measure. In the prespecified favorable-positive orientation, Hedges' *g* was **0.506264** (95% CI **0.086 to 0.926**; *n* = **90**). However, this is not a pooled result, and a raw MD in seconds could not be calculated.

The evidence supplied is insufficient for a definitive meta-analytic conclusion. A complete analysis requires the source-study identifiers, arm-level means and SDs, arm-level sample sizes, crying-duration units and definitions, procedure details, intervention modality, risk-of-bias assessments, and reasons that the remaining studies did not contribute data.
